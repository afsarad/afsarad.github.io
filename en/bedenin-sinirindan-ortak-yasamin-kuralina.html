From the Boundary of the Body to the Rules of Shared Life

Disgust, reciprocal benefit, social norms and the human effort to sustain shared life

HomeArticlesTürkçe baskı
Conceptual sociological study · 22 November 2026 · Adem Afşar

A person leaves the bathroom, cleans their nose, washes their face and dries their hands. A moment earlier, they tolerated for a short while something that had been inside or had just left the body. Yet as time passes, their connection with it weakens. It may be easy to clean one’s own mucus from the sink when it has just landed there; once it has dried, knowing it came from oneself does not remove the disgust. When it belongs to someone else, expectations of privacy and courtesy join the reaction.

The body’s boundary and disgust

This is more than a matter of cleanliness. A substance that performs a function inside the body takes on a different meaning when it leaves. Time, place, ownership and the way it is left behind change our response. Disgust may protect us from possible contamination, and it may also reflect social classifications that define what belongs where. Mary Douglas’s work on purity, pollution and social boundaries offers a useful framework for examining how these classifications acquire meaning [1].

The fact that feces can carry some disease-causing agents gives handwashing after using the toilet and keeping food areas clean a real health rationale. Some bacteria, viruses and parasites can spread when traces of feces reach the mouth through hands, water, food or surfaces [2]. This does not mean every trace carries disease, nor that smell reliably indicates illness. Health precautions do not, by themselves, explain the full social meaning of disgust.

We cannot assume that bodily needs have always been hidden to the same degree throughout human history. In ancient Rome, people used public latrines in which they sat side by side. This shows that the social organization of toilet use can vary; it does not show that defecation and eating were treated as the same activity. Shared latrines also had their own spatial boundaries and forms of privacy [3].

The movement of bodily functions into private spaces can be related to changing social habits over time. Norbert Elias’s study of long-term social processes helps explain the increasing regulation of bodily conduct and the movement of some acts from public settings into private spaces [4]. This change did not happen in the same way or at the same pace in every society; it nevertheless shows how everyday habits can become rules of courtesy and privacy.

Changing social needs and rules

We cannot state with a precise count that hunter-gatherer communities had few rules while settled Neolithic communities had many. Most of their rules were not written down and are not directly visible in the archaeological record. A sounder comparison asks which shared problems needed regulation as ways of life changed.

In hunter-gatherer life, food sharing, cooperative hunts, childcare, access to resources, conflict resolution and group mobility mattered. Research shows that sharing and cooperation were central to these communities’ lives and that norms could support collective action [5]. Present-day communities cannot be treated as exact replicas of every prehistoric society; they do show that mobile and small-scale groups also need shared norms.

In the Neolithic, production, storage and settled life created new needs for coordination. Once food was stored, more enduring questions arose: how to protect people’s labor, seed and stores; how to distribute food in times of scarcity; and who could access land. Evidence of early granaries in the Jordan Valley suggests that storage and new forms of social organization emerged even before agriculture was fully established [6]. Sedentism did not automatically create hierarchy or inequality; research on Çatalhöyük shows that storage and differences in property could coexist with relatively egalitarian features [7].

The story is not “first we had no rules, then we became rule-bound.” Different ways of life required different needs to be coordinated. One central function of social rules was to make reciprocal benefit sustainable.

Benefit, value balance and reciprocity

People’s contributions are not identical. Their labor, knowledge, needs and capacity to support others differ. Shared life continues not by giving everyone the same thing, but by recognizing different contributions and needs and establishing a sustainable balance among them.

Money and precious metals can become common measures for comparing different goods and forms of labor. Literature can carry people’s experiences and feelings to others, including later generations. Tone of voice, facial expression, courtesy, thanks and apology are also symbolic means of regulating relationships. There is no need to claim that all of these were consciously invented at one moment for this single purpose. As they are repeated, however, they can become ways of making shared life easier, recognizing contributions and sustaining relationships.

These methods build trust when behavior supports them. Thanks becomes meaningful when conduct shows that a contribution has not been forgotten. An apology becomes a genuine repair when hurtful behavior changes. When words have no behavioral counterpart, courtesy can preserve only the appearance of a relationship rather than shared life itself.

Reciprocity is not limited to an exchange between the same people at the same time. Over a lifetime, people move between giving and needing support. A person who provides care today may need care tomorrow. Helping someone should not depend on calculating how much they contributed in the past. A person has value because they are human; both the one who helps and the one who receives help are human. Recognizing that we may pass through different stages of life moves care beyond personal gain.

From the immediate circle to universal empathy

Even without seeing other societies, a person can observe difference in their immediate circle. In a family, neighborhood or workplace, not everyone shares the same opinions, food preferences, colors, ways of working or dreams. This experience can be a starting point for understanding that no individual’s or community’s standards represent all people.

The immediate circle is not an exact model of the whole world, but it reveals on a small scale the basic issues of living together: difference, disagreement, accommodation, need and reciprocity. From this experience, one can accept that people one has never met have lives of their own, even without knowing their details. Empathy is not simply projecting our own feelings onto another person. At its foundation is the recognition that my world is not the whole human world.

The mistake is not caring about the benefit of one’s own community. The mistake is treating events within one’s own circle as the whole world’s reality, accepting one’s own knowledge as the only truth and making others’ experiences invisible. Language, religion, ancestry and other identities are not problems in themselves. When they become measures of who is right, valuable or worthy of help, solidarity within a community can coexist with exclusion.

Rules that sustain common benefit

A person joining a shared setting has a responsibility to learn the rules already in force there. Shared life, however, does not mean existing rules can never change. Participants can understand what sustains the common balance and take part in forming and revising rules without undermining it.

A person or group may receive less of one opportunity while benefiting more from another. Common benefit does not mean that everyone receives the same thing; it means considering different benefits together. Education should not persuade people to accept a predetermined conclusion. It should help them see actual conditions, compare different benefits and make their own judgments. Whether an arrangement produces common benefit is assessed by examining its effects objectively, not merely by believing that it does.

A rule should be judged by whether it helps sustain common benefit, not by how old or familiar it is. If it serves that purpose, it can be preserved; if it no longer does so or excludes some people from shared benefit, it should be reconsidered together. Change, too, should protect the balance of shared life.

Conclusion: the principle called utopian

People sustaining reciprocal benefit despite their differences, supporting one another through periods of vulnerability and valuing a person beyond their usefulness are sometimes described as utopian aims. Here, “utopia” is not a claim that this is truly impossible. It is a word used to reveal the scale of the question and ask where we are going wrong.

Perhaps the basic mistake is that people can see differences in their own circles yet fail to extend that observation to distant communities. They accept that not everyone around them is the same, but when they consider other societies, they may treat their own knowledge and values as the measure of all humanity. Rules developed to sustain reciprocal benefit can then become boundaries that protect only “our” benefit.

The differences we see in our immediate circles give us a starting point for understanding human diversity without knowing every person. The task of shared life is not to erase these differences, but to build rules together that sustain reciprocal benefit and human dignity within them.

This is the awareness that can be passed to future generations: not to mistake the world of one’s own circle for the whole world; to accept difference as an ordinary part of being human; to assess common benefit objectively; and to shape rules according to the mission of shared life. What is called “utopia” may be a principle we have not yet turned into a general and reliable social rule for everyone.

Adem Afşar’s Sociological Analysis

Adem Afşar · Interdisciplinary Sociological Analyst

Bodily boundaries begin in personal experience, while rules around cleanliness, privacy and mutual respect take shape in shared life. Their meaning depends not only on disgust, but also on people’s ability to live without harming one another and while preserving reciprocal benefit. We should consider how common life is sustained under different conditions without treating the standards learned in our own surroundings as the single measure for all humanity.
