Migration Policies and Societal Security

Thinking of Türkiye only as a country receiving immigrants obscures part of its population movements. People leave the country as well as enter it. These movements occur for different reasons, under different legal statuses and within different life stories. Their common feature is that a society must adapt to human mobility. Alongside recording these movements, the state's task is to maintain arrangements capable of responding to changing needs.

The central argument I advance in this essay is that, in managing large and rapid migration movements, institutional capacity, political resolve, continuity in governance and social trust must be assessed together. I also approach Türkiye's efforts in response to mass displacement from Syria within this framework. In my assessment, a government able to shoulder the same responsibility over a long period and retain public support has played an important part in this process. This political assessment needs to be discussed alongside concrete services, difficulties encountered and continuing needs.

Türkiye as a Country of Immigration and Emigration

According to the 2025 international migration statistics published by TÜİK on 24 June 2026, 393,829 people immigrated to Türkiye and 403,216 left Türkiye. Of those arriving, 91,952 were Turkish citizens; of those leaving, 155,119 were Turkish citizens.[1] It would therefore be incorrect to interpret total arrivals and departures solely as foreigners arriving and Turkish citizens leaving.

The scope of these data also matters: Syrians under temporary protection are not included in the relevant TÜİK statistics.[1] We cannot use annual migration flows interchangeably with the temporary-protection population present in the country on a particular date. One describes movement over a period; the other describes a population registered on a given date.

When I think of substitutions in football to describe the many arrivals and departures in Türkiye, I do not mean that people replace one another in terms of their qualities. The analogy concerns the pace and simultaneity of movement. Reducing incoming and outgoing people to a numerical balance is insufficient to understand the changes taking place. Occupation, age, education, length of stay and reasons for migration must also be examined.

Different Movements, Different Responsibilities

Moving abroad for education, migrating for work and fleeing war to seek protection are not the same experience. The opportunities and risks a person encounters differ in each case. Changes in public personnel within the country are also a separate process from international migration. These areas may be considered together in terms of human resources, but cannot be quantified as though they were parts of the same event.

We must also pay attention to legal terminology when discussing the population arriving in Türkiye from Syria. The Presidency of Migration Management explains the temporary-protection arrangements for people arriving within the specified scope because of events in Syria.[2] Without confusing the everyday use of the word refugee with the details of legal statuses, this essay focuses particularly on the service and integration needs arising from the presence of Syrians under temporary protection.

The International Organization for Migration's 2025 annual report on Türkiye, citing Migration Management data, gives the year-end number of Syrians under temporary protection as 2.347.756.[3] This scale shows that we face not simply a border-management issue, but a responsibility for public services and living together extending over many years.

What Do I Mean by Strong Governance?

By strong governance, I mean not only the ability to make decisions but also the ability to provide the personnel, budget and inter-institutional cooperation those decisions require. Announcing a decision is a beginning. Sustaining its implementation across provinces, adapting it to changing needs and monitoring shortcomings require further work.

This is where I see the value of continuity in governance. An approach that establishes a registration system one year but neglects educational needs the next cannot address migration's long-term effects. Children's school attendance, adults' participation in working life and the planning of local services cannot be treated in isolation. Pursuing the same objective over many years may make these connections easier to establish.

This does not mean that every government remaining in office should automatically be considered successful. The value of continuity must be assessed through implementation outcomes. I argue not for assigning unconditional value to duration itself, but for including the capacity to sustain long-term responsibility in assessments of success.

Trust in Leadership and the Expectation of Results

A citizen cannot know the technical details of everything the state does. However, citizens can observe what results promises produce over time. Their experience of unfinished and completed projects may influence their trust in subsequent policies. This personal witnessing has an important place in my assessment in favour of long-term governance.

I consider the presence of a leader deemed worthy of support important for the social basis of difficult decisions. A leader's taking responsibility during a crisis, explaining a position and following implementation may contribute to this trust. But sustaining trust also requires the ability to discuss outcomes and make problems visible. The existence of support does not remove the need to investigate shortcomings in services.

I see Marmaray as a brief illustration of this thought. Ministry records state that services across the Bosphorus began on 29 October 2013.[5] The experience of turning a long-term undertaking into a functioning service may influence citizens' expectations of governing capacity in other areas. Nevertheless, a transport project's success does not by itself establish migration-policy outcomes; migration must be evaluated using its own indicators.

Capacity Demonstrated in Migration from Syria

I value the service capacity Türkiye developed in response to the large population movement from Syria. In this assessment, I attach particular importance to children's education, registration processes and the assumption of long-term public responsibility. The educational effort appears not only in the government's own account. UNICEF's July 2024 report explicitly records the substantial work of the Turkish government and its partners to enable Syrian children to participate in education.[4]

The same report also examines out-of-school children and barriers to access.[4] These two findings must be read together. Providing an important service should not render remaining needs invisible; continuing needs should not erase services already provided. Policy evaluation must see what has been done and what remains to be done with equal clarity.

My positive assessment concerns sustained effort and service-delivery capacity in the face of a substantial responsibility. Each area must be examined separately before claiming that all problems have been solved or that every practice has been equally successful. The value of strong governance should also be sought in its willingness to take ownership of continuing problems.

Preserving Public Order and the Scale of Success

In my assessment, Türkiye's ability to maintain public order nationwide while accommodating a population of this size over many years is a significant achievement internationally. By success, I do not mean that no individual or local criminal incidents have occurred. My criterion is preventing a large population movement from becoming a widespread and persistent public-order crisis. This assessment expresses my political and social observation.

Individual incidents do not alone explain overall governing capacity; nor should their importance to victims be underestimated. Every incident must be investigated, individual responsibility established and victims protected. For the same reason, attributing one person's crime to an entire population cannot substitute for a security assessment.

In public security, I consider current records, identification, border management, crime prevention and the functioning of law-enforcement and judicial institutions together. Resolve in these areas must be accompanied by respect for individuals' rights and knowledge of the specific case. Security policy should enable both citizens and people under protection to conduct everyday life within predictable rules.

I believe strong and stable governance has contributed significantly to carrying this responsibility. The international population comparison below illustrates the scale of protection undertaken. Ranking countries' public-security outcomes, however, requires separate indicators produced for the same periods and using the same definitions.

Societal Security Means Continuity in Everyday Life

I do not limit societal security to public-order incidents. A child's access to school, people's ability to find housing, the regulation of work and predictable public services are also parts of secure shared life. This framework requires considering citizens' needs alongside the basic security of people seeking protection.

Population growth in a neighbourhood may change demand for schools, transport or healthcare. Yet attributing every difficulty to migration without investigation is not explanatory. Housing supply, economic conditions, existing service capacity and population distribution must also be examined. Understanding local needs requires looking at provincial and neighbourhood conditions alongside national totals.

Listening to citizens' concerns about access to services is not the same as portraying all migrants as a threat. Likewise, caring about migrants' security does not require dismissing local residents' difficulties. Government's task is to identify problems concretely and develop fair, workable responses.

Employment, Education and Living Together

Migration's social effects are closely connected with the jobs people can obtain and their working conditions. Policy should therefore meet labour needs while upholding registered employment, occupational safety and equitable conditions. An employer's responsibility does not disappear because of a worker's origin. Applying common employment rules matters more than setting local and migrant workers against each other.

In education, enrolment numbers alone are insufficient. School attendance, language learning and children's ability to participate in the learning environment must also be monitored. Supporting local schools and teachers can strengthen the social foundations of this process. Sustaining the same service as needs expand requires joint planning by central government and local units.

Social integration does not mean expecting everyone to share the same identity. Understanding common rules, respecting one another's rights in everyday relationships and resolving problems through accessible institutions are more concrete measures of living together. Regularly reporting results in these areas can make trust less dependent on rumours.

How Should We Assess Success?

We should state clearly which questions we seek to answer when evaluating migration policy: How current are the records? How is children's educational participation changing? How are local service needs met? What is the situation regarding access to registered employment and working conditions? Do complaint channels work? On what grounds are public resources allocated?

These questions do not produce a single success score. They do, however, connect general opinions with concrete implementation outcomes. A government with political support answering criticism through data is consistent with my understanding of strong governance. Criticism should likewise identify which practice it evaluates and through which outcome.

Türkiye's geographical position and regional relationships prevent migration from being treated solely as an issue of domestic governance. International responsibility-sharing and conditions in neighbouring countries are also parts of long-term policy. However, external developments do not remove the responsibility to monitor domestic service quality.

International Statistical Comparison of Mass Protection

This comparison is based on UNHCR's refugee-protection population, rather than economic migration selected through educational and occupational points. Temporary protection in Türkiye is defined through the relevant population groups arriving from Syria; it is not a labour programme selecting people by qualifications or professional-competence scores.[2] This does not mean arrivals lack education or occupations. Other countries' admission routes and security checks are not identical either. The comparison concerns the size of protection populations recorded within the same statistical category.

UNHCR's year-end 2025 “Refugees under UNHCR's mandate” series and the World Bank's 2025 total-population data were used for the same period.[6][7] People whose asylum applications remain pending, Palestinian refugees covered by UNRWA and UNHCR's separate category of “other people in need of international protection” are not included in this table. It therefore does not represent all foreign nationals or all forcibly displaced people in these countries.

Türkiye has one of the largest absolute hosting totals in this sample. In population-relative terms, however, the indicators for Lebanon, Chad, Jordan, Uganda and Germany are higher than Türkiye's. Assessments of scale must therefore consider both total numbers and national population. This seven-country table is not a world ranking.

Another important long-term comparison is 2021: UNHCR reported that Türkiye hosted the world's largest refugee population at that year-end, approximately 3.8 million, while Uganda and Pakistan each hosted approximately 1.5 million and Germany approximately 1.3 million.[10] This numerically supports the view that Türkiye undertook a very large responsibility. Looking only at year-end 2025 does not show the hosting scale in earlier years.

How Comparable Are Public Security Data?

For Türkiye, a statement in the 2018 activity report of the Grand National Assembly's Human Rights Inquiry Committee gives incidents involving Syrians as an annual average of 1.32% of all public-order incidents in 2014–2017.[8] This does not mean that “1.32% of Syrians committed crimes”; the denominator is the total number of incidents, not the number of people. Nor can the report's statement be used as a current 2025 crime rate.

In Germany's Federal Criminal Police Office 2024 police statistics, excluding immigration-law violations, 172,203 suspects were recorded in the “Zuwanderer” group comprising specified protection and residence statuses, compared with 1,967,731 suspects overall within the same scope.[9] Dividing these figures gives approximately 8.8%. This does not mean that 8.8% of the people in the group committed crimes or were convicted. The statistics measure suspects, not convictions.

Dividing these percentages cannot establish how many times more successful Türkiye is than Germany. The periods, population groups and measurement units differ. Türkiye's data concern Syrians, while Germany's cover several nationalities and residence statuses. A compatible series using the same offence definitions, age and sex distributions, population-relative rates and recording methods is needed. As no such comparison using common definitions could be established from the sources examined, no ranking of public-security success was produced.

Crime statistics and the occurrence of a widespread nationwide public-order crisis are also not the same indicator. The latter claim requires separate definitions and examination of incidents' extent, duration, severity and relationship to migration. The size of the hosted population does not alone prove how effectively public order was preserved or which political factor produced that outcome.

Result of the comparison: Common international population series support the conclusion that Türkiye undertook a very large mass-protection responsibility. My view of accommodation on this scale as a significant success is an assessment with a numerical context. The stronger claim that it is “more successful in public security than the countries compared” cannot be verified through the presently incompatible security percentages.

The Value of Continuity Emerges in Sustained Responsibility

In Türkiye, a country of both immigration and emigration, the central issue is how social life can be sustained amid change on this scale. In my assessment, stable governance, resolute leadership and public support are important foundations for carrying this responsibility. This is my political assessment of continuity's contribution to governing capacity, not a definitive historical judgement about what would have happened under another government.

I seek the significance of this support not only in announcing major decisions, but in following through on responsibilities over many years. Migration policy's success should be visible in functioning institutions, sustainable services, people's security and fair arrangements for shared life. Stability's value becomes concrete when responsibility can be sustained despite each new need.
