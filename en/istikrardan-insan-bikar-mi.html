Can People Tire of Stability?

Continuity of government amid global crises and Türkiye’s comparative record

Adem Afşar · 3 November 2026
← Perspectives on Current Affairs · Comparison tables · References

I am someone who values stability. This conviction did not arise solely from books or political rhetoric. I lived through periods when the future of governments was frequently debated, days when decisions were postponed, and the effects of economic uncertainty on our daily lives. In my own experience, I saw that the public bears a substantial share of the cost of political instability.

For this reason, I value the continuity of AK Party government since the election of 3 November 2002. I feel no need to conceal my appreciation of the current administration. I also regard its ability to renew electoral support over many years as an important political achievement. Nevertheless, I believe democratic quality must be assessed in terms of law, freedoms and accountability as well as elections.

I believe the administration strives to improve people’s lives. This is my political judgment. In evaluating the results of its actions, however, I consider it important that readers can access the same documents. Appreciating a period does not require regarding every decision made during it as flawless. Giving achievements their due is compatible with acknowledging the need to correct problems.

In this essay, I examine the principal global and regional shocks our country has faced over the past twenty-four years through official international documents. My question is: What opportunities did continuity of government provide in facing these shocks; in which outcomes was it successful, and in which areas is a better balance needed?

Who pays the bill for uncertainty?

The formation or collapse of a government, or its inability to make decisions, may initially seem to be a problem for politicians. Yet uncertainty can affect businesses’ investment decisions, banks’ lending conditions and public financing. Postponed investment may mean businesses that never open; higher financing costs may mean lower production and reduced public resources.

Constantly changing priorities can also make long-term projects harder to complete. Reconsidering files after every change of personnel, unclear responsibilities and disrupted coordination between institutions consume society’s time. These effects are not an inevitable consequence of every change of government; clear rules and strong institutions can reduce transition costs.

It would be wrong to explain Türkiye’s 2001 crisis solely through political disagreements. The OECD’s 2002 review also points to banking fragility, fiscal weaknesses and external vulnerability. However, it notes that political tensions and concerns about the continuation of the programme kept the risk premium high. Political confidence is therefore one element of economic confidence, rather than its sole explanation. 1

Continuity and accumulated institutional experience

Continuity of the governing party after 2002 does not mean that the entire period passed under the same cabinet or system of government. The Presidential System of Government was introduced in 2018. Accordingly, the lifespan of a government, political alliances, policy continuity and institutional continuity are used here as distinct concepts. 2

Coalitions are not inherently unsuccessful either. A coalition with a common programme, a division of responsibilities and a mechanism for resolving disagreements can work effectively. Under single-party government, meanwhile, institutions may lack coordination or policies may fluctuate. We need to examine not only how many parties make a decision, but also the information on which it rests and how it is implemented.

Türkiye’s reform experience supports this distinction. The World Bank stresses the importance for recovery of governments after 2002 continuing the structural reforms begun in 2001. This example shows that preserving the achievements of an earlier period and a new administration’s determination to implement policy can complement each other. 3

2008–2009: Banking resilience, substantial losses in production

The resilience of Türkiye’s banking system during the global financial crisis is a documented achievement. The IMF’s 2010 assessment lists political stability alongside fiscal discipline, financial supervision reforms, inflation targeting and the European Union process among the factors underpinning pre-crisis strengthening. Banks’ capital and liquidity buffers also supported resilience. 4

Resilience, however, does not mean coming first on every indicator. In the current World Bank series, real growth in 2009 was approximately −4.9 percent in Türkiye, −5.5 in Germany and −3.8 in Spain. Brazil’s contraction was more limited, while Poland and South Korea maintained growth. We therefore cannot say that Türkiye suffered less damage from this crisis than every country in the comparison. 5

The defensible conclusion is more specific: the relative soundness of the banking system provided important protection against the contraction in production being compounded by a major banking collapse. Identifying which instrument worked is more instructive than declaring overall success.

The European debt crisis and the global interest-rate shift

Crises are not confined to years of major collapse. The European debt crisis and expectations in 2013 of a change in US monetary policy affected capital flows to emerging economies. The IMF’s review of Türkiye records these fluctuations and the importance of dependence on short-term external financing. 6

This experience explains why an established administration must work on reserves, debt maturity and diversity of financing. Clear centres of decision-making can facilitate intervention; a sound economic balance sheet determines the room available for it. The magnitude of external shocks and vulnerabilities accumulated domestically must be assessed together.

COVID-19: The achievement and cost of maintaining production

2020 provides one of the clearest results of the comparison. In the same World Bank data series, Türkiye grew by approximately 1.8 percent while the other five economies in the comparison group contracted. The contraction reached 10.9 percent in Spain and 4.1 percent in Germany. Türkiye’s capacity to sustain production clearly stands out on this measure. 5

This outcome cannot simply be dismissed as coincidence. The IMF’s 2021 assessment notes the role of rapid credit expansion, public banks and liquidity support in the recovery. The same document also records that these choices intensified inflation, external imbalances and pressure on reserves. It thus presents the gains achieved alongside the risks incurred. 7

Nor can the pandemic’s balance sheet be limited to production. In the OECD/EU comparison for March 2020–October 2022, reported COVID-19 deaths per million people were 1,214 in Türkiye, 1,848 in Germany, 2,431 in Spain and 3,127 in Poland. These figures, however, are not age-standardised measures of total pandemic losses. Differences in testing and death-registration practices limit the comparison. Türkiye is not included in the same report’s excess-mortality comparison. This table alone cannot establish superior health management. 8

Maintaining economic activity must not make loss of life or inequalities in education and employment invisible. A full social balance sheet encompassing health, education and lost income requires broader data than a growth table.

Growth or lower inflation?

We must not confuse different years here. Türkiye’s real growth of 1.8 percent relates to 2020, while its annual average inflation of 72.3 percent relates to 2022. Average consumer inflation in 2020 was approximately 12.3 percent. These two figures therefore cannot establish that 72.3 percent inflation was the necessary price of 1.8 percent growth. 5, 10

Preserving productive capacity during the pandemic was important. A business that closes because of a temporary loss of income may also lose its employees, supply relationships and years of accumulated skills. Credit and support that prevent this rupture have value beyond a single year’s growth rate. However, when increases in spending and credit exceed productive capacity, they can raise demand for foreign currency and pressure on prices. Indeed, the IMF’s pandemic assessment records the recovery alongside the vulnerabilities that emerged. 7

Contraction does not automatically halt price increases either. Production may shrink while energy or foreign-exchange costs rise; economic stagnation can coexist with high inflation. I therefore do not believe the alternatives should be framed as “let production stop” or “let us grow at any cost.” The aim is to combine support that protects businesses and employment with price stability, productivity and predictable policies. These data cannot tell us exactly how many percentage points of growth or inflation an alternative policy would have produced.

Real growth is an increase in production adjusted for price changes; it is not merely a rise in prices. Yet greater production does not show that every household’s purchasing power has increased. We need to be able to consider the losses of those on fixed incomes, businesses’ survival and workers’ ability to retain their jobs within the same assessment.

The war in Ukraine: A shared shock, different inflation outcomes

Russia’s war against Ukraine affected many countries through energy and food markets. The FAO’s international food commodity price index rose by 14.3 percent on average in 2022 compared with the previous year. This index is not the same measure as countries’ supermarket prices or overall consumer inflation; it indicates a shared external pressure. 9

While Türkiye’s annual average consumer inflation in 2022 was 72.3 percent, rates in the comparison group ranged from 5.1 to 14.4 percent. The OECD’s review of Türkiye includes depreciation of the lira and domestic policy choices alongside global energy and commodity prices in its explanation of inflation. 10, 11

We must therefore acknowledge two realities together: the external shock facing Türkiye was real, but that shared shock is insufficient to explain all of the country’s price increases. Resilience in production does not automatically protect the purchasing power of people on fixed incomes.

Germany is also a useful example. The OECD’s 2025 review notes that the rapid replacement of Russian gas with other sources, supported by government assistance, prevented a deeper contraction. This shows that adaptation and coordination also matter in other countries. We can compare Türkiye’s production growth with Germany’s price stability on different criteria; we cannot derive an overall ranking of administrations from a single indicator. 12

Syria and forced migration: Countries do not carry the same burden

The cost of wars does not end with energy bills. In UNHCR’s end-2023 data, Türkiye hosts approximately 3.3 million refugees and Germany 2.6 million. These are rounded figures based on the report’s definitions, not current population figures for 2026. 13

This difference must be considered when assessing Türkiye’s capacity in education, health, housing and local services. Total numbers alone cannot establish per capita costs, the fiscal burden or the success of migration management. Population size, income levels, external support and legal statuses must also be considered. Protecting people and supporting host communities’ service capacity must be considered together.

The Red Sea, drought and trade routes

UNCTAD’s 2024 assessment shows that security-related disruptions to Suez transits and water restrictions in the Panama Canal strained global transport at the same time. Suez transits fell by 42 percent and Panama transits by 49 percent from the levels the report treats as their peaks. These percentages do not represent Türkiye’s trade losses or an annual contraction in world trade. 14

Such events show that crisis management requires preparation beyond daily political debates: alternative routes, supplier diversification, port capacity and continuous exchange of information with industries. The opportunities provided by Türkiye’s logistical position can count as successes only on the basis of actual transport volumes, costs and investment data.

Earthquakes: A major burden that was not shared

The 2023 earthquakes cannot be presented in this essay as a shock that the other countries experienced simultaneously. The World Bank’s early assessment of 27 February 2023 estimated direct physical damage at $34.2 billion. This was neither the total cost of reconstruction nor a final estimate of losses. 15

This additional burden must be considered when interpreting Türkiye’s subsequent economic outcomes. Nevertheless, an increase in production from reconstruction spending does not mean that lost welfare has been fully restored. Rebuilding a destroyed structure generates economic activity while society is also trying to compensate for a major loss of assets. Preparedness and oversight before a disaster are as much part of the record of government as coordination afterwards.

2025–2026: Avoiding premature judgments about continuing shocks

Tariff uncertainty, regional wars and energy costs must be included in current assessments. The WTO’s update of 8 October 2026 records stronger-than-expected adaptation in global merchandise trade alongside a weaker outlook for services. The annual rates for 2026 and 2027 in the report are forecasts, not completed-year results. 16

The IMF’s October 2026 World Economic Outlook also draws attention to the difficulties of energy-importing economies, inflationary pressures and shrinking policy buffers. These documents describe the current external environment; they do not prove that Türkiye is the country managing all these shocks best. 17

What stability means for society

The comparison shows strong results for Türkiye in pandemic-period production and subsequent growth in total output. The same comparison also reveals the heavy cost in price stability. A political debate that selects only one of these findings leaves out half of the social experience.

An important advantage of a long-serving administration is that it does not have to rebuild institutional operations every time a crisis occurs. Established responsibilities, implementation experience and continuing investment can save time. But institutions’ ability to communicate accurate information upwards and to correct mistaken decisions matters as much as a clear hierarchy.

These tables are not an experiment measuring success or failure caused by continuity of government alone. Countries differ in income, population, production structure, dependence on tourism and energy resources. Nor can these data directly reveal the counterfactual outcome that the same country would have experienced under a different government.

Can people, then, tire of stability? Over time, they may forget the effort through which familiar services and security were achieved. Noticing losses that were prevented may be harder than noticing a problem that occurs. For this reason, I consider it important to explain services delivered and opportunities preserved with supporting documents. Public knowledge of them helps people base their judgments on firmer ground.

The value I place on stability also carries the memory of uncertainties I experienced in the past. One reason I appreciate the continuity of this administration is the importance I attach to following through on long-term work and using accumulated experience in crises. I do not claim this always happens with equal success. I believe we must consider growing costs while acknowledging the gains shown by the sources.

I value stability because I believe a country needs continuity to build its future. Strengthening what that continuity means in people’s lives requires protecting production, purchasing power and fair services together.

Data appendix and method

The comparison group is not a global league table retrospectively ranked by results. Germany was chosen for industry and the European energy shock; Spain for tourism and the pandemic; Poland for convergence within Europe; South Korea for manufacturing and exports; and Brazil as a large emerging-economy comparison. None is a precisely matched control country for Türkiye. Differences between advanced and emerging economies have been retained in the interpretation.

The macroeconomic tables use World Bank World Development Indicators series drawn from the same source. The API responses were last updated on 8 October 2026, and accessed on 3 November 2026. The comparison window is 2009–2024; documents from 2025–2026 are used separately for current context. Historical growth rates in earlier publications may differ because of subsequent national accounts revisions. Figures are rounded to one decimal place; missing values have not been treated as zero.

Limits of the claims

ClaimAssessment in this study
Türkiye maintained production better than the comparison group in 2020.Supported by real growth data; does not encompass all pandemic costs.
Türkiye suffered less damage than all other countries in 2009.Not supported; Poland and South Korea grew.
Institutional and policy continuity can contribute to resilience.Consistent with reports from the period; these tables alone do not measure causation.
All inflation in Türkiye results from external wars.Not supported; official international assessments also record domestic factors.
A lower reported COVID-19 death rate establishes overall health success.Cannot be inferred; age structure, differences in registration and excess mortality must be considered.
The final balance sheet of the 2026 crises is available.Cannot be inferred; the year is continuing and annual projections are not actual outcomes.

This text covers the principal documentable shocks; it is not an exhaustive inventory of every adverse event over 24 years or of all fiscal and social consequences. Further comparisons are needed for real wages, poverty, learning losses, health outcomes and the coverage of fiscal support. This text makes no claim to overall superiority in those areas.
