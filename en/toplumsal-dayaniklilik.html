Adem Afşar · Conceptual Study · 1 November 2026

Does a society’s ability to withstand crisis depend solely on individuals’ sacrifices? Or does resilience require institutional and structural conditions beyond solidarity?

Societies may face problems of different kinds, including natural disasters, economic contraction, epidemics, political conflicts and technological transformations.

Responses to these problems reveal not only a society’s existing strength but also its capacity to adapt to changing conditions.

1. What is social resilience?

Social resilience can be understood as a society’s capacity to maintain basic functions in the face of shocks, limit resulting harm, adapt to changing conditions and reorganize its institutions when necessary.

This definition does not confine resilience to the ability to return to a previous state.

Pre-crisis conditions may themselves contain inequality, insecurity or structural inadequacies.

A society’s resilience should be assessed not only by overcoming a crisis but also by the social order it produces afterward.

2. Are solidarity and resilience the same?

Solidarity refers to individuals’ ability to support one another when facing a shared problem.

Resilience is a broader capacity encompassing this support together with resource use, functioning institutions, implementation of decisions and the continuation of social life.

For example, following a major earthquake, people may provide one another with food, shelter and transport.

Yet if health services, communications, infrastructure and public coordination are not functioning, society’s crisis-management capacity may remain limited despite strong solidarity.

Solidarity

A relationship of mutual assistance and support in the face of shared problems.

Resilience

The capacity to preserve basic functions in the face of shocks, adapt and restructure when necessary.

These concepts may support each other but should not be used interchangeably.

3. An assessment through Ibn Khaldun

Ibn Khaldun’s understanding of asabiyyah is an important starting point for understanding the political and social consequences of communities’ capacity for collective action.

Asabiyyah may strengthen bonds among a community’s members and their capacity for joint action.

However, social resilience in its contemporary sense is not a concept Ibn Khaldun directly used.

The assessment here therefore compares his historical analysis with contemporary debates on resilience.

4. Institutions’ role in resilience

Societies are more than the sum of individuals. Institutions such as law, education, healthcare, the economy and public administration perform important functions in sustaining shared life.

An institution’s existence does not show that it functions effectively under every condition.

Institutional resilience requires clearly defined duties, information flows, appropriate resource use and the capacity to make decisions under exceptional conditions.

What matters is not merely whether institutions appear strong, but how far they fulfil their functions when facing problems.

5. Individuals, society and responsibility

Social resilience is also related to individual responsibilities.

While seeking to protect their lives and immediate circles, people can also contribute to the functioning of social institutions.

However, individual sacrifice should not become an expectation that people continually assume duties institutions ought to fulfil.

An important connection with the Circles of Responsibility approach emerges here.

Individuals have responsibilities to themselves, their families and immediate circles, while public institutions have defined obligations toward society as a whole.

Confusing these responsibilities can make assessing a crisis’s real causes more difficult.

6. Adapting to crisis

Social resilience is not solely an effort to preserve the existing order.

Sometimes a crisis exposes the inadequacies of existing institutions and habits.

In such cases, resilience may require developing more effective methods rather than continuing previous practices unchanged.

The distinction between resisting change and adapting to change is therefore important in evaluating resilience.

Central research question

Is a stronger indicator of social resilience a society preserving its existing order during crisis, or its ability to change inadequate institutions?

7. Resilience and inequality

Not all members of society are affected equally by crises.

Income, housing, healthcare access, age, working conditions and social relationships may differentiate individuals’ opportunities in the face of crisis.

Society remaining standing overall therefore does not mean every member is protected to a similar degree.

Assessing resilience should examine not only institutional continuity but also who bears the concentrated harms.

8. Social learning after crisis

Emerging from a crisis and learning from it are not the same.

Post-crisis assessments are important for identifying deficiencies, developing new methods and improving institutions.

Social learning means not merely remembering problems but transferring experience into future decisions and practices.

The long-term dimension of resilience thus also relates to how far society converts its experience into institutional knowledge.

9. The limits of resilience

Resilience may not signify a positive outcome in every situation.

For example, an unjust order surviving crises does not make that order desirable for society.

Resilience assessment should therefore not be confined to whether a system continues.

The nature of the preserved order, its benefits, resulting harms and individuals’ living conditions should also be considered.

10. Conclusion

Social resilience requires considering solidarity, institutional capacity, resource management and adaptability together.

Solidarity may be strong while institutions remain inadequate.

Institutions may survive while particular sections of society suffer serious harm.

Measuring resilience therefore requires more than survival or continuity of order.

Social resilience concerns not only how much shock a society can withstand but also what conditions it can reproduce afterward.

This conclusion is the study’s conceptual assessment, not a direct quotation from any thinker.
