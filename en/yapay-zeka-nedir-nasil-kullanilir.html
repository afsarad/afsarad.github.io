What Is Artificial Intelligence, and How Can We Use It Responsibly?

Adem Afşar · 16 November 2026

What is AI? Everyday uses Asking questions Trust Thinking Parallel time Short answers

Artificial intelligence is a broad term for systems that use patterns in data to perform tasks such as prediction, classification, or content generation. In everyday life, it can help us draft text, learn about a topic, compare options, and organise work. Receiving a useful answer, however, does not establish that it is correct or that the system should make our decisions.

Our central question is this: how can we benefit from these capabilities while retaining responsibility for thinking and evaluation?

What is artificial intelligence, and how does it work?

Artificial intelligence is not a single type of program. A system that classifies images has a different task from one that generates a conversation. This article focuses on generative language models that provide written answers to our questions.

During training, these models learn linguistic patterns in data; they then generate a continuation of text in response to a given context. Some applications can also use search, calculation, or file-analysis tools. An answer is therefore not necessarily a compilation of information just found on the internet. Fluent language alone does not establish accuracy. NIST identifies the production of plausible but false or fabricated information as an important risk of generative systems.[1]

How can we use AI in everyday life?

A useful starting point is a small task with a result that can be checked. Organising meeting notes, asking for examples of a difficult concept, or grouping a shopping list are possible tasks. The user's own knowledge helps them assess the result.

A shopkeeper might organise product descriptions; a student might compare explanations of an unfamiliar argument; a researcher might classify their own notes according to their questions. In each case, the purpose and evaluation criteria should be clear. Making work faster and improving its quality need to be assessed separately.

How should we ask AI a question?

A useful question explains the task and the conditions under which we are working. Rather than simply asking for an article, specifying the subject, reader, length, and limits makes the work easier to evaluate.

“Prepare an explanation of neighbourhood solidarity, approximately 500 words long, for adults encountering the topic for the first time. Use examples of neighbourly relations and shared spaces. Distinguish my interpretation from research findings; explicitly state when you cannot verify a source.”

We can then challenge the answer: “What is the weak point of this argument?”, “Which assumption did you use?”, or “Have you added anything that changes my meaning?” Such questions do not guarantee accuracy, but they involve the user in evaluation.

Can we trust artificial intelligence?

Trust needs to be assessed in relation to the task and how the result is checked. Giving a note headings and verifying a historical claim require different checks. Names, dates, figures, and references in particular should be compared with original documents. Naming a source does not prove that it exists or supports a claim.

Users should also examine an application's data-use terms and understand what information will go where before sharing private material. UNESCO's ethical approach emphasises human oversight and accountability, calling for ultimate responsibility to remain with people and institutions.[2]

Does AI discourage people from thinking?

There is no single answer for every user. Accepting an answer without questioning it differs from using it to test our thinking. The distinction I make here is conceptual: it is not a research finding about an inevitable effect of technology use.

A person can offer an observation, ask a system for possible explanations, and compare those explanations with their own experience and sources. Thinking in this process is reconsidered rather than transmitted in one direction. Just as fluent writing can persuade a person, the person's ability to say “That is not what I meant” matters too.

What does “producing parallel time” mean?

I use this expression to describe the possibility of advancing parts of intellectual work while everyday responsibilities continue. An idea can be recorded during a short break; notes can be organised while the person attends to another task; they can then return to examine the result. Physical time does not multiply. What changes is how tasks that can progress within the same period are organised.

This is not a measured productivity gain for everyone. Checking, correcting, and reconsidering the output also takes time. Any gain is meaningful only when that effort is included.

Short questions, clear answers

Do I need programming skills to use AI?

Many conversational applications let you begin in everyday language. Advanced setups and automations may require different skills.

Does AI always look things up online?

No. Access to external sources depends on the application, available tools, and the features of the particular session.

Can I directly trust references supplied by AI?

Open the source and check its author, date, and whether it supports the claim.

Does using AI eliminate original thought?

Use alone establishes neither originality nor its absence. We need to examine who framed the question, who evaluated the interpretations, and whose position the final text expresses.

My approach to this relationship rests on a principle: people welcome convenience, but want to retain control. Artificial intelligence is valuable insofar as it gives people more time and opportunity to think.
