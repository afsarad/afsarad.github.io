The Power of Advertising in Choosing an Artificial Intelligence Platform: Visual Superiority, Comparability and Realized Benefits — Part II

Keywords: Comparative advertising, authority cues, rankings, multi-model subscription, user expectations, realized benefits.

Read Part I

Abstract

This follow-up study examines ranking tables, associations with expertise and wealth, promises of access to multiple models and age-targeted messages in advertising for artificial intelligence platforms. It argues that trust generated by advertising should not be confused with the quality of evidence, or platform access with the work a user can complete. It offers a qualitative analysis based on advertising screenshots recorded by the user, without making unverified judgments about the advertisements' technical accuracy or companies' intentions.

1. Introduction: From visual magnitude to borrowed authority

The first study discussed comparability through a portrayal of an artificial intelligence platform as a huge vehicle and its competitors as ordinary cars. New observational material reveals another form of the same problem: rather than providing a technical comparison, an advertisement can create an impression of superiority through a person's reputation, a title implying financial magnitude or a ranking table. The question therefore changes: does placing a product first show which tasks it performs better, and for which users?

2. The ranking table: Measured achievement or a designed impression?

Screenshots of a sponsored video published under the name Vincen display “$180M founder ranks every AI tool” above a numbered list of artificial intelligence brands. In some frames Vincen is first, while in others the first position is empty, suggesting an edited sequence in motion. These screenshots do not prove that the person in the video independently produced this ranking, establish its criteria or reveal the complete conversation. This uncertainty itself is the subject of our analysis: the ranking format can readily give viewers the impression that a comparison has been conducted, although its method remains invisible.

3. The implication of wealth and the transfer of authority

The phrase “$180M founder” highlights the ranker's economic success. It may evoke competence and trust among the advertisement's target audience. Yet someone's wealth or entrepreneurial background does not itself prove a particular software product superior in accuracy, safety, accessibility, task completion or cost. The distinction we propose is between 'authority attributed to a person' and 'verifiable evidence about a product.' Views should not be attributed to the person shown without separately verifying their identity, the original context of their statement and permission to use the footage.

4. The competitor's name is visible, the comparison criterion is not

Explicitly displaying competitors such as Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, DeepSeek and Grok strengthens the comparative presentation. But listing brand names is not the same as conducting a measurable comparison. Without the same version, task, dataset, price and usage limits, 'first' and 'third' are methodologically incomplete. The issue in comparative advertising is not naming competitors, but whether consumers are given the criteria that make the ranking meaningful.

5. The Merlin example: The difference between multiple access and equivalent use

Screenshots of a sponsored advertisement for Merlin by Foyer list multiple language, image and video models together, emphasizing “all in one subscription.” Convenient access to many tools through one interface may be a genuine value proposition. However, listing models in one subscription does not establish that they include all features offered by their own providers, unlimited usage or equivalent performance. Different numerical credit/energy indicators beside the models further underline the need to examine usage conditions. Their precise meaning cannot be established without reviewing plan terms. The key question for users should be not 'how many models are there?' but 'which model can I use for my work, how much, and under what conditions?'

6. The demonstrated function and the completed task

The advertisement also demonstrates summarizing a web page. Such a demonstration indicates that a particular task can be performed, but alone provides insufficient data about accuracy, fidelity to sources, reproducibility and total cost across texts of different lengths. Successfully producing a brief summary and completing a long-term research, coding or organizational workflow require different levels of assessment. The demonstration should therefore be tested again within a work scenario specific to the user.

7. Addressing age groups: An invitation to opportunity or anxiety about falling behind?

Another sponsored image promotes a training program for adults over 45 with the statement, 'Artificial intelligence will not replace you. But someone using artificial intelligence might.' The emphasis on 'just 10 minutes a day' presents learning as accessible, while 'without falling behind' raises the possibility of loss or exclusion. Two messages operate simultaneously: an opportunity to acquire skills and concern about being left behind. It cannot be said that this frightens every viewer, but perceptions can be studied in relation to age, job security, technological experience and learning goals.

8. Heterogeneous users and advertising's differing effects

The same ranking table may simplify a beginner's decision, while an experienced user may look for its criteria. The promise of many models in one subscription may appeal to someone conducting brief trials, whereas someone managing a complex project may prioritize quotas and uninterrupted work. These differences do not require classifying viewers as superior or inadequate. The sociological issue is how users' purposes, resources and experiences give different meanings to the same advertising promise.

9. Promised, expected and realized benefits

The three-part distinction proposed in the first article can be extended here: promised benefit is what advertising explicitly states or shows. Expected benefit is the conclusion viewers draw for their own work. Realized benefit is the output, cost and continuity verifiable during use. The distance between them may reflect not only the product's technical qualities but also ambiguity in advertising and the user's formation of expectations. Evaluation should therefore record advertising claims and viewers' inferences separately.

10. Testable research propositions

H1: Ranking advertisements lacking an explicit method may increase perceived superiority without technical evidence among some user groups. H2: When asked about ranking criteria, participants with stronger critical evaluation tendencies may lower their advertising credibility ratings. H3: Promises of multi-model access may inflate expected benefits when quotas are not disclosed beforehand. H4: A 'falling behind' frame may increase the desire to acquire skills but also provoke resistance to the advertisement among some viewers. H5: Work goals and cost limits established before exposure to advertising may reduce the expectation–experience gap in platform selection. These are hypotheses for future experiments and surveys, not verified findings.

11. Research design and ethical limits

The next research stage should archive dated complete advertisements, sponsor information, the original context of images and audio, terms of use and comparative claims. Participants could view part of an advertisement and rate perceived superiority, trust and purchase intention; researchers could then examine whether assessments change after explaining ranking methods and subscription limits. Experimental samples should encompass different purposes and experience levels. Screenshots alone must not be used to claim that speakers approved the advertisement, produced an actual ranking or that companies deliberately intended to mislead.

Conclusion: Ranking first does not mean being best suited to a user

Looking large in an image, appearing first on a list or offering many models under one subscription does not itself mean that users will complete their work. Advertising's persuasive force and a product's user-specific benefits are separate questions. This installment deepens the first article's critique of comparability through authority, rankings and subscription access. The central proposition remains: the impression of superiority offered to consumers becomes meaningful only through explicit criteria and verifiable usage outcomes.
