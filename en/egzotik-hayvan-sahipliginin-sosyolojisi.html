If There Is a Python Upstairs: The Sociology of Unusual Animal Keeping

Why do people keep animals beyond familiar household pets?

A report brought news of a sighting of a lion in Sarıyer, Istanbul. The Istanbul Governor’s Office said searches found no concrete evidence that a lion was in the area; after reviewing later footage, officials assessed that the animal was neither a lion nor another big cat. We therefore cannot describe a lion roaming Sarıyer as a confirmed event. The report does, however, invite a broader question: Why might someone keep a tiger, python or other animal outside the familiar range of household pets?

This essay does not label people who make such choices or diagnose their personalities. It asks what individual motives, social meanings and historical conditions may shape a choice that falls outside commonly accepted everyday practices. We approach the question through research and verifiable examples.

Need or desire?

We cannot place the desire to keep a tiger or python on the same level as basic needs such as food, shelter and safety. Here, “need” refers less to a biological necessity than to a search for meaning, curiosity, attachment, an activity or distinction. Even so, this desire cannot be reduced to a single cause. For one person it may be admiration; for another, an interest dating back to childhood; for someone else, companionship and care.

A mixed-methods study of 220 reptile keepers in Portugal found that participants described affection for their animals in terms of love, admiration and passion. The researchers grouped motivations for long-term keeping into four categories: convenience, entertainment or hobby, companionship, and a sense of duty of care. Sixty-four per cent of respondents regarded their reptile as a family member. These findings do not prove that all reptile keepers share the same motives; the sample was limited to 220 people in Portugal who answered the survey. They do show why exotic animal keeping cannot be explained only as display or thrill-seeking.

A separate study of 719 people participating in online reptile groups in Brazil found that keepers could have multiple motivations; emotional reasons were prominent, alongside entertainment and expectations of convenience. A key question raised by these studies is whether the belief that an animal is easy to care for matches its actual biological needs. The researchers caution that providing appropriate conditions for reptiles may be more demanding than commonly assumed. The point is not to condemn an owner’s intentions, but to understand that good intentions and adequate care are not always the same thing.

Mike Tyson’s tigers: one answer to “why?”

It is verifiable that Mike Tyson kept tigers in the past. A small but important correction: the animals were tigers, not leopards. In a 2024 conversation with Rosie Perez, Tyson said that while he was in prison, his car dealer suggested how cool it would be to have a tiger beside him in a Ferrari; Tyson said he wanted tiger cubs, and three arrived at his home. In his own account, the initial spark was an unusual, striking image that brought a car and a tiger together. This does not tell us everything about his motivations, but it does show that distinction and display were part of how he described the choice.

Rather than generalising from this example, we can treat it as a case where one type of motivation is visible. Emotional attachment to an animal may coexist with pleasure in its unusualness, a wish to attract admiration, or the means to own something rare. The sociological question is not “What kind of person is this?” but what social conditions allow an animal itself to become a status symbol.

From Roman arenas to the present: the social meaning of wild animals

In Roman history, venationes were public spectacles in which wild animals were displayed and hunted in arenas. The Metropolitan Museum of Art’s historical account notes that, in the late Roman Republic, animals such as lions, leopards, tigers, crocodiles and hippopotamuses were brought from Africa and other distant regions for shows. Their supply depended on a wide imperial network of hunters, captors, trainers, carriers and suppliers. An Oxford study of ancient sport and spectacle interprets exotic animal displays as making visible Rome’s geographic reach, its claim to power over nature, and the generosity of those who financed the games.

This history does not show that today’s household animal keeping directly descends from ancient Rome. The contexts are not the same: Rome’s practice was a political and public spectacle; today’s private ownership brings together care relationships, trade, social media and regulation. Yet history reminds us that societies have treated wild animals not only as living beings, but also as symbols of access to distant places, wealth, power and the ability to shape other people’s attention.

Private choice, shared life

Imagine learning that a neighbour in the apartment upstairs keeps a python. Rather than judging the neighbour, we can ask how private space connects with shared life. Appropriate conditions for the animal, continuity of care, the possibility of escape or injury, neighbours’ expectations about information and safety, and responsibility in an emergency all create shared concerns around an individual choice. This does not mean that everyone who keeps a python creates danger; species, conditions, knowledge of care and applicable rules need to be examined in each case.

The evolution of modern social order has brought more areas where personal choice cannot be explained simply by saying “it belongs to me.” Housing, apartment buildings, transport networks and digital visibility bring people closer together, while increasing the effects individual decisions may have on others and on the animal. Responsibility therefore involves more than labelling the behaviour: access to reliable information, suitable care conditions, workable rules and public institutions that respond when problems arise are also part of the relationship.

Questions we can examine together

In studying the sociology of unusual animal keeping, we will distinguish among different motivations rather than collapsing them: curiosity and admiration; companionship and emotional attachment; hobby and entertainment; perceived ease of care; distinction and status; and claims of rescue or conservation. We can ask which social settings strengthen each motive, how markets and social media may feed desire, and how visible the animal’s needs remain in these accounts.

The question is not how to belittle a person: Why might someone want to keep an animal beyond familiar household pets? What relationships, opportunities and meanings shape that desire? Where does private choice meet shared life and responsibility for care? As the inquiry develops, we will assess personal accounts, research findings, historical examples and current institutional arrangements as distinct kinds of evidence.
