Human Beings Within Stability: Education, Virtue and Economic Order

Adem Afşar · 3 November 2026
← Current Affairs · References

People want stability. They expect secure livelihoods, solvable problems and a foreseeable future. Yet unease and demands for change also emerge in societies thought to have achieved stability. Does this mean people tire of tranquillity? Or does an order seen as stable from outside produce different losses and anxieties within?

To answer this, we must first consider how well people understand the order in which they live. Being satisfied with a situation differs from knowing how it works; opposing a practice differs from understanding its consequences.

Experiencing Harm, Anticipating Danger

Farmers have concrete calculations for evaluating their work: What did I sow, how much did I harvest and at what price did I sell? Money received at the end of production offers a direct measure of their economic situation. Although correctly assessing every cause of a loss requires further knowledge, they can see the loss itself.

Institutional changes may lack this clarity. Understanding how an administrative reform or judicial intervention will affect future access to remedies requires evaluating consequences not yet experienced. Knowledge and foresight supplement direct experience here.

The emergence of a social response may therefore depend not only on harm's magnitude but also on its visibility and intelligibility. People sometimes immediately resist a loss they experience; sometimes they remain silent because they cannot recognise a danger not yet felt. Treating silence as approval and mobilisation as awareness is consequently misleading.

The Responsibility to Know

The knowledge I mean does not require everyone to specialise in every science. People should learn how their surroundings are governed, their own rights and responsibilities, others' rights and changes affecting them.

Which institution is responsible for which service? Who decides, implements and oversees? How can a practice be challenged? What does a changed rule mean in everyday life?

Learning these things forms part of respect for oneself and shared life. Ignorance not only makes claiming one's own rights harder; it may lead to blaming the wrong person, violating another's rights or supporting a practice that will also cause oneself harm.

Citizens' responsibility to learn cannot, however, be separated from institutions' responsibility to explain. Rules and decisions must be accessible, understandable and traceable. While expecting informed participation, we cannot ignore conditions obstructing access to information.

The Limits of Collective Consciousness

We form moral standards of being human within shared life. Yet these standards are not independent of time, geography and power relations. Conduct accepted in a society need not be beneficial or just for everyone.

The exhibition of Congolese people at Belgium's 1897 Tervuren exhibition and the 1958 Brussels World's Fair provides disturbing examples. Circles considering themselves civilised were able to normalise the humiliation of others. [1]

Collective consciousness therefore cannot be treated as a guarantee of rightness. When examining what is accepted, we must also ask how this acceptance formed, who had a voice and who was excluded.

Education therefore cannot merely teach prevailing standards; it should also enable their evaluation. While learning about the order in which they live, people should be able to question what that order does to human beings.

How Knowledge Changes People

Education means individuals need not relive every experience of preceding generations. Even when thinking independently, people may remain within the limits of personal experience. Learning from others' lives expands those limits.

But acquiring knowledge has a more difficult consequence: people reassess their past in light of what they learn. They may recognise that conduct previously considered justified was wrong, that they wronged someone or spoke too confidently about something they did not know.

Sincerity is decisive here. Knowledge can serve domination or self-criticism. What I expect from education is that people apply learned standards to themselves: that they can change their opinions, apologise when necessary and try to repair harm they caused.

This is what I mean by “Education makes us human”: knowledge finding expression in conduct and self-examination.

Facing Another's Powerlessness

One encounter testing these standards occurs when someone else needs us.

Years ago, while discussing refugees' arrival, I was deeply shaken when an acquaintance viewed their desperation as a sexual opportunity. This was one person's remark and could not be generalised to others. Yet the sorrow it left has not faded after many years.

During the same period, a young Syrian girl apologised before describing her needs. I told her that she was not the one who should apologise; we should feel ashamed that humanity had stood by while she endured those experiences. Afterwards, we helped arrange for her needs to be met.

These encounters make me consider a distinction: do we see another's vulnerability as a situation calling for responsibility or an opportunity to exploit?

For me, the humanity of the person before me is a value limiting my own conduct. Another's hardship does not increase my rights over them.

Between Respect for Labour and Consumer Pressure

The same standard matters in economic relationships. The ability to bargain does not justify exploiting another's necessity to their detriment.

I see bargaining's moral function as a process prompting both parties to reflect on the value of the product and labour. This is my proposed understanding of exchange, not a claim to explain a religious practice's definitive rationale. Indeed, the High Board of Religious Affairs considers bargaining permissible, while relevant hadiths praise leniency in buying and selling. [2], [3]

A buyer's responsibility does not end with payment. Protecting and maintaining a product and not needlessly exhausting its potential usefulness continue respect for labour. Acquiring it free does not mean producing it cost nothing.

Here, however, we encounter an economic tension. While users want lasting use, a business earning revenue through frequent replacement may have different aims. When premature deterioration is deliberately engineered, the concept of “planned obsolescence” applies; that every short-lived product was designed this way requires separate proof. [4]

Merely telling consumers to “take care” is therefore insufficient. Repair, spare parts, durability and sales models must also be evaluated. The existence of economic models based on extending product life shows that profit need not depend on constant replacement. [5]

My central question is: to what extent do economic incentives support the thrift and responsibility society expects from individuals?

Conditions for Sustaining Virtue

Virtuous behaviour cannot be explained through education alone. Livelihood, security and basic needs also affect conduct. However, Maslow's approach to needs should not be read as a rigid sequence in which deprivation necessarily eliminates morality. Maslow also notes that people may forgo basic needs for ideals. [6]

My distinction is this: worsening conditions may make acting in accordance with an adopted value difficult, but the person does not entirely revert to the state before learning that value. Knowing what is right, embracing it and being able to act accordingly can be distinguished.

We therefore cannot equate poverty with immorality or prosperity with virtue. Material security may reduce some pressures but does not automatically produce respect for others. Education may provide moral foundations but cannot guarantee their application under severe pressure.

Thinking about an ideal person and society requires considering both areas together. People are responsible for learning, questioning and accepting responsibility for their conduct. Social arrangements must also be assessed in terms of conditions enabling the behaviour they expect of people.

A society's moral character concerns not only how much virtue it expects, but how far it enables people to live virtuously.
