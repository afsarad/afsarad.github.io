Gender Perceptions Across Generations

Three generations living in the same family may have different expectations about women's and men's responsibilities. A grandmother may support her daughter's participation in employment while considering it natural that she should still do all the housework. A young person may advocate equal sharing of duties yet fail to make the contribution expected in daily life. These examples suggest that what we say and do, and our attitudes in different areas, do not always move in the same direction.

Birth years alone are insufficient to understand gender perceptions across generations. We need to consider together the circumstances in which people grew up, their access to opportunities, the responsibilities they undertook and the expectations placed on them. The approach I advocate here examines the transmission and transformation of social roles without using generational names as fixed labels defining people.

Examining expectations about roles

This article concerns the duties associated with women and men in family, education, employment and public life. Who is expected to perform a task, who should make a decision and which behavior is considered appropriate for whom are among the questions in debates on gender roles.

The European Institute for Gender Equality relates gender roles to culturally appropriate behaviors and responsibilities, highlighting their connection with conditions such as access to resources and household structure, and their capacity to change over time.[1] We should therefore distinguish between a familiar division of duties and one treated as compulsory and unchangeable for everyone.

One family member's responsibility for cooking or care does not itself demonstrate injustice. What matters is how the choice arose, how other responsibilities are shared, whether the contribution is recognized and whether arrangements can be reassessed when circumstances change. Apparently identical divisions may carry different meanings in different relationships.

Generation, age and historical period

When we observe differences between today's young and older people, we should consider three explanations. People occupy different life stages, grew up in different historical circumstances and experience the same period's events from different positions. Grouping these under a single generational characteristic may simplify explanation while erasing important distinctions.

Pew Research Center's methodological explanation states that a survey at a single point in time cannot definitively separate age and generational effects. Even longitudinal data require additional methodological assumptions because age, period and birth cohort are mathematically linked.[2] Finding a difference between young and older people at one time therefore does not prove that it results solely from generational membership.

Experiences such as marriage, childcare, unemployment or retirement may matter to how a person evaluates shared duties. Someone who has not yet assumed care responsibilities may answer differently from someone who has provided care for years. We need to ask about experienced responsibility as well as birth year.

Methodological caution learned from the Kütahya study

The research included in my thesis working text examined attitudes toward gender roles among 234 people reached in Kütahya in May and June 2023. The text states that convenience sampling was used, along with the Gender Roles Attitude Scale developed by Zeyneloğlu and Terzioğlu.[3][4]

The study's abstract reports different tendencies toward traditional and egalitarian attitudes among participant groups. However, the generational groups differ markedly in size and the sample is concentrated among younger groups. Presenting the findings as the views of everyone in Kütahya or all generations in Türkiye would therefore be inappropriate. A few people's responses in a small group cannot define an entire generation.

What I find important in this study is investigating the experiences informing responses rather than placing people in a generational ranking. Education, employment, marital status and family responsibilities are dimensions that should be considered separately. An answer to a survey is also not a direct record of all a person's everyday behavior.

Roles learned and reconstructed within the family

Children learn the distribution of family duties from more than spoken words. Who interrupts their work to meet a need, whose rest is considered natural and whose contribution is visible can also be part of the learning environment. When behavior is repeated frequently, people may forget that it is a choice open to discussion.

However, treating the family as a one-way mechanism of transmission is also incomplete. As children grow, they bring new experiences home; parents may reorganize duties as working and living conditions change. More than rules pass between generations: their reasons are questioned, some are retained and others changed.

We should therefore avoid judging every past division of duties as though today's opportunities had existed. Understanding past conditions does not require preserving present inequalities. The issue is to examine which need produced an arrangement and whether it remains justifiable today.

Changing educational and employment opportunities

Indicators of education and employment in Türkiye provide relevant context for discussing social roles. A joint publication by TÜİK and UN Women gives the proportion of higher-education graduates among people aged 25 and over in 2024 as 23.6% for women and 26.8% for men.[5] These data provide concrete information about educational opportunities; they do not themselves measure attitudes toward gender roles.

Relationships between educational attainment and attitudes can be studied. Nevertheless, possession of a diploma does not establish egalitarian behavior in every relationship. Nor should the experience and reasoning about shared duties of people with limited educational opportunities be dismissed. Understanding behavior requires avoiding reduction to a single indicator.

Participation in employment does not guarantee that family responsibilities will automatically be redistributed. Paid working hours, care needs and domestic labor must be considered together. Recognizing both income-generating labor and contributions without direct payment can help us understand how shared life is maintained.

Stated principles and everyday practice

People may say that women and men should have equal opportunities. Yet their family decisions, habits or working arrangements may not fully align with that principle. The reasons should be investigated. Someone may not be practicing their principle, but their means, working hours or access to care services may also constrain its implementation.

Attitude questions alone are therefore insufficient. We can also investigate who performs particular tasks, how decisions are made and how arrangements can change. For example, two families claiming that duties are fairly distributed may define fairness differently: one may emphasize equal time, another existing capacity and needs.

I propose avoiding an immediate judgment of character based on a discrepancy between words and behavior. We should first understand the discrepancy and the circumstances producing it. Preserving the need for explanation does not prevent criticism where necessary; it clarifies its grounds.

Differences within the same generation

Being born in the same years does not mean growing up in the same family, receiving the same education or bearing the same responsibilities. Rural and urban experiences, income, occupation and social circles vary within a generation. A generational average cannot reveal all these differences.

Treating all young people as egalitarian and all older people as traditional is therefore not an explanatory conclusion. Endorsing a traditional division of duties in one area does not establish the same attitude on every issue. Asking separately about education, employment, family decision-making and care responsibilities may reveal a different picture.

The concept of generation is useful because it opens questions about shared historical experiences. Its limitation arises when this commonality is assumed to eliminate individual differences. Research should explain differences as well as similarities.

Must difference turn into conflict?

The division of duties embraced by one generation may seem incomprehensible to another. But responding to disagreement only with labels such as disrespect or backwardness obscures the experience that needs discussion. The conditions in which a practice was learned and its consequences today can be examined together.

The question I find useful in intergenerational discussion is not who is better, but how a particular expectation arose and whose life it now affects, and how. Concrete circumstances such as changed working hours or a relative needing care may require reconsideration of previously unquestioned arrangements.

Equality should not mean everyone doing the same task at every moment. Fundamental criteria may include not restricting rights, opportunities and participation in decisions in advance because of gender, and justifying duties according to need and capacity. This is the article's normative assessment, not a predetermined judgment of how every family functions.

Looking beyond labels to relationships

Understanding gender perceptions across generations is more demanding than praising one age group and blaming another. It requires examining historical conditions, life stages, opportunities and everyday responsibilities together. Local research findings acquire meaning within these broader questions while retaining their sampling limits.

For me, it is important to look for social change in how people relate to each other rather than the names they give each other. Who can speak, whose labor is recognized, which expectations can be questioned and how burdens are shared are concrete questions of everyday life. Understanding generational differences offers an opportunity to reassess shared life through these questions.
