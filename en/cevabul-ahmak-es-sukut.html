Faith, understanding and responses

When the maxim of answering foolishness with silence may be set aside

Adem Afşar

Why am I breaking my silence?

I do not consider it necessary to answer every claim or prolong every argument. Yet I do not accept my silence being presented as ignorance, agreement or an inability to respond. On this page I am breaking my silence to examine claims about the Qur’an, Islam and the Prophet Muhammad, and to refute those based on misinformation, incomplete accounts or faulty reasoning by showing why they fail.

In the Twenty-Ninth Letter of Mektubat, Bediüzzaman Said Nursi invokes the maxim about answering foolishness with silence, then proceeds to answer, explaining that there are unfortunate intelligent people behind some of those he calls foolish. This is a paraphrase of his transition. The passage shows that his use of the maxim does not exclude explanation in every circumstance. My reason concerning the misinterpretation of my silence is my own assessment. [1]

Here I will address arguments rather than name individuals or channels. I do not apply the description in the title to everyone who asks a question. My response addresses the person reading a claim and seeking to understand it.

Does later compilation mean unreliability?

In response to the person who draws this conclusion…

You move from the Qur’an’s compilation into a book after the Prophet Muhammad’s death to the conclusion that it consists of unreliable statements created afterwards. Before making that move, we need to distinguish writing down, memorising, gathering material together and making copies. Asking when a text was compiled is different from asking when its contents first appeared.

What does the source tell us?

Bukhari’s report numbered 4986 describes the task of compilation under Abu Bakr and states that Zayd ibn Thabit had written revelation for the Prophet Muhammad. It recounts the use of written materials and people’s memorisation in gathering the text. This is the information conveyed by that report; its historical value can be examined separately. It would not, however, be accurate to present this account as a description of statements being invented for the first time during that task. [2]

Manuscripts can be investigated separately from transmitted reports. The manuscript at the University of Birmingham contains portions of Surahs al-Kahf, Maryam and Ta-Ha. Its parchment was dated to 568–645 CE with a probability of 95.4%. This measurement concerns the parchment’s age; it does not, by itself, establish the exact year the ink was applied or the date of the Qur’an’s entire text. [3]

My thinking: Words kept within everyday life

I begin with faith that the Qur’an is God’s word. When investigating its transmission, I also want to consider its place in the lives of its first recipients. We cannot assess words heard once by a single person and recalled years later under the same conditions as an address that people repeatedly read, learned and discussed.

My hypothesis is that those who embraced the Qur’an returned to its verses through meaning, commitment and the satisfaction they wished to experience again, while opponents examined the same words to formulate objections and weaken their influence. At the heart of my idea of a “single agenda” lies the sustained engagement produced by these opposing orientations. I am not saying that eating, working or family life disappeared; I am referring to shared words repeatedly entering different areas of life.

Words can remain in circulation through opponents’ objections as well as supporters’ repetition. The memories of younger people, adults and elders may overlap and interact. Different interpretations of the same wording do not automatically show that people did not know that wording or that the text changed. Argument can itself be a form of repetition.

This is my proposed explanation concerning social memory; I do not present it as a research finding demonstrating that everyone at the time was equally engaged. How remembering and mutual checking operated in transmission must be tested against historical sources. Accounts of Qur’anic memorisation discuss oral learning, memorisation and reference to written texts together. [4]

What can we conclude?

The date of compilation matters in historical investigation. I do not dismiss the interval involved, chains of transmission or relationships between manuscripts. My point is that later compilation does not by itself mean later invention. Conversely, showing the possibility of strong social memory does not by itself prove that every detail was preserved without error.

I ask you to state the claim through concrete reasons: let us show which expression changed and when, and which record supports or weakens which account of transmission. Without equating gathering material together with creating it, let us speak within what the evidence establishes. My answer here addresses a particular inference: “It was compiled later” does not, by itself, entail “It is unreliable.”

If human beings are limited, can they know nothing about God?

In response to the person who draws this conclusion…

You say that human reason, senses and experience are limited. From this you conclude that human beings can have no knowledge of God, who is understood to be unlimited. You even argue that a God whom human reason could comprehend would no longer count as unlimited. To examine this reasoning, we must first clarify the relationship between “knowing” and “comprehending completely”.

Does knowing something exhaust it?

We may possess some true knowledge about a person without knowing all their memories, thoughts or every situation they might experience. The incompleteness of our knowledge does not make everything we know false. This example is not intended to place God in the same category of being as a human. It illustrates a distinction: acquiring knowledge about a subject does not require encompassing every aspect of it.

Consequently, “Human beings are limited” and “Human beings can have no true knowledge about what is unlimited” are different propositions. To reach the second conclusion, you also need to show that all true knowledge requires comprehending the known being in its entirety. Without providing that additional reason, you turn human limitation into absolute ignorance.

Nor does something come under the knower’s power or authority simply because it is known. The conception formed in a human mind is limited. The limitations of that conception do not establish that the being under consideration must necessarily fall within those same limits.

The question I ask from my faith

I believe that God possesses absolute power. Starting from that belief, I ask: if I accept that God can create human beings, why should I rule out from the outset His ability to address those He created in a way they can understand? An address suited to its recipient’s capacity does not mean that the speaker’s entire being fits within that capacity.

This question does not, by itself, prove that any particular text is revelation. A specific text’s claim to divine origin must be examined separately. My objection here concerns your use of human limitation to rule out the intelligibility of divine address from the outset. In my faith, revelation means God addressing human beings in a way they can understand, rather than human beings encompassing God in His entirety.

The effort to understand and human authority

I apply the same standard to people who speak in the name of religion. My inability to understand a verse today does not show that no human being can understand it. Someone else’s greater knowledge does not make all their interpretations unquestionable either. I can learn from a teacher while also asking about their reasons, sources and the limits of their interpretation.

“I have not understood” describes a person’s own situation. “Nobody can understand” is a much larger claim about all people and all times. That claim requires its own justification. Neither belief nor objection releases a person from this responsibility.

Where my answer leads

Human limitation requires us to respect the extent of what we know. It does not, however, automatically establish that every thought about God is meaningless or that all knowledge is impossible. We should examine each claim through its own reasons. Being unable to encompass God completely and being unable to know anything about Him are different conclusions. My answer here addresses the inference that equates the two.

Do different interpretations of the same verse show that the Qur’an is contradictory?

In response to the person who draws this conclusion…

The objection I examine is this: if people reading the same verse reach different conclusions, that shows the text is contradictory or its meaning cannot be determined. In assessing this objection, we should first ask where the disagreement lies. Two people offering mutually exclusive interpretations and a text issuing mutually exclusive judgements on the same subject under the same conditions are separate matters.

What does disagreement between interpreters tell us?

Consider two readers asking different questions of a text. One asks under what conditions an action is permitted; the other asks whether that action is required in every situation. Even when they discuss the same sentence, they are seeking answers to different questions. Confusing permission with obligation, or the description of an event with a requirement to follow its example, can produce an opposition absent from the text.

In another case, two readers may indeed give opposite answers to the same question. We cannot then declare both correct. We need to examine which meaning is better supported by the words, the sentence’s construction and the text as a whole. Yet an error in one interpretation does not necessarily mean that the error belongs to the text itself.

The analogy I draw with legal interpretation

Consider this example: two legal scholars interpret the same statutory provision differently, and their disagreement is referred to a competent authority for assessment. The existence of these interpretations would not establish that the statute necessarily contradicts itself. The text’s clarity, the concepts it uses and whether the specific case falls under that provision would require separate discussion.

I do not draw this analogy to equate the Qur’an with a human law. My concern is the human activity of understanding a text. Reading a statement together does not ensure that everyone reads it with the same knowledge, questions and expectations. A group may reach a shared interpretation, but its agreement alone does not prove that interpretation correct either. The reasoning still requires examination.

How can we think about addressing past and future?

I believe that the Qur’an addresses past and future. Within that faith, I accept that an address concerning a problem experienced by its first recipients can also help a later reader think about another problem. A new situation may reveal a question we had not previously asked. The text can remain unchanged while the reader recognises a wider relationship.

For example, considering how to apply a principle requiring justice within a family and considering how to apply it in working life involve different questions. This is not offered as an explanation of a particular verse. It illustrates how one principle can generate different questions of application under different conditions. A change in application does not necessarily mean that the principle has been reversed.

I therefore see the possibility of discovering new connections of meaning at different times as consistent with the continuing relevance of the Qur’an’s address. However, I do not use the number of interpretations as proof of divine origin. An interpretation’s relationship to the text must be demonstrated. My belief that the Qur’an addresses the future does not authorise me to present every thought that occurs to me as a verse’s meaning.

How will I examine a claim of contradiction?

I will first set out the two judgements being placed in opposition. Are they statements in the text, translation choices or conclusions drawn by an interpreter? Do they concern the same subject, the same conditions and the same level of meaning? If there is a real opposition, we cannot dismiss it simply as “a difference of interpretation”. We should explain our reasons for resolving it, or acknowledge that we have not yet resolved it.

This is the distinction on which my answer rests: Disagreement among interpreters does not, by itself, show that a text contradicts itself. Disagreement may be the starting point of an investigation. To reach a conclusion, we must show which meaning is derived from which statement and for what reasons.

Does making something understandable distort the truth?

In response to the person who draws this conclusion…

I examine an objection encountered in an essay dated 30 January 2017: presenting a divine communication at a level human beings can understand, using familiar comparisons, is itself treated as distortion. I disagree with that conclusion. We need to distinguish constructing an account in a form its recipient can understand from corrupting the meaning it conveys.

A selective account is not necessarily a false account

Consider a teacher explaining a subject through concepts familiar to a student. The teacher does not say everything they know about the subject at once. They may leave some details for later or illustrate a relationship through an example. Such choices may leave an account incomplete, but not every omission makes the information given false.

Simplification can, of course, introduce an error. An example may misrepresent the relationship being explained. In that case, we establish the error by explaining what the example distorts and how. The mere use of an example does not establish that it is wrong. We should maintain the same standard when examining a religious text.

Does the Qur’an regard its recipients’ language as a problem?

Verse 4 of Surah Ibrahim connects sending messengers in their communities’ own language with the purpose of making an explanation understood. This part of the verse does not present using the recipients’ language as a defect in the communication. I infer from it that intelligibility serves the purpose of the address. That last sentence is my assessment. [5]

I believe that the Qur’an is God’s word. Within that belief, I do not regard God addressing human beings in language they can understand as a deficiency in His power. A human being may be unable to encompass all truth while still understanding something communicated to them. Communicating a particular thing to a recipient does not require communicating everything in all its details.

Does a shared word imply the same kind of being?

Verse 11 of Surah ash-Shura states that God has no likeness while also describing His hearing and seeing. I consider these statements together. The verse’s use of words familiar to human beings cannot simply be equated with saying that God possesses the same organs or biological processes as humans. How that equation is derived from the text must be demonstrated separately. [6]

If we take a word’s use in relation to human beings and impose the same limitations on every use in relation to God, we determine the meaning before beginning our investigation. Conversely, inventing whatever metaphor we wish whenever a difficulty arises is no solution either. We should justify our understanding of an expression through its words, context and the text’s other relevant explanations.

How will I assess a comparison?

When someone argues that an account contains a comparison, I will first ask which aspect is being compared. Establishing a particular relationship between two things does not mean treating all their properties as identical. Descriptions of paradise and hell also require this care. Which expressions convey direct statements, comparisons or other forms of expression must be considered separately through the relevant verses.

This answer does not claim to prove the truth of all religious accounts at once. The inference I examine is more specific: A statement’s being expressed in language human beings can understand does not establish that it necessarily distorts the truth. To defend a claim of distortion, you must go beyond the existence of intelligible expression and show where and how the meaning has been corrupted.

Was there silence about the killing of children for eighteen years?

In response to the person who draws this conclusion…

An essay dated 5 August 2017 argues that, because the condition against killing children in Surah al-Mumtahanah was communicated late, God and the Prophet Muhammad remained silent about this wrong for eighteen years. My answer is clear: no such period of silence can be calculated without considering the earlier condemnations and prohibitions. I will first examine the relevant verses, then distinguish condemnation from practical prevention. Without judging the claimant’s inner intentions, I will hold the conclusion they draw to account.

Does inclusion in a later verse mean it was first stated then?

Verses 8–9 of Surah at-Takwir describe the girl buried alive being asked for what offence she was killed. In the traditional ordering presented by Diyanet, the surah belongs to the Meccan period and is seventh in the order of revelation. I do not treat that ordering as a calendar giving the precise day of every verse; nevertheless, within the source framework concerned, we find an early condemnation. [7]

Verse 31 of Surah al-Isra also explicitly prohibits killing children for fear of poverty and describes it as a grave sin. Diyanet’s introduction to the surah places this verse in the Meccan period. This is more than a general appeal for compassion: it is an explicit prohibition of killing children. [8]

Al-Mumtahanah 60:12 concerns women pledging allegiance on conditions against associating partners with God, theft, adultery and killing their children. The Kur’an Yolu commentary also recounts similar commitments in the First Pledge of Aqabah and records differing reports concerning the relationship between the verse’s revelation and later practices. We cannot read such a pledge as establishing the date when every prohibition it contains was first communicated. [9]

We should also check the eighteen-year calculation

The essay containing the objection equates the eighth year after the Hijrah with the eighteenth year of prophethood. The chronology in the TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi places the first revelation in 610 and the Hijrah in 622. That equation is inconsistent with this chronology. The main answer, however, is not to replace one number with another: a period of silence cannot be calculated without considering the earlier verses. [10]

The persistence of a wrong does not show it was permitted

The social distinction I emphasise is this: declaring a behaviour wrong does not mean that everyone practising it stops simultaneously. We should investigate the fears, understandings of livelihood, social pressures and habits that could lead a family to kill its child. Investigating these causes does not justify the act; it helps us understand how it might be prevented.

Today, passing a law does not end every violation. Adopting an educational approach does not ensure that it becomes behaviour in every family at the same time. I offer these examples to explain the difference between announcing a rule and implementing it within a society, rather than to prove the details of the past.

Which period’s capacity to intervene are we assessing?

A person’s rejection of an act and their authority to stop it everywhere by force are separate matters. We should ask at what date, in which community and in which incident intervention was possible. We cannot assume that the institutions and reach of a present-day state already existed in the past.

I therefore do not assess the Meccan and Medinan periods as having identical administrative conditions. The Prophet Muhammad’s governing and adjudicating authority in Medina must also be considered. TDV’s account discusses the Medinan agreement’s referral of disputes to him and his position in government. Reducing the entire period to a single explanation of “lack of authority” would also be mistaken. My question concerns actual authority and feasible intervention in each specific case. [10]

Testing the impression created by an account against evidence

Used without establishing the frequency and extent of events, the expression “watched for eighteen years” can suggest a deliberately maintained system of killing, repeatedly taking place in full public view. That impression is not a historical record. We must separately investigate how many incidents occurred in which communities, who knew about them and what they could do. We can neither attribute the same conduct to every family nor claim without specific evidence that intervention occurred in every case.

I believe that God’s word addresses human responsibility. I do not understand the communication of a command as an event that removes human agency and automatically changes all behaviour. This is my assessment within my faith. My textual response to the historical claim of silence concerns the existence of early condemnation and prohibition.

The abortion example: Applying the living–non-living distinction to the present

My present-day example here is abortion. When assessing the past, I also ask the same question about protecting life in my own time. A living embryo or fetus developing in the womb is biologically a living human organism. Being unborn does not make it non-living. The biological account of fertilization and embryonic development should be distinguished from assessments of the moral and legal status of that life. [11]

When an induced abortion ends the life of a living embryo or fetus, prenatal human life ends. This is the biological fact I emphasise. Establishing that something is alive does not, by itself, settle the circumstances in which intervention may be permissible; the woman’s life and health, the stage of pregnancy and the specific circumstances also enter the assessment.

The abortion example I give here concerns an intervention that ends the life of a living embryo or fetus. Medical care needed after miscarriage or death in the womb is a different situation. The World Health Organization likewise discusses induced abortion, care related to pregnancy loss and care following intrauterine fetal death as distinct situations within abortion care. [12]

My moral question is this: what standards do we use today to protect prenatal human life? An intervention’s legal status does not make moral assessment unnecessary. This comparison does not justify a killing in the past; it questions the assumption that our own era has resolved every issue concerning the protection of life. The truth of the claim of “eighteen years of silence” must still be determined through the relevant verses and historical sources.

Holding a claim to account without judging intentions

The Turkish expression “Eating the grapes or beating the grower?” asks whether a discussion aims at understanding or at discrediting its target. Yet I cannot claim certain knowledge of someone’s intentions on the basis of a single mistaken inference. What I can assess here is the information they use, the relevant statements they leave out and the conclusion they draw.

The omission in this case is not incidental. A judgement of years of silence is constructed from al-Mumtahanah 60:12 without taking at-Takwir 8–9 and al-Isra 17:31 into account. Reading these verses together makes the proposition that “there was no opposition” untenable. When and to what extent social practices changed remains a separate question for investigation. [7–9]

A discussion directed towards finding the truth must also consider counterevidence. When an omission is demonstrated and the claim is corrected, inquiry advances. If the same judgement is maintained without answering the counterevidence, the problem extends beyond the original lack of information: the method of discussion also comes into question. I accept this standard for my own answer too.

This is therefore what I ask of you: I do not claim to know your intentions. But your conclusion must be defended in light of the verses you did not take into account. I expect you to reassess your claim alongside these verses. An answer consists in demonstrating a claim’s grounds or correcting its error, rather than belittling a person.

The persistence of a wrong does not show that nobody opposed it. In this case, early condemnation and explicit prohibition leave the inference of “eighteen years of silence” without a basis. Who knew what and what they could do in particular incidents must be investigated separately. Understanding the difficulty of social change requires us both to defend a child’s right to life and to test our own claims against counterevidence.

Does similarity to earlier texts show that the Qur’an was copied?

In response to the person who draws this conclusion…

An essay dated 5 August 2017 argues that the Prophet Muhammad altered previously known Jewish narratives to present a new religion. We should distinguish three separate judgements: two accounts resemble one another; one was transmitted from the other; and that transmission was knowingly presented as divine communication. Establishing the first does not automatically prove the other two.

Let us show precisely what the similarity is

In a comparison, I first want to see which passages of which texts are being compared. Is the common element a general subject such as justice or responsibility? Is it the presence of the same figures? Or is there a more specific correspondence between the sequence of events, distinctive details and expressions? We cannot give all these observations the same evidential weight.

We should then investigate the date of the source being compared, the form of the relevant account at that time and possible routes of transmission. Oral transmission belongs within this investigation too; evidence for every relationship need not take the form of a surviving manuscript. Nevertheless, showing that an account could have been known in an environment differs from demonstrating how a particular person learned it. We must distinguish a possibility supported by evidence from a conclusion it establishes with certainty.

Does the Qur’an conceal its relationship with earlier revelations?

Al Imran 3:3–4 describes the Qur’an as having been revealed to confirm earlier scriptures and refers to the Torah and Gospel as earlier divine communications. Al-Ma’idah 5:48 describes the Qur’an’s relationship with previous scriptures in terms of confirmation and oversight. The Qur’an therefore does not present itself as an entirely disconnected address with no antecedents. [13–14]

I believe that the revelation sent to earlier prophets also came from God. Within that belief, shared subjects and accounts are unsurprising. The Qur’an’s relationship with earlier divine addresses is not, in my faith, a defect to conceal. I do not, however, treat that belief alone as historical evidence for someone who begins from different assumptions.

Returning to a subject does not remove its value

The fact that an idea has been expressed before does not remove its truth or its importance to new recipients. The value of recalling justice does not depend on being the first to mention it. Similarly, when assessing a narrative, I consider which behaviour it questions and which responsibility it places upon its reader, as well as the figures it describes.

This approach does not make investigating relationships between texts unnecessary. Two texts describing the same event may have different emphases and conclusions; these differences also need to be demonstrated through specific comparison. Calling an account “a myth” or “revelation” at the outset cannot replace that comparison.

I distinguish a claim of transmission from an accusation of deception

Research may strongly support a historical connection between a particular account and another tradition. Such a finding deserves serious consideration. Demonstrating a connection, however, differs from demonstrating that a person knowingly fabricated something and attributed it to God. An accusation of deliberate deception requires additional reasons concerning intention and conduct.

Nor can a believer dismiss the issue by saying “The earlier source was corrupted” without examining the details. We should explain which text, in which historically situated form, is being compared with which verse. Explaining our own assumptions and offering a reason open to shared investigation must remain distinct.

The scope and conclusion of my answer

I do not claim here to have resolved the entire transmission history of a particular narrative. I do not disregard similarity; I ask which conclusions are drawn from it and for what reasons. Similarity to earlier texts does not, by itself, prove that something was knowingly copied and presented as revelation. Such an accusation must be defended through specific textual comparisons and historical reasons. I accept the same standard of care for my own answer.

Abu Jahl or al-Walid ibn al-Mughirah? Why does the person addressed matter?

In response to the person who draws this conclusion…

An essay dated 5 August 2017 constructs an account in which the Prophet Muhammad sought to win Abu Jahl over but began denouncing him when he did not accept the religion. After Surah al-Alaq, al-Muddaththir 11–29 is added to the account, and the inferences continue as though the same person were being discussed. We should first check this connection: do the historical explanations of these verses actually identify the same person?

The explanation of Surah al-Alaq

The Kur’an Yolu commentary recounts reports connecting the later part of Surah al-Alaq with Abu Jahl. Its explanations address conduct intended to prevent the Prophet Muhammad from worshipping. The revelation of the opening passage is also distinguished from that of the later part. Consequently, describing a surah as the first to be revealed does not establish that all its verses were revealed together on the same occasion. [15]

We encounter a different person in Surah al-Muddaththir

The same commentary explains al-Muddaththir 11–17 as verses reported to have been revealed concerning al-Walid ibn al-Mughirah. Its explanation of 18–30 likewise recounts a report of al-Walid being consulted about what discourse to develop against the Qur’an. The TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi entry “Velîd b. Mugīre” also connects verses 11–26 with him. [16–17]

I maintain an important distinction here: the people’s names are not written in the text of these verses. Identifying them depends on commentary and historical reports. I therefore do not say that they are named in the verse; I state whom the explanatory sources we are using identify.

Combining two people changes the inference

Adding information about one person to verses associated with another can create a continuous account of events in a single person’s life. Yet our own narration, rather than the source, then supplies the connection. To conclude that someone was first courted and that these statements were subsequently made about that same person after rejection, we need to show that the people and events really correspond.

Treating two people distinguished by the sources as one cannot replace that justification. If such a connection is defended, an alternative report or other grounds should be stated explicitly. Belonging to similar circles or opposing the same religion does not justify transferring every statement and event to a single person.

Does inviting someone to believe endorse their previous behaviour?

Another step in the argument also requires examination: inviting a person to adopt a belief or change their conduct does not show that all their previous behaviour was considered good. An invitation can also seek their abandonment of conduct regarded as wrong. Consequently, “He was someone they sought to win over” does not, by itself, establish “He was previously considered wholly good”.

This distinction does not prove every detail of a particular historical event. It does, however, identify where the reasoning needs additional justification. We should establish the person, then the event, and then the context of the statement.

Where my answer leads

Identifying this confusion of sources does not resolve every religious debate. The inference concerned does, however, require reassessment. We cannot complete a claim about one person with verses associated with another and present the result as a single life story. Criticism is tested by the accuracy of its connections as well as the force of its language. The same standard applies to my answer.

Why I am opening this section

In this section I want to examine claims made on social media about the Qur’an, Islam and the Prophet Muhammad. I will focus particularly on arguments that appear contradictory at first sight, leave readers with unanswered questions or lead to misleading conclusions through incomplete accounts. By presenting my own thinking and its reasons, I hope to help readers reconsider these matters.

I use the expression in the title as a maxim here; I do not present it as a Qur’anic verse or a hadith. I do not use the word “foolish” to describe everyone who asks a question. To understand an objection, we first need to examine what the person says and the reasons they offer.

Silence towards whom and with what outcome

Sometimes ending an argument can prevent insults and anger from escalating. On social media, however, the person posting a statement is not its only reader. Others may see it and feel confused because they do not know how to answer. This is the distinction I want to explore: we can stop an unproductive exchange with one person while continuing to explain the claim to those who read it.

For this reason, I do not think we must join the argument beneath every post. We can describe the claim clearly on our own page and examine its foundations. A response can also address readers seeking to understand the issue. In deciding whether to respond, I will ask what our explanation can clarify.

When I consider an explanation necessary

I consider an explanation worthwhile when part of a verse is quoted while its continuation is withheld; a translation choice is presented as the text’s only possible meaning; a statement whose source cannot be found is attributed to the Prophet; or an individual’s conduct is used to draw conclusions about the entire religion. These are types of claim we will examine, rather than advance judgements about a particular post.

If we encounter a difficulty that genuinely needs investigation, I will state it plainly too. To establish that a claim is invalid, we need to show what is wrong with its premises, information or conclusion. I also want to distinguish explaining my own faith from offering shared evidence to someone who begins from different assumptions.

How we will examine each claim

First, we will convey the claim without changing its meaning and record the post’s source and date. We will then identify the verse, transmitted report, historical information or reasoning on which it relies. After examining the full text and context, we will ask whether the claim’s conclusion actually follows from that information.

My own thinking will appear under a separate heading. The Qur’anic text, hadith reports, explanations in Risale-i Nur and my own assessment will remain distinguishable. When drawing on Risale-i Nur, we will identify the work and relevant section. We will not present wording we cannot verify as the source’s own statement.

Responsibility in responding

A misleading claim does not mean we know with certainty the intentions of the person sharing it. We will not build our examination around judging a person’s worth or inner life. Humiliating someone who asks a sincere question undermines the purpose of offering an explanation.

My aim in this section is to help people with questions about their faith see the issue more clearly. We will look for the strength of our answers in their reasoning. Correcting an error in my own assessment is part of the same responsibility.
