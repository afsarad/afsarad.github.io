Faith, understanding and responses

When the maxim of answering foolishness with silence may be set aside

Adem Afşar

Why am I breaking my silence?

I do not consider it necessary to answer every claim or prolong every argument. Yet I do not accept my silence being presented as ignorance, agreement or an inability to respond. On this page I am breaking my silence to examine claims about the Qur’an, Islam and the Prophet Muhammad, and to refute those based on misinformation, incomplete accounts or faulty reasoning by showing why they fail.

In the Twenty-Ninth Letter of Mektubat, Bediüzzaman Said Nursi invokes the maxim about answering foolishness with silence, then proceeds to answer, explaining that there are unfortunate intelligent people behind some of those he calls foolish. This is a paraphrase of his transition. The passage shows that his use of the maxim does not exclude explanation in every circumstance. My reason concerning the misinterpretation of my silence is my own assessment. [1]

Here I will address arguments rather than name individuals or channels. I do not apply the description in the title to everyone who asks a question. My response addresses the person reading a claim and seeking to understand it.

Does later compilation mean unreliability?

In response to the person who draws this conclusion…

You move from the Qur’an’s compilation into a book after the Prophet Muhammad’s death to the conclusion that it consists of unreliable statements created afterwards. Before making that move, we need to distinguish writing down, memorising, gathering material together and making copies. Asking when a text was compiled is different from asking when its contents first appeared.

What does the source tell us?

Bukhari’s report numbered 4986 describes the task of compilation under Abu Bakr and states that Zayd ibn Thabit had written revelation for the Prophet Muhammad. It recounts the use of written materials and people’s memorisation in gathering the text. This is the information conveyed by that report; its historical value can be examined separately. It would not, however, be accurate to present this account as a description of statements being invented for the first time during that task. [2]

Manuscripts can be investigated separately from transmitted reports. The manuscript at the University of Birmingham contains portions of Surahs al-Kahf, Maryam and Ta-Ha. Its parchment was dated to 568–645 CE with a probability of 95.4%. This measurement concerns the parchment’s age; it does not, by itself, establish the exact year the ink was applied or the date of the Qur’an’s entire text. [3]

My thinking: Words kept within everyday life

I begin with faith that the Qur’an is God’s word. When investigating its transmission, I also want to consider its place in the lives of its first recipients. We cannot assess words heard once by a single person and recalled years later under the same conditions as an address that people repeatedly read, learned and discussed.

My hypothesis is that those who embraced the Qur’an returned to its verses through meaning, commitment and the satisfaction they wished to experience again, while opponents examined the same words to formulate objections and weaken their influence. At the heart of my idea of a “single agenda” lies the sustained engagement produced by these opposing orientations. I am not saying that eating, working or family life disappeared; I am referring to shared words repeatedly entering different areas of life.

Words can remain in circulation through opponents’ objections as well as supporters’ repetition. The memories of younger people, adults and elders may overlap and interact. Different interpretations of the same wording do not automatically show that people did not know that wording or that the text changed. Argument can itself be a form of repetition.

This is my proposed explanation concerning social memory; I do not present it as a research finding demonstrating that everyone at the time was equally engaged. How remembering and mutual checking operated in transmission must be tested against historical sources. Accounts of Qur’anic memorisation discuss oral learning, memorisation and reference to written texts together. [4]

What can we conclude?

The date of compilation matters in historical investigation. I do not dismiss the interval involved, chains of transmission or relationships between manuscripts. My point is that later compilation does not by itself mean later invention. Conversely, showing the possibility of strong social memory does not by itself prove that every detail was preserved without error.

I ask you to state the claim through concrete reasons: let us show which expression changed and when, and which record supports or weakens which account of transmission. Without equating gathering material together with creating it, let us speak within what the evidence establishes. My answer here addresses a particular inference: “It was compiled later” does not, by itself, entail “It is unreliable.”

If human beings are limited, can they know nothing about God?

In response to the person who draws this conclusion…

You say that human reason, senses and experience are limited. From this you conclude that human beings can have no knowledge of God, who is understood to be unlimited. You even argue that a God whom human reason could comprehend would no longer count as unlimited. To examine this reasoning, we must first clarify the relationship between “knowing” and “comprehending completely”.

Does knowing something exhaust it?

We may possess some true knowledge about a person without knowing all their memories, thoughts or every situation they might experience. The incompleteness of our knowledge does not make everything we know false. This example is not intended to place God in the same category of being as a human. It illustrates a distinction: acquiring knowledge about a subject does not require encompassing every aspect of it.

Consequently, “Human beings are limited” and “Human beings can have no true knowledge about what is unlimited” are different propositions. To reach the second conclusion, you also need to show that all true knowledge requires comprehending the known being in its entirety. Without providing that additional reason, you turn human limitation into absolute ignorance.

Nor does something come under the knower’s power or authority simply because it is known. The conception formed in a human mind is limited. The limitations of that conception do not establish that the being under consideration must necessarily fall within those same limits.

The question I ask from my faith

I believe that God possesses absolute power. Starting from that belief, I ask: if I accept that God can create human beings, why should I rule out from the outset His ability to address those He created in a way they can understand? An address suited to its recipient’s capacity does not mean that the speaker’s entire being fits within that capacity.

This question does not, by itself, prove that any particular text is revelation. A specific text’s claim to divine origin must be examined separately. My objection here concerns your use of human limitation to rule out the intelligibility of divine address from the outset. In my faith, revelation means God addressing human beings in a way they can understand, rather than human beings encompassing God in His entirety.

The effort to understand and human authority

I apply the same standard to people who speak in the name of religion. My inability to understand a verse today does not show that no human being can understand it. Someone else’s greater knowledge does not make all their interpretations unquestionable either. I can learn from a teacher while also asking about their reasons, sources and the limits of their interpretation.

“I have not understood” describes a person’s own situation. “Nobody can understand” is a much larger claim about all people and all times. That claim requires its own justification. Neither belief nor objection releases a person from this responsibility.

Where my answer leads

Human limitation requires us to respect the extent of what we know. It does not, however, automatically establish that every thought about God is meaningless or that all knowledge is impossible. We should examine each claim through its own reasons. Being unable to encompass God completely and being unable to know anything about Him are different conclusions. My answer here addresses the inference that equates the two.

Why I am opening this section

In this section I want to examine claims made on social media about the Qur’an, Islam and the Prophet Muhammad. I will focus particularly on arguments that appear contradictory at first sight, leave readers with unanswered questions or lead to misleading conclusions through incomplete accounts. By presenting my own thinking and its reasons, I hope to help readers reconsider these matters.

I use the expression in the title as a maxim here; I do not present it as a Qur’anic verse or a hadith. I do not use the word “foolish” to describe everyone who asks a question. To understand an objection, we first need to examine what the person says and the reasons they offer.

Silence towards whom and with what outcome

Sometimes ending an argument can prevent insults and anger from escalating. On social media, however, the person posting a statement is not its only reader. Others may see it and feel confused because they do not know how to answer. This is the distinction I want to explore: we can stop an unproductive exchange with one person while continuing to explain the claim to those who read it.

For this reason, I do not think we must join the argument beneath every post. We can describe the claim clearly on our own page and examine its foundations. A response can also address readers seeking to understand the issue. In deciding whether to respond, I will ask what our explanation can clarify.

When I consider an explanation necessary

I consider an explanation worthwhile when part of a verse is quoted while its continuation is withheld; a translation choice is presented as the text’s only possible meaning; a statement whose source cannot be found is attributed to the Prophet; or an individual’s conduct is used to draw conclusions about the entire religion. These are types of claim we will examine, rather than advance judgements about a particular post.

If we encounter a difficulty that genuinely needs investigation, I will state it plainly too. To establish that a claim is invalid, we need to show what is wrong with its premises, information or conclusion. I also want to distinguish explaining my own faith from offering shared evidence to someone who begins from different assumptions.

How we will examine each claim

First, we will convey the claim without changing its meaning and record the post’s source and date. We will then identify the verse, transmitted report, historical information or reasoning on which it relies. After examining the full text and context, we will ask whether the claim’s conclusion actually follows from that information.

My own thinking will appear under a separate heading. The Qur’anic text, hadith reports, explanations in Risale-i Nur and my own assessment will remain distinguishable. When drawing on Risale-i Nur, we will identify the work and relevant section. We will not present wording we cannot verify as the source’s own statement.

Responsibility in responding

A misleading claim does not mean we know with certainty the intentions of the person sharing it. We will not build our examination around judging a person’s worth or inner life. Humiliating someone who asks a sincere question undermines the purpose of offering an explanation.

My aim in this section is to help people with questions about their faith see the issue more clearly. We will look for the strength of our answers in their reasoning. Correcting an error in my own assessment is part of the same responsibility.
