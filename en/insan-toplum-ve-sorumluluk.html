Human Beings, Society and Responsibility

Imagine cleaning an apartment building’s staircase only as far as our own door. Our doorstep is clean, but on the way downstairs we pass through dirt left by someone else. Although we have protected our own space, we have failed to make the shared space liveable. This small example from everyday life illustrates an important aspect of the relationship between individuals and society: our individual well-being does not depend solely on what we do for ourselves.

Others’ labour is present in the bread we eat, the roads we use, the education we receive and the care we need. In turn, our actions enter others’ lives: sometimes through production, sometimes through care, and sometimes through careful use of a shared resource. Social responsibility begins with recognising this interdependence. How it should be shared is a separate question of justice.

Living together is more than being alike

It is insufficient to see a community merely as the sum of people living in the same place. Neighbourhood relations, the division of labour, trust, rules and shared needs connect people’s lives. People who have never met may need the same water network, transport system and health services.

Durkheim, in The Division of Labor in Society , distinguishes solidarity arising from similarity from solidarity arising from the interdependence of differentiated tasks. In solidarity developed through the division of labour, people becoming different from one another does not mean social bonds disappear entirely.[1] This distinction matters because it prevents explaining shared life solely through having the same opinions.

The idea I defend on this basis is that people do not need to agree on everything to live together; they need to respect the conditions that make one another’s lives possible. Protecting the rights of someone with a different belief, occupation or political view does not mean endorsing all their choices. It means accepting that the shared order should provide a space in which they, as well as we, can live.

The near and wider circles of responsibility

The approach I call Circles of Responsibility proposes considering together a person’s obligations to themselves, their family, their immediate surroundings and wider social spheres. These circles are not a ranking in which human worth diminishes with distance. They describe relationships of responsibility that vary with proximity, need, resources and duties undertaken.

It is understandable for someone to prioritise their own child’s needs. This priority, however, cannot justify indifference to other children’s safety. Similarly, attending to one’s own neighbourhood’s problems does not justify excluding other neighbourhoods from shared resources. Concern for those nearby and indifference towards those farther away are not the same attitude.

The scope of the circles cannot be limited to relationships between people either. Protecting water, soil and the shared living environment relates to the conditions in which future people can live. Nevertheless, expecting everyone to intervene in every problem to the same extent is unrealistic. Knowledge, resources, influence and role must be considered together when assessing responsibility.

This approach is used here as a framework for reflection. It is not claimed to be a model explaining all responsibilities or one validated by field research.

Family solidarity and changing resources

A family member’s illness or loss of economic resources can change the direction of responsibility. Someone who previously provided support needing support themselves does not diminish their worth within the family. Other members may add new responsibilities to their existing burdens to sustain their shared whole.

Explaining this relationship from the outset as an account of debts can miss how sacrifice is experienced. While helping, people do not know who will attain a better occupation, income or education years later. The contribution may have been undertaken voluntarily in response to the need at the time.

Conversely, mentioning a past sacrifice later does not necessarily mean demanding repayment. A person may feel their actions have not been appreciated. The contribution itself, the meaning that person attaches to it and how others assess it are separate questions. Understanding family life requires preserving these distinctions.

Goodwill does not mean unlimited duties

The same member doing everything in an association may show that tasks get done; it does not show they are shared fairly. If people turn only to the person who previously agreed, without ever approaching others with comparable resources, we must question how solidarity is organised.

Helping once does not constitute indefinite consent to all subsequent requests. The person requesting responsibility also has an obligation to explain the task’s scope, consider alternatives and provide necessary support. Asking only “Why did you agree?” can make the requester’s part invisible.

Nor can every unequal allocation be called exploitation. Expertise, explicit commitments, urgency or others’ invisible burdens may explain differences. The aim, therefore, is not to make contributors suspicious, but to protect both continuity of help and the person’s capacity.

The boundary between freedom and responsibility

Social responsibility does not confer authority over every aspect of an individual’s life. Disapproving of another person’s choice is not sufficient on its own to justify intervening in it. Mill, in On Liberty , distinguishes choices concerning one’s own life from behaviour affecting others’ rights and interests; he discusses the limits of social intervention within this tension.[2]

Drawing on this discussion, the criterion I propose is to make the justification for a demand for responsibility visible: Who is affected, what need is involved, what is being asked of the person, and on what authority does the request rest? These questions can help assess both indifference and expectations of unlimited intervention.

Being able to set boundaries is not a total rejection of responsibility. However, the source of an obligation also matters. Leaving a voluntary role and abruptly leaving someone who needs care without support cannot be assessed in the same way. Duration, a safe handover and others’ circumstances must be considered.

The circle widens in public office

Prioritising those close to us in personal relationships is not the same as prioritising them when managing public resources. If an office enables someone to make decisions on society’s behalf, it also changes the scope of their responsibility. According to the normative principle defended in this essay, the measure of public office is the ability to justify one’s conduct to everyone within its remit, beyond personal loyalties.

Responsibility must therefore not be left solely to individual goodwill. Clear job descriptions, explanations for decisions and oversight mechanisms can limit shared life’s dependence on personal connections. Expecting people to be good and striving to establish a well-functioning order must proceed together.

Responsibility, resources and knowledge

Knowing that a problem exists does not mean having the power to solve it alone. Likewise, not directly causing a problem does not automatically remove all our obligations in relation to it. Someone witnessing an injury may be able to call for help rather than provide specialist treatment. An employee who notices a workplace hazard may notify the appropriate person responsible even if unable to remove the hazard alone. These examples show that responsibility does not always require the same action.

Assessment should therefore ask three questions together: What did the person know, what opportunities for intervention did they have, and what duty had they undertaken? After learning the outcome, it may be unfair to expect everyone to have foreseen it. Conversely, knowingly ignoring a clear danger cannot be explained merely by saying “I did not do it.” A specific assessment must rest on knowledge of the event and its circumstances.

Capacity should not become a general excuse for evading responsibility. Yet an understanding of responsibility that ignores human limits is also impracticable. What is needed is to establish support and cooperation that enable duties to be fulfilled, rather than giving everyone the same burden.

Rules, objection and mutual trust

Rules for shared life clarify what people can expect of one another. But a rule’s long use does not show that it produces fair outcomes in every situation. The division of duties may change, needs may differ and previously unrecognised harm may become visible. Responsible conduct therefore includes asking for a practice’s justification when necessary.

Immediately accusing someone who objects of opposing solidarity can make a shared problem harder to see. An employee may be objecting not to the work being done, but to the burden constantly being left to them. A citizen may accept the need for a public service while questioning its allocation criteria. Separating the subject of a request from the way it is shared makes these discussions clearer.

Trust cannot be maintained solely through declarations of goodwill. We must be able to assess together a person’s promises and actions, the authority they hold and burden they undertake, and what they demand of themselves and expect of others. Establishing relationships in which errors can be acknowledged, reasons explained and burdens redistributed is part of the understanding of responsibility defended in this essay.

The sustainability of shared life

Making society’s continuity depend on those able to sacrifice ever more can concentrate responsibility within a narrow circle. Equal contributions may not be possible, but the reasons for differences should be explainable. No definitive judgment on fair allocation is possible without establishing the relationship between needs, resources and duties.

While building their own lives, people also produce the conditions in which they will live with others. Responsibility means recognising this relationship: contributing when necessary, sharing burdens when necessary, and being accountable when holding authority. The quality of shared life should be sought not only in how much sacrifice is made, but also in how far the need for that sacrifice is reduced and how the burden is shared.
