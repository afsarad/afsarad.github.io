Qur’an, hadith and Risale-i Nur: a reading guide

Adem Afşar · 17 November 2026

How can we distinguish the Qur’an, hadith and Risale-i Nur?

When beginning to understand a religious statement, we need to identify what kind of text it is. A verse, a Turkish explanation of that verse, a report attributed to the Prophet and a later author’s interpretation require different source references. This guide offers a starting point for reading with those distinctions in mind.

What is the difference between a Qur’an translation and commentary?

A translation attempts to convey the meaning of the Qur’an in another language; this involves translation choices. Commentary explains and interprets verses with reference to language, context and relevant sources. When reading a Turkish explanation, we need to see whether it is the Arabic verse, a translation or an explanation. Differences in wording between translations of the same verse should be examined through their reasons and contexts. [1]

What is hadith, and how can a statement’s source be investigated?

Hadith is a central term for reports concerning the Prophet’s words, actions and approvals. Sharing a statement as a hadith does not remove the need to investigate its source. Its work and chapter, transmission and assessments of reliability are separate matters to examine. A post that merely labels something a hadith does not supply that information. [2]

What is the relationship between the Qur’an and hadith?

Verse 44 of Surah al-Nahl addresses the Prophet’s task of explaining to people what was revealed to them. Diyanet’s Kur’an Yolu commentary considers that task in relation to verbal explanation and practical example. This connection does not require confusing the source category of a Qur’anic verse with that of a hadith report. Readers can investigate the relationship of meaning by following the sources behind an explanation. [3]

Where does the Risale-i Nur Collection fit into this reading?

When reading the Risale-i Nur Collection, comprising works by Bediüzzaman Said Nursi, we distinguish the source passage, any verse or hadith it invokes, and the interpretation offered by its commentator. Our essay “Inspiration, Revelation and Reading the Universe” examines the collection’s distinction between revelation and inspiration with references to works and sections. This guide provides an introduction to that examination. [4]

What questions can we ask while reading?

We can ask who made a statement, where it appears and what comes before and after it. We can then compare our understanding with what the text says. For example, if we do not yet know whether a piece of advice is a verse, a hadith or an author’s interpretation, our first task is to investigate that distinction. Valuing a statement does not prevent accurate attribution. This reading sequence is the method Adem Afşar proposes here.
