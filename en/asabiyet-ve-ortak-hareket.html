From Solidarity to Political Power, from Shared Purpose to Institutional Order

Adem Afşar · Conceptual Study · 1 November 2026

Why do people act together? How can a shared danger, a shared need or an expectation of a common future bring different individuals together?

These questions show that social solidarity is not merely emotional closeness; it is also related to the capacity to produce collective action.

Ibn Khaldun’s concept of asabiyyah is one historical explanation of this relationship. Applying the concept to contemporary societies, however, requires separate consideration of historical context and new institutional conditions.

1. Asabiyyah in Ibn Khaldun

In the Muqaddimah, Ibn Khaldun invokes asabiyyah to explain communities’ capacities to protect one another, act together and attain political supremacy.

Asabiyyah cannot be reduced to blood ties alone. Although kinship is an important source, alliances, living together, shared experience and mutual commitment can also strengthen solidarity.

In Ibn Khaldun’s analysis, strong asabiyyah is a decisive factor in establishing political authority. Yet the consolidation of political power can gradually transform the relationships of solidarity that made it possible.

These explanations are based on the Muqaddimah’s sections on asabiyyah and royal authority. [1]

Conceptual distinction

Although asabiyyah relates to social solidarity, it does not mean mutual assistance alone. It also encompasses a community’s capacity to defend itself, undertake collective action and be effective within power relations.

2. Initial conditions for collective action

People do not always need to share the same opinions, identity or way of life to act together.

A natural disaster, economic crisis, security need or shared production goal can lead different individuals towards temporary or lasting association.

Three elements are proposed for consideration together here:

Shared need

The existence of a need that individuals find difficult to meet alone.

Shared purpose

Defining the response to the need around a specific goal.

Mutual trust

Participants being able to develop reasonable expectations of one another’s behaviour.

This threefold distinction is not a classification taken directly from Ibn Khaldun. It is an analytical framework proposed in this study for examining collective action.

3. When does solidarity become power?

Strong solidarity among a community’s members does not, on its own, show that political power will emerge.

For solidarity to become political power, conditions such as organisation, the use of resources, decision-making capacity and continuity of collective action become important.

Ibn Khaldun’s emphasis on the relationship between asabiyyah and royal authority provides a historical explanation of this transformation. [1]

Being able to act together makes a common power possible; how that power will be used is a separate question of responsibility.

4. What happens when a shared purpose is achieved?

One of this study’s central questions is how an association changes once its shared purpose has been achieved.

An organisation initially formed to meet a specific need may wish to continue existing after achieving its purpose.

This desire is not always negative. It may be useful for preserving gains, meeting new needs and ensuring institutional continuity.

Nevertheless, the initial shared purpose and the organisation’s later interests may also diverge.

Main research question

Under what conditions does a structure established to meet a shared need continue serving its original purpose, and under what conditions does preserving its own existence become its primary objective?

5. From solidarity to institutional order

When collective action becomes continuous, needs arise to allocate tasks, establish decision-making procedures and manage resources.

At this stage, rules, duties and institutional relationships may partly replace the initial personal commitments.

Institutionalisation and solidarity, however, are not necessarily opposites. Institutions can preserve shared purposes while also reshaping relationships among members.

What needs examination, therefore, is not merely the institution’s existence, but the purpose it serves and to whom it is accountable.

6. The exclusionary aspect of solidarity

Strong group commitment can generate trust and mutual assistance among members.

The same commitment, however, may also lead to the rights of individuals outside the group being pushed into the background.

For example, employees supporting one another within an institution can produce positive results. But if this support turns into concealing mistakes or favouring personal connections over competence, it conflicts with public responsibility.

The relationship between asabiyyah and nepotism also being discussed in contemporary scholarship shows that the concept should not be treated solely as positive solidarity. [3]

7. The weakening of asabiyyah

Ibn Khaldun discusses how the consolidation of political power can bring changes to the ruling community’s living conditions and relationships of solidarity.

The relationship between power, prosperity and forms of government matters in this approach. Conditions of struggle during a founding period differ from those under established government. [1]

This historical explanation, however, does not directly establish that all states will pass through the same stages or collapse in the same way.

Contemporary institutions, legal orders, technology, international relations and economic structures must be assessed separately.

8. Cycle or transformation?

At this point, the discussion of asabiyyah joins another of our conceptual studies, the “Not a Cycle, but a Spiral” approach.

The transformation of communities that gain strength through solidarity throughout history may suggest recurring processes.

Similarity, however, does not mean historical conditions are identical.

Every new political and social structure takes shape alongside knowledge, institutions and experiences inherited from previous periods.

When examining historical transformations, recurring patterns must therefore be distinguished from changing conditions.

9. Critical assessment

Some questions remain open when using asabiyyah to explain contemporary societies.

Through what mechanisms does commitment developed through kinship and face-to-face relationships find a counterpart in large and complex societies?

If individuals united around a common goal have different expectations, how should the success of their association be measured?

If the benefits a community provides to its own members come at the cost of harm to other communities, how should this association be assessed?

These questions require investigating both the historical explanatory power of asabiyyah and the limits of its application to contemporary societies.

10. Conclusion

Asabiyyah is an important historical concept for understanding people’s capacity for collective action.

The existence of solidarity, however, does not mean it is always fair, inclusive or beneficial to society as a whole.

Why collective action emerges matters, as do the purposes for which it continues, who governs it and who is affected by its outcomes.

A community’s power to act together should be assessed not only by what it can achieve, but also by how it bears responsibility for what it achieves.

This proposition is not a quotation from Ibn Khaldun. It is this study’s concluding proposition, developed through reflection on the concept of asabiyyah.

12. Academic development plan

In subsequent work, relevant sections of the Muqaddimah will be selected, page numbers of the translation used will be verified and in-text citations will be expanded.

Relationships between asabiyyah, social solidarity, institutionalisation and political authority will be compared with different theoretical approaches.

In particular, Ibn Khaldun’s original explanations, later researchers’ interpretations and Adem Afşar’s own assessments will be presented separately.

This work is not yet a completed peer-reviewed academic article, but a conceptual study whose sourcing will be developed.
