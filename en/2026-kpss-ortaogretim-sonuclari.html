Sociology of Current Affairs · 18 November 2026

When are the 2026 KPSS secondary education results due? Why do public-sector jobs matter?

According to the ÖSYM calendar, results for the KPSS secondary education examination held on 25 October 2026 are due on 19 November 2026. Once released, candidates will be able to view their scores through the ÖSYM results portal. ÖSYM President Bayram Ali Ersoy reported that 2,985,438 candidates applied for the exam. The figure counts applications. After scores are released, placement in a public-sector job depends on the requirements of the position and the relevant recruitment process. [1]

ÖSYM results portal

On 18 November, “When will the KPSS secondary education results be announced?” appeared among rising searches on Google Trends in Türkiye. This shows what information people were seeking. Understanding why candidates took the exam would require speaking with them and examining their working conditions. Beyond the result date lies a question about what people hope to secure for their future when they consider a public-sector job. [2]

The value of a job also depends on how far ahead a person can plan

Imagine receiving two job offers. The first pays more, but the hours change frequently and contract renewal is uncertain. The second pays less, but the employee knows what they will earn at the end of the month and when they will be working next week. Someone who pays rent alone or cares for a relative might find the second offer more suitable. Someone else might prioritize higher earnings or the chance to advance in a profession.

This hypothetical example illustrates one part of what job security can mean in daily life. Knowing your earnings and hours makes it easier to decide whether to enroll in a course, move into your own home or share caregiving responsibilities. Working in the public sector can be seen as one way to meet those expectations. Whether it does so depends on the position and its working conditions.

One person takes the exam, but a choice can affect the whole household

A candidate considering a position in another province must weigh the job alongside local rent, moving costs and responsibilities at home. Of two candidates with the same score, one might have family support during the first few months. The other may want to keep contributing to the household budget or stay close to a relative who needs care. The same vacancies can therefore lead to different choices.

A family may also expect something different from public employment than the candidate does. Relatives might value continuity of work while the candidate prioritizes working in the field they trained for or staying in their city. Adding a position to a preference list can bring those expectations into conversation. What matters sociologically is the choice itself and the support a candidate can draw on to make their own decision.

What matters after results day?

Nearly three million applications show how widely the exam figured in people's plans. To understand the reasons for that interest, researchers could compare the experiences of people already in work with those looking for their first job. Someone seeking a change of job may want more predictable hours. Others may want work that fits their qualifications or allows them to stay where they live. These are possibilities to investigate. The application count alone cannot show how common each reason is. [1]

A centralized exam allows applicants’ scores to be compared using a common measure. After that, clearly worded vacancies, explicit qualifications and a predictable application timetable can help candidates make informed decisions. A candidate can work out whether a move is feasible, for example, once they know the location and duties of the job. This everyday experience also matters to institutional trust. Can applicants make plans based on the information they receive, and are the published conditions applied consistently as recruitment proceeds?

On results day, candidates will have a new piece of information. Its value will become clearer when they see which jobs they can apply for and what those jobs could change in their lives. The attention paid to KPSS raises a wider question about working life. Beyond providing a living, how much control does a job give people over their own future?
