Publication Approach and Use of Sources

Principles for understanding human behaviour alongside the social conditions and relationships in which it takes shape.

HomeArticles and essaysConceptual studies

This page explains the questions behind the writing on this site, how sources are used, and how the distinction between evidence and interpretation is maintained. The aim is not only to present a conclusion, but also to give readers a way to follow the reasoning and grounds behind it.

What questions guide my study of people and society?

When I examine a social phenomenon, my first step is not to place a person or group into ready-made categories such as right or wrong, guilty or innocent. I ask: What conditions make a behaviour possible? Which institutions, rules, resources and relationships shape the choices people face? How does a decision acquire meaning in people’s everyday lives, in public services and in shared responsibilities?

My starting point is this sentence: “The human being is the same everywhere, at all times.” People may share basic human needs and capacities, while living conditions, institutions and available opportunities differ. The meaning of a similar behaviour cannot be understood without examining the context in which it occurs. Unusual cases can also help us ask how the boundaries of what is considered ordinary are formed, without turning those cases into labels for a whole group.

How does the interdisciplinary perspective work?

Sociology remains at the centre of this work. History helps trace how a phenomenon develops over time; archaeology can reveal what material traces and places tell us; philosophy and political thought help examine the foundations of concepts and institutions; social psychology can bring the interaction between individual and group behaviour into view. I do not bring every field into every essay to the same degree. I use the contributions relevant to the question at hand.

This approach is more than placing terms from different fields side by side. A concept or historical example belongs in a text when it helps explain the social relationships under study. The aim is to connect disciplines in a way that makes the question easier to understand.

The path I follow in an inquiry

  1. Define the question: Make clear which social phenomenon and relationship the essay seeks to understand.
  2. Establish the context: Consider the connections among the event’s time, place, institutions, participants and material conditions.
  3. Trace the relationships: Assess individual choices alongside social expectations, rules and decision-making processes.
  4. Separate evidence from interpretation: Do not present what a source reports and the meaning I draw from it as though they were the same. Identify an explanation as an interpretation or proposal when that is what it is.
  5. Show the limits: Consider what the data, example or source review can explain and what remains uncertain.

A sociological explanation in an essay does not, by itself, claim to prove an individual’s intentions or every decision made by an institution. Readers should consider an interpretation together with its evidence and scope.

How do I use sources?

For current events, I consult direct announcements and records, official data and reliable reporting about the event where possible. For historical and theoretical subjects, I turn to primary texts, academic studies and reputable publications on the subject. I assess whether a source actually supports the claim in the text; a list of sources alone does not guarantee accuracy.

Source use varies with the type of writing. A reflective essay may begin with a personal observation; a conceptual study shows how it engages with relevant theories; a source-based research essay aims to identify the works and data behind it in a way readers can follow. I take care to distinguish a quotation from a primary text from my own interpretation. When the date or scope of information matters, I state it in the text.

Where original concepts and interpretations belong

The views of Ibn Khaldun, Machiavelli and other thinkers are not presented on the same footing as my interpretations developed in dialogue with them. Concepts such as “not a cycle, but a spiral,” “society’s synergy of reaching agreement” and “circles of responsibility” are analytical proposals developed on this site. I treat them as contributions whose explanatory value and limits can be discussed, not as established scientific consensus.
