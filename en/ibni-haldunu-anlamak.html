About the Book

Understanding Ibn Khaldunis an intellectual journey toward understanding the questions through which Ibn Khaldun examined societies, rather than memorizing his concepts. Asabiyyah, umran, the state, production and social ties are explored through comparisons extending from Rome to China and from Göbeklitepe to the modern world.

The book distinguishes Ibn Khaldun's views, what historical sources demonstrate and the author's original interpretations. Society's synergy of agreement and not a cycle, but a spiral are analytical proposals developed in this work.

From the Preface

“This book was not written to explain Ibn Khaldun again to those who already understand him. It was written to show how he thought to people who read Ibn Khaldun but cannot understand him.”

DEDICATION AND ACKNOWLEDGMENTS

To my dear wife Hülya

I dedicate this book to my dear wife Hülya, not because she always supported me, but because she showed me what it means to continue thinking even when I was not supported.

“You are too old now.”

“You will never achieve anything from now on.”

“Do not bother with these things.”

“Who do you think you are?”

Words like these can extinguish a person's desire to think.

Amid all this, I continued asking myself whether age prevents people from thinking, learning, being curious and creating something new.

Perhaps this book is, in part, an answer to that question.

Every day I became curious about a new subject. I read. I asked. I debated. I was mistaken. I thought again. And eventually I knocked on Ibn Khaldun's door.

The dedication of this book therefore carries a meaning very different from a simple acknowledgment:

Sometimes what moves people forward is not only the support they receive; the limits they encounter can also force them to test themselves.

I thank Hülya for being part of this long journey in my life.

PREFACE

“I Read Ibn Khaldun, but I Cannot Understand Him”

When I first heard Ibn Khaldun's name, what I encountered were the great ideas of a great thinker.

Then I began reading the Muqaddimah.

After a while, I asked myself:

“Why am I reading this but unable to understand it?”

There were sentences.

There were concepts.

There was asabiyyah.

There was umran.

There was the state.

There were Bedouin and sedentary ways of life.

There were the rise and fall of societies.

But seeing how all these connected was not easy.

This book emerged from that difficulty.

This book was not written to explain Ibn Khaldun again to those who already understand him.

It was written to show how he thought to people who read Ibn Khaldun but cannot understand him.

I was one of those people.

I will therefore not talk down to the reader here.

I will not simply say, “This is Ibn Khaldun,” and move on.

First, we will ask together:

What was Ibn Khaldun trying to see?

Then:

Which concept did he use to express that observation?

Then:

Which mechanism within society does this concept explain?

And finally:

Can we see the same mechanism in other societies?

Perhaps, by the end of the book, the most important change readers experience will be this:

“I no longer memorize what Ibn Khaldun said; I can understand how he looked at things.”

Adem Afşar

AN EXCERPT FROM THE INTRODUCTION

Comparing Humanity's Solutions, Not Human Beings

The starting point of this book is a simple idea:

Human beings are human wherever in the world they live.

The person on the banks of the Nile is human.
So is the person in Mesopotamia.
So is the person in Rome.
So is the person on the Central Asian steppe.
So is the person in China.
So is the person in today's Istanbul.
So is the person in today's New York.

The environments in which people live differ.

Climates differ.
Lands differ.
Resources differ.
Dangers differ.
Technologies differ.
Accumulated knowledge differs.
Modes of production differ.

But humanity's fundamental problems do not disappear entirely.

People must eat.
They must find shelter.
They must ensure their security.
They must protect their children.
They must produce.
They must cooperate with others.
They must share or distribute resources.
They must resolve conflicts.
They must establish rules to live together.

And above all, they try to secure their future as far as possible.

How, then, should we explain the great differences we see throughout history?

Perhaps what differs is not the human being but humanity's solutions.

About the Book

An intellectual study considering Ibn Khaldun's observations on umran, asabiyyah, the city, production and power in their historical context.

The work questions the ties that enable communities to act together and how those ties are transformed under changing social conditions.

Main Themes

  • Umran and the formation of society
  • Asabiyyah and social solidarity
  • Production, urbanization and civilization
  • Power and social change
  • Ibn Khaldun's intellectual legacy
