Being Oneself: Awareness, Innate Dispositions and Social Coherence

Adem Afşar · Interdisciplinary Sociological Analyst · 25 November 2026

What does it mean to be oneself?

A statement in a post by Alaaddin Güven on 25 November 2026, suggesting that a person exists insofar as they are themselves, invites reflection on character and social formation. Preserving character and explaining its formation are distinct matters. Change is not necessarily deterioration; people may revise their behaviour as they understand new conditions.

Humanity’s shared inheritance

A newborn enters a world produced by earlier generations. Language, names for objects, knowledge, moral standards and institutions are collective achievements. Removing these hypothetically from an individual would remove much of their social identity. Yet this inheritance does not prevent individuality; it provides a basis for choice. Mead’s account of the social formation of the self offers a useful framework for considering this distinction.

Conditions and character

The familiar saying that hard times produce strong people and easy times ordinary people invites reflection on the influence of circumstances. It is not a universal law: hardship can strengthen or damage people, and favourable conditions can foster creativity and responsibility. The central question is how people interpret their circumstances.

Subjective benefit and action

A school donor may find satisfaction in having their name remembered or in remaining anonymous. Modesty may bring verbal or non-verbal appreciation; insistence on anonymity may bring satisfaction through the fulfilment of one's own choice. Neither possibility negates the public value of the gift. Cooley’s looking-glass self helps illuminate social appreciation. However, experienced satisfaction must be distinguished from the original motive.

Individual behavioural coherence

Being oneself does not mean merely acting differently from others. People may consciously adopt historically tested patterns of conduct that fit contemporary circumstances, pursue the greatest feasible common benefit and accord with their innate dispositions. Such compatibility refers to tendencies and capacities, not the legitimacy of every impulse. Common benefit must also be considered alongside individual rights and avoidance of harm.

Awareness and autonomy

Deciding to be oneself depends on awareness of one's formation and the consequences of one's actions. Without developed awareness, a person may fulfil social functions yet struggle to establish conscious behavioural coherence. This does not diminish human worth; it concerns the limits of behavioural autonomy. Awareness can develop through education and experience.

Adem Afşar’s sociological analysis

A person can decide to be themselves only by becoming aware of how they have been formed. Consciously adopting, from humanity’s collective achievements, patterns of behaviour suited to contemporary conditions, social benefit and their own innate characteristics is the foundation of a coherent way of acting unique to that individual. Read alongside the proposition that human beings are fundamentally the same everywhere and at all times, this approach suggests that shared human tendencies can develop into different patterns of conduct under different historical conditions. It is a conceptual proposition open to inquiry, not a proven universal law.
