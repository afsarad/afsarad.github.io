Adem Afşar · Conceptual Study · 1 November 2026

Does the recurrence of similar events mean history repeats itself? Or does every recurrence take place at a different historical position because of inherited conditions?

The rise and decline of states, economic crises, political struggles and social transformations may show similar patterns throughout history.

Yet similarity differs significantly from identity. This study questions the limits of explaining historical processes solely through cyclical repetition.

1. Cyclical Understandings of History

Cyclical understandings of history emphasise that certain historical processes may recur through similar stages.

Stages such as formation, strengthening, institutionalisation, decline and dissolution may become important in these approaches.

However, not every cyclical approach claims that the past repeats itself unchanged.

Our criticism therefore concerns not the study of historical patterns, but their interpretation as absolute identity.

2. Ibn Khaldun's Historical Analysis

In the Muqaddimah, Ibn Khaldun examines the formation of political power, the role of asabiyyah and the strengthening and eventual transformation of dynasties.

His analysis identifies important differences between social solidarity during formation and the conditions of established rule.

Continuity of rule, prosperity, habits, resource use and political relationships may influence dynasties' historical trajectories.

Ibn Khaldun's explanations of dynasties matter for the study of historical patterns. [1]

However, the spiral approach advanced here is neither a concept belonging to Ibn Khaldun nor his direct statement.

3. The Difference Between a Cycle and a Spiral

Cycle

A conceptual model describing a process that passes through certain stages and returns to its starting point.

Spiral

A model used to describe similar patterns reappearing under conditions changed by the effects of previous processes.

What matters in the spiral analogy is that similar events can occur on different historical planes.

This approach does not claim that history necessarily progresses or that each new period is better than the previous one.

Similar events may occur in history, but no event is entirely independent of the effects of what preceded it.

4. Historical Accumulation

Societies do not lose their past experience entirely.

Knowledge, law, technology, production methods, institutions and social habits may be transmitted to later periods in different forms.

This transmission does not always produce positive outcomes.

Inherited institutions may facilitate solutions to some problems while perpetuating others.

Historical accumulation may therefore be a source of persistent problems as well as development.

5. Path Dependence and Historical Choices

The path-dependence approach used in historical institutionalism investigates how earlier decisions can affect later options.

Paul Pierson discusses the importance of decision timing, event sequencing and the lasting effects of certain institutional choices in political processes. [2]

This approach can be compared with the spiral analogy in understanding how the past affects the present.

However, the approaches are not identical. Path dependence seeks to explain specific causal mechanisms, while the spiral serves here as a more general framework of historical interpretation.

6. Historical Time and Experience

Reinhart Koselleck emphasises the relationship between past experience and expectations of the future in understanding historical time. [3]

People do not act solely according to what happened in the past.

Expectations of what might happen in the future also influence their decisions.

An event apparently of the same kind may thus produce different meanings and outcomes in different periods.

7. Similar Crises, Different Societies

Imagine two societies experiencing economic crises.

Unemployment, production losses and political debate may arise in both.

Yet their institutions, production structures, accumulated knowledge and experience of previous crises may differ.

Apparently similar crises may therefore lead to different social outcomes.

This is a hypothetical example for conceptual explanation, not an empirical comparison of two particular countries.

8. Does History Repeat Itself?

In everyday language, “history repeats itself” describes the recurrence of similar events.

Historical research must nevertheless explain the level at which recurrence occurs.

Is what recurs an institution, a form of behaviour, a political relationship or different mechanisms producing similar outcomes?

Without answering these questions, merely stating that events resemble one another is insufficient for historical explanation.

9. Limits of the Spiral Approach

The spiral analogy does not prove that all historical processes move in a single direction.

Some institutions may disappear, knowledge may be lost and societies may face more severe problems than before.

The spiral must therefore not become a claim of inevitable progress or continuous improvement.

Nor is it sufficient to assume that every inherited influence matters.

Concrete historical research must show which influence changed the subsequent period and through which mechanism.

Central research question

When similar historical events occur in different periods, how can we identify the historical accumulation and institutional differences explaining their different outcomes?

10. Social Responsibility and Historical Learning

Transmitting historical experience to later generations means more than remembering the past.

Understanding the causes of past problems may contribute to different decisions when similar conditions arise.

Yet the existence of historical experience does not mean societies will necessarily make correct decisions.

Historical learning should therefore be considered together with access to information, critical evaluation and institutional practices.

11. Conclusion

Historical processes may contain recurring patterns.

However, the conditions in which those patterns occur change over time.

Societies approach new historical situations with inherited knowledge, institutions, relationships and problems.

Historical recurrence must therefore be distinguished from historical identity.

History may encounter similar questions again, but it does not occupy the same historical point at every encounter.

This conclusion is an interpretation developed within Adem Afşar's “Not a Cycle, but a Spiral” approach. It is not a direct quotation from any thinker.
