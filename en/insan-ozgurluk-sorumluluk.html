Adem Afşar · Conceptual Study · 1 November 2026

Do people determine their behavior through their own choices, or are they directed by the expectations of the society in which they live?

Does freedom mean only being able to choose? Or is accepting the consequences of a choice also an inseparable dimension of freedom?

This study discusses the relationship between individual choice and social responsibility through the effects of norms, culture and moral consistency.

1. Are human beings the same everywhere?

Human beings are the same everywhere; the conditions they create to sustain their lives differ.
— Adem Afşar

This proposition does not claim that people exhibit identical behavior in every society.

People's basic needs, such as food, shelter, security and sustaining life, may share common features.

However, the institutions, values, habits and social relationships created to meet these needs differ according to historical and geographical conditions.

Shared biological characteristics and cultural differences should therefore be treated not as opposites but as phenomena to examine together.

2. Freedom and choice

Freedom is closely related to individuals' ability to choose.

Not every choice, however, is made under equal conditions.

Economic means, education, law, social pressure, family relationships and political conditions can expand or restrict the options available.

The apparent choice of a behavior therefore does not prove that the choice was made free of all pressure.

Formal freedom of choice

An option being legally or apparently available to choose.

Practical opportunity to choose

An individual's actual ability to consider and pursue that option under existing conditions.

3. The formation of social norms

Social norms shape which behaviors are regarded as appropriate, expected or acceptable in a society.

Individuals can learn these norms through family, education, everyday relationships and social institutions.

Émile Durkheim's approach to social facts is important for examining social rules that exist outside individuals and can influence their behavior.

However, a society's acceptance of a norm does not itself establish its moral rightness.

4. How human are we when alone?

A person's humanity is measured by how human they remain when alone.
— Adem Afşar

This proposition does not mean that individuals lose their human status when alone.

It asks how closely the values people claim to embrace under others' scrutiny align with their behavior when no scrutiny is present.

For example, there is a difference in moral consistency between valuing honesty only when one might be caught and maintaining the same standard when nobody is watching.

However, a single act performed alone cannot justify a definitive judgment of someone's entire moral character.

5. Moral consistency and social control

Social control can help behavior conform to certain rules.

Nevertheless, reasons for following rules are not always the same.

Individuals may behave similarly because they fear punishment, wish to avoid social exclusion or genuinely embrace the relevant rule.

Because outwardly similar behaviors can have different underlying reasons, appearances alone are insufficient.

6. Are norms the same in every society?

Societies can differ in historical experiences, beliefs, economic circumstances and cultural relationships.

Behavior considered ordinary in one society may therefore be disapproved of in another.

Yet the existence of cultural differences does not exempt every practice from criticism.

Questions involving individuals' right to life, security, freedom and human dignity require assessment beyond social acceptance.

7. Social acceptance and moral rightness

Common behavior in a society does not always coincide with morally defensible behavior.

The historical normalization of discrimination or inequality in certain societies does not establish the justice of those practices.

Sociological explanation and moral evaluation should therefore be distinguished.

Sociological question

Under what conditions do people adopt and maintain a particular behavior?

Moral question

How should this behavior's consequences for others be evaluated?

8. Does freedom remove responsibility?

The ability to make choices about one's own life is an important dimension of freedom.

Some choices, however, affect more than the person making them.

Responsibility arises when behavior affects other people's rights, safety or conditions of shared life.

John Stuart Mill's discussions of liberty provide an important basis for comparing individual choice with harm to others.

However, the boundaries of harm and society's authority to intervene require separate discussion.

9. Connections with circles of responsibility

Individuals' responsibilities toward themselves, their families, immediate circles and broader society may arise in different ways.

This approach is discussed in greater detail in Circles of Responsibility.

Prioritizing close relationships does not justify disregarding other individuals' fundamental rights.

The relationship between freedom and responsibility is therefore not confined to one's immediate circle.

10. Opposing views and limitations

The approach to moral consistency advocated here remains open to several questions.

Human behavior may change under different circumstances. Fear, pressure, deprivation and insufficient knowledge can affect decisions.

Assessing behavior therefore requires examining circumstances as well as outcomes.

Furthermore, opposing society's prevailing norms does not always mean being irresponsible.

In some circumstances, resisting an unjust social rule may be required by moral responsibility.

Central research question

Within what limits can an individual's moral consistency be assessed by comparing behavior under social scrutiny with behavior in its absence?

11. Conclusion

People may share basic needs, but the ways they meet those needs differ according to social circumstances.

Individual freedom concerns the opportunity to choose. Responsibility requires evaluating choices' consequences and effects on others.

Moral consistency helps us question the relationship between people's professed values and their behavior.

Human freedom becomes visible in the capacity to choose; responsibility in one's relationship with the consequences of that choice.

This conclusion is the study's conceptual evaluation, not a direct quotation from another thinker.

12. Academic sources

The following works are initial sources for detailed comparison in later studies. This list does not mean that every claim in the text has yet been supported by page-specific citations.

  1. Durkheim, Émile (1895). Les Règles de la méthode sociologique. A foundational work on defining and examining social facts.
  2. Mill, John Stuart (1859). On Liberty. A foundational work on individual liberty, social intervention and the harm principle.
  3. Fromm, Erich (1941). Escape from Freedom. An examination of relationships between freedom, insecurity and the turn toward authority.
  4. Bandura, Albert (1991). Social cognitive theory of moral thought and action. In: Handbook of Moral Behavior and Development, Volume 1: Theory. Lawrence Erlbaum Associates. A theoretical study of self-regulation in moral behavior.

13. Academic development plan

Future studies will systematically distinguish social norms, moral judgment, individual freedom and responsibility.

Durkheim's, Mill's and Fromm's approaches will be compared through their original works, and page references in the editions used will be verified.

The conditions under which the proposition about moral consistency could become a researchable hypothesis will also be discussed.

This text is a conceptual study under development, not a completed peer-reviewed academic article.
