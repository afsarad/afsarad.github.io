About the author

Understanding society, questioning its causes

Adem Afşar holds bachelor's degrees in sociology, archaeology, history, and international foreign trade and logistics management. He has pursued a master's degree in sociology through the thesis stage.

He continues his research and writing on the historical development of societies, the formation of political power, relations between individuals and society, social responsibility, and the conditions of living together.

His work considers historical events not only through their outcomes, but also through the social conditions, institutional structures and human behavior that produce those outcomes.

Understanding a social phenomenon means questioning the conditions that produce an outcome, as well as observing the outcome itself.

His unpublished research and conceptual work covers migration and refugee policies; coordination between the decision makers shaping these policies and the institutions implementing them; relations between human rights and public security; social power relations in relatively closed settlements; gender perceptions across generations; and social expectations in the face of political stability.

Critical Comparison and Opposing Views

This section tests our propositions against opposing views revealing their limits, as well as ideas supporting them. EK-01 · Open discussion

Circles of Responsibility: proximity and impartiality

Our proposition
Priority of responsibility toward those close to us does not eliminate obligations toward outer circles.
Critical objection
Prioritizing by proximity may continually defer the needs of distant people with equal moral worth. Which obligation should take priority and when may also remain unclear.
Relationship to the literature
Care ethics emphasizes the moral importance of close relationships; discussions of impartiality examine tensions between personal attachments and obligations toward everyone. These approaches do not constitute a single view.
Contemporary testing
Support for family, distribution of public resources and choices of assistance during global crises should be compared using separate examples; the conditions limiting priority toward those close to us should be explained.
Source
Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy — Impartiality; Feminist Ethics.
Status
Critical comparison draft; originality and empirical validity have not yet been established.
EK-02 · Open discussion

Not a Cycle, but a Spiral: similarity and change

Our proposition
Similar historical events do not carry the same social scope in different periods.
Critical objection
Treating each event as entirely unique may overlook comparable regularities and continuities between periods. The spiral analogy alone provides no explanatory mechanism.
Relationship to the literature
Mannheim's approach to generations' historical location provides grounds for comparison; subsequent generational research emphasizes the difficulty of separating age, period and cohort effects.
Contemporary testing
Variables demonstrating continuity and those demonstrating discontinuity across similar events should be defined beforehand; the metaphor's explanatory strength should be tested through examples.
Source
Rudolph and colleagues (2021) — Generations and Generational Differences; Oxford Bibliographies — The Concept of Generations.
Status
Record of a theoretical objection; the ideas are not treated as identical.
EK-03 · Open discussion

Asabiyyah and Collective Action: the limits of solidarity

Our research question
Under what conditions does solidarity become collective action in contemporary society?
Critical objection
Solidarity does not always produce public benefit; collective action may also exclude, oppress or serve only particular groups' interests. Solidarity and organizational capacity are not the same variable.

Malešević critically compares Ibn Khaldun's and Gellner's explanations of group solidarity and discusses the ideological and coercive dimensions of organization.
Contemporary testing
Examples of collective action in digital networks, voluntary solidarity and exclusionary organization should be coded separately; purposes, means and outcomes should not be conflated.
Source
Malešević (2015) — Where does group solidarity come from? (abstract access).
Status
Detailed comparison with the source's full text is pending.

We identify not only examples supporting a proposition but also findings that would require its reconsideration. The author's intellectual propositions and field data reported in the master's thesis are not interchangeable. Similarity is not historical or conceptual identity.

Researchable question

Possible method

Conditions for reconsideration

Not a Cycle, but a Spiral

In the thesis conclusion, the overall mean attitude score is reported as 117.48 ; the generational group means are reported as 71.67for Baby Boomers, 103.45for Generation X, 119.16for Generation Y, and 121.24 for Generation Z. These are sample findings reported in the thesis; not definitive judgments about the whole of society.

The thesis conclusions state that hypotheses concerning education level and employment status were rejected because no statistically significant differences were found between the compared means. This example illustrates our principle of explicitly recording findings that do not support the expected relationship .

Methodological limitation: Generational shares in the thesis sample are unequal: Baby Boomers 2.6%; X 8.5%; Y 46.6%; Z 42.3%. Interpretation must therefore consider group sizes, sampling method and the age–period–cohort distinction. Cross-sectional comparison alone does not prove that generational membership causes attitude differences. The thesis data are not presented as directly testing the four theoretical propositions above.

Direct quotations are presented in quotation marks with work details and, where possible, page information. Summaries and interpretations are clearly distinguished. A thinker's view is not presented as though it verified our proposition.

The author is responsible for the accuracy of quotations, sources and interpretations. Bibliographic details and important claims are checked independently before publication.

Distinguishing drafts, completed texts and publications

BD-001 · Full-text outline

This text is an unsubmitted theoretical conference paper draft. Its section headings and arguments remain open to development; it cannot be presented as a completed academic article without page-level checking of original sources and author approval.

1. Introduction and research problem

In areas such as employment, social assistance, education and public administration, decision-support systems may guide human decisions at various stages. The issue is not simply whether a system makes mistakes, but who has which obligations to foresee, prevent and remedy them. The study asks: How can the responsibilities of actors participating in technical decision processes be distinguished according to their authority and opportunities to intervene?

2. Conceptual distinctions

Responsibility does not have a single meaning. Causal contribution, professional duty, legal obligation, moral duty and public accountability should not be reduced to one another. A software-generated recommendation does not automatically remove the responsibility of the person deciding to implement it or of institutional oversight mechanisms. Conversely, technical designers should not be assigned decision-making authority they do not possess.

3. Framework for the literature review

Helen Nissenbaum’s discussion of accountability in a computerized society, Virginia Eubanks’s work on the social effects of automated public decisions and Frank Pasquale’s examination of opaque algorithmic systems provide initial fields of comparison. The claims supported by these works have not yet been verified against original texts and page numbers. Accordingly, no direct quotations or verified citations are offered here.

4. Adapting the Circles of Responsibility approach

The Circles of Responsibility approach was initially developed to consider changing priorities and duties among oneself, one’s immediate circle and wider social circles. Its transfer to AI governance does not claim exact equivalence: closeness to family and institutional authority are different things. In this paper, circles will serve as a research heuristic that makes relationships between actors’ spheres of influence and duties visible.

5. Analytical assessment criteria

The proposed examination will ask five questions together: (i) Who determined the scope of the decision? (ii) Who could reasonably have foreseen its consequences? (iii) Who could intervene or stop the system? (iv) To whom can the affected person appeal? (v) Who has the resources and authority to remedy the error? These questions are not a universal legal responsibility formula, but a checking framework for comparative analysis.

6. Case-study design

The first hypothetical example may involve an algorithm ranking job applications that systematically disadvantages certain candidates. A second may involve automated risk scoring in social assistance applications. In each example, the developer, purchasing institution, implementing manager, overseer and affected person are examined separately. These are not presented as real field data or documented events; selecting concrete cases is a subsequent research stage.

7. Critical objections and the search for responses

The first objection is that distributing responsibility among many actors could allow everyone to evade accountability. This risk requires clear criteria for authority and capacity to intervene. The second is that the language of proximity-based responsibility could weaken impartiality in public services; this study discusses distinguishing types of obligation rather than favouring relatives. The third is that the theoretical scheme will not produce identical outcomes across legal systems; this limit is explicitly acknowledged.

8. Method, limits and research ethics

The study’s first stage is conceptual and normative analysis. A second stage could be designed as document-based comparative case analysis; however, no sample, dataset, interviews, fieldwork or ethics committee permission currently exists. If examples containing personal data are used, data minimization, anonymization and compliance with applicable regulations must be planned separately. This text advances no empirical findings.

9. Discussion and provisional conclusion

Discussion of social responsibility in AI-mediated decisions may remain incomplete if it focuses solely on the person giving final approval or solely on the system designer. The proposed contribution is to reveal the conditions under which different duties in a decision chain converge or diverge. Nevertheless, the approach’s explanatory power cannot be considered validated before testing against counterexamples and real documents.

An actor’s causal contributionto a decision does not by itself establish that the actor is morally blameworthy or legally responsible . 13. Three counterexamples: Where does our framework struggle?

  1. Final approval without authority: A manager clicks “approve” on screen but cannot see the system’s scoring and has no authority to reject it. Assigning all responsibility solely on the basis of the final signature is not explanatory; the chain of authority must be examined.
  2. Unforeseeable error: The developer has conducted reasonable tests; harm arises from an unforeseeable interaction outside documented limits. A causal connection alone does not establish fault; nevertheless, duties of notification and correction may still arise after the event.
  3. A gap where everyone has partial authority: The institution, developer and overseer point to one another; the affected person cannot find anyone to address. The framework cannot then be deemed to have produced a “circle of responsibility”; it requires clear channels for application and correction and identification of an ultimately accountable party.

14. The limits of Circles of Responsibility

The original approach explaining priority toward close relations does not justify privileges based on kinship or proximity in public decisions. The transfer here is solely intended to consider obligations as interrelated but differing in scope. The criterion determining institutional responsibility is not personal proximity , but duty, authority, foreseeability, capacity to intervene and effective recourse. The possibility of a conceptual break between these two uses should remain one of the study’s central objections.

15. Revision criteria and subsequent research

The framework should be narrowed or abandoned if decision-making authority and corrective duties cannot be reliably mapped to the actors in documented cases; if identical authority arrangements produce contradictory results across institutions; or if the approach offers no additional clarity over existing accountability models. The next stage will select at least two documented cases and separately verify decision flows, contracts, oversight records and appeal channels. This research has not yet been conducted.

Voluntariness, Goodwill and Fairness in the Distribution of Responsibility

Working status: A theoretical draft within AR-001 Circles of Responsibility, developed from the author’s explanations and awaiting author approval and literature comparison. It claims neither empirical validation nor peer review.

1. The source of responsibility: How it is acquired comes before its scope

Expansion of responsibility is not a uniform process. Distinguish (a) expansion voluntarily undertaken after considering one’s abilities, education, sacrifices, goals and resources; (b) a role accepted in a political organization, foundation, association or educational institution because it aligns with one’s ideals; and (c) additional burdens undertaken in response to others’ requests, expectations or repeated demands. Being offered a role, accepting it and sincerely embracing it are not the same stage.

2. Capacity and sustainability

A decision to expand responsibilities should account for existing family obligations, time, material and nonmaterial resources, health and decision-making capacity. Even a strong desire to realize an ideal does not eliminate the possibility of ill health, fatigue and impaired decision quality. New responsibilities may affect resources devoted to existing ones; this does not mean previous obligations automatically cease.

3. Unfair distribution of a burden that begins with a request

When one person is continually assigned tasks because of their goodwill and readiness to say “yes”, while the same contribution is not requested from others with similar skills, authority, resources and existing workloads, distributive justice requires scrutiny. One-off assistance should be distinguished from recurring expectations whose refusal carries a social cost. Proposition: Voluntary assistance cannot justify systematically exempting others from similar responsibilities.

5. Counterexample and limit

Two people with similar skills may have different duties, existing burdens or legal responsibilities. Apparent equality alone therefore cannot establish exploitation. Circles of Responsibility: A Measurable Research Model

1. Theoretical scope and two separate axes

Scope axis: obligations toward oneself, family, immediate circle, institutions, society and wider humanity/the environment. Acquisition axis: (A) purposefully undertaking a role by one’s own decision, (B) accepting through commitment to a shared ideal, (C) undertaking it through requests and expectations, (D) care, crisis or legal obligations arising outside one’s choice. These forms are not reducible to one another. Group D in particular is not treated as a voluntary personal choice.

3. Sample questionnaire items — unvalidated draft

Suggested responses: 1 = Strongly disagree; 2 = Disagree; 3 = Undecided; 4 = Agree; 5 = Strongly agree. Questions should be answered with reference only to a specific task experience in the past three months.

  1. I decided to undertake this task with sufficient information to assess its consequences.
  2. My own goals or the ideals I embrace were decisive in accepting this task.
  3. Refusing this task could have had negative consequences for my relationships or reputation.
  4. The same contribution was requested from others with similar resources and duties.
  5. While undertaking this task, I was able to maintain my existing family and personal obligations.
  6. I had sufficient time, skills and support resources for the task I undertook.
  7. The material or nonmaterial benefits of my contribution were shared fairly with those who provided support.
  8. Being asked to perform this task repeatedly put pressure on me.

Measurement caution: Because the items address different concepts, they cannot simply be summed to produce one “responsibility score”. Reverse coding, factor structure, reliability and validity can be assessed only after pilot administration and analysis.

4. Preliminary hypotheses and alternative explanations

  1. H1: Under similar conditions of authority and capacity, concentrating requests on a small number of helpful people may be associated with lower perceived distributive justice.
  2. H2: As the perceived social cost of refusal increases, the gap between apparently voluntary acceptance and freedom of decision may widen.
  3. H3: Shared ideals and adequate support may be associated with finding an additional role meaningful, but do not necessarily eliminate the possible adverse effects of a high workload.
  4. H4: Self-chosen expansion and externally compelled expansion may be experienced differently even at similar workload levels.

Rival explanations: differences in expertise, availability, formal role description, prior commitments, urgency and interpersonal relationships. Without measuring these, request counts alone cannot establish exploitation.

5. Proposed method and ethical limits

Stage 1: Exploratory interviews with purposive sampling from family, work, association, foundation, educational and political organization contexts; recording participants’ own definitions of responsibility and boundaries. Stage 2: Testing items through cognitive interviews and a small pilot study. Stage 3: A comparative cross-sectional study with an adequate, justified sample; longitudinal follow-up if possible. Associations do not prove causation.

Participation must be informed and voluntary; employers and party or institutional leaders must not compel participation. Sensitive information such as health and political affiliation should not be collected unless necessary; anonymization, data retention, withdrawal rights and required ethics committee/institutional permissions must be specified in advance. There are no participants, questionnaire results, statistical findings or ethics committee approvals yet.

6. Theoretical limit

This research design does not reduce the entirety of Circles of Responsibility solely to workload: the social and ecological scope of the circles is preserved. The specific subproblem made measurable here is the source, fair sharing and sustainability of expanding responsibilities. Theoretical originality, causal effects and generalizability have not yet been demonstrated.

AR-001 · Circles of Responsibility: Comparative Case Testing

Status: Theoretical work / fictional thought experiments. These cases are not actual participant accounts, observations, questionnaire findings or verified causal relationships. The author’s central argument and analytical extensions should be assessed separately.

Starting proposition

Continually asking the same individual for assistance initially accepted in goodwill, without approaching others in similar circumstances, may create a justice problem even when capacity has not been exceeded. Voluntary initiation, continuity and fair distribution are distinct dimensions here.

Comparative fictional cases

  1. Family — recurring assistance: Three adult siblings have similar time and financial resources. Only one meets their elderly parent’s weekly needs because that sibling agrees every time. Examination: Treating initial consent as valid for subsequent weeks; opportunities for the other siblings to contribute; the non-deferrable nature of care needs; rotation of the role. Counterexample: If the other siblings have invisible care burdens, the assumption of equal allocation becomes invalid.
  2. Association — treating a volunteer as a permanent organizer: A member who successfully organizes one event is expected to undertake the same task at every event; other suitable members are not approached. Examination: Clear role definition, time limits, rotation, appreciation and the relational cost of refusal. Counterexample: If the member explicitly committed to the role for a specified period, part of the expectation may be legitimate.
  3. Political organization — alignment between ideal and role: A person receives an unsolicited offer of local organizational work. They accept because of shared ideals. Examination: The external origin of the offer and the internal reason for acceptance are separate; decision-making authority, role duration, organizational support and rights to criticize and withdraw matter. Counterexample: Commitment to an ideal does not justify burnout or lack of accountability.
  4. Workplace — goodwill becomes workload: Among four employees with similar duties and competencies, only the helpful individual regularly receives extra work. Examination: Task allocation records, job descriptions, pay/returns, risks of refusal, health and decision quality. Counterexample: If that person has contractually specified additional duties or different working hours, the comparison must be reconstructed.

Rival explanations and limits

Differences in task allocation may arise from expertise, explicit commitments, preferences, trust, availability or invisible obligations. Exploitation cannot be diagnosed without examining these factors. Conversely, successful task completion alone does not prove fair allocation. Commitment to ideals and nonmaterial satisfaction can be real benefits, but they do not negate health risks or fundamental rights.

Proposed empirical test

A vignette experiment changes only one feature of the same case: (A) offering the task to all suitable people in rotation, (B) repeatedly approaching only the person who previously accepted. Participants separately rate perceived fairness, voluntariness, freedom to refuse and expected burnout risk. A second stage varies existing workload and explicit commitments to test alternative explanations. Sampling, ethics permission, scale validity and statistical analysis have not yet been determined.

Provisional conclusion: Expansion of a circle of responsibility is not a single quantity; scope, mode of acquisition, distributive justice and sustainability should be examined together. This conclusion is a theoretical proposal, not an empirically validated finding.

AR-001 · The Responsibility of Those Who Request Responsibility

Status: A conceptual examination developed from the author’s view that goodwill should not be exploited and similarly situated people should be treated fairly. The classifications below are analytical proposals, not field findings or legal judgments.

Changing the direction of the central question

“Why did the individual accept the role?” is insufficient on its own. We must also examine: “Why was this person specifically asked? Why were other suitable people not approached? How is the requester responsible for the consequences of this decision?” Goodwill must not become the sole criterion for task allocation.

A two-sided relationship of responsibility

Person undertaking the taskPerson or institution requesting the task
Assess capacity, existing burdens and conditions of acceptanceExplain the reason for the need, its scope and duration
Give voluntary consent, set boundaries and reassess when circumstances changeDo not punish refusal or treat consent as indefinite authorization
Monitor the outcomes and risks of the undertaken workConsider alternative suitable people, role rotation and support resources
Avoid commitments without necessary authority and resourcesProvide authority, time, training and resources appropriate to the role

Six questions for assessing the legitimacy of a request

  1. Necessity: Is the task truly necessary, or is it delegated for convenience?
  2. Suitability: Have the person’s competency, health, time and existing burdens been considered?
  3. Alternatives: Have others in similar circumstances been approached reasonably and fairly?
  4. Consent: Can the person refuse without fear of social, economic or institutional sanctions?
  5. Returns and support: Are material or nonmaterial contributions recognized and necessary resources provided?
  6. Review: Are the role’s duration, sharing and conditions of termination clear?

These questions form an assessment framework, not automatic scoring or a definitive diagnosis of exploitation. Circumstances such as urgent care, specialist expertise, prior commitments and legal duties may change the range of alternatives.

Distinguishing exploitation from legitimate role expectations

Possible pattern of exploitation: Requests repeatedly target the same person; similarly situated others are systematically excluded; saying “no” carries a relational cost; the role’s boundaries are unclear; the requester makes no reasonable attempt to redistribute the burden. No single sign is conclusive; the pattern must be considered as a whole.

Possibility of legitimate differentiation: A distribution that appears unequal may be justified by specialist expertise, an explicit time-limited commitment, role authority or others’ invisible obligations. The justification must nonetheless be transparent and proportionate.

Two comparative thought experiments

Association: At an event with five suitable members, the coordinator always calls the most helpful member. The same task is never offered to others. Even if the member performs successfully, the process may be unfair. The coordinator is responsible not merely for completing the event but for the procedure of sharing the task.

Urgent care: An elderly family member urgently needs help; only one sibling is reachable at that moment. That person’s temporary assumption of the role does not automatically justify leaving all long-term care to them. Immediate necessity and permanent allocation must be assessed separately.

Proposed research design

Vignettes may systematically vary the requesting actor (friend/manager/institution), the availability of suitable alternatives (absent/present), frequency (once/repeatedly), the cost of refusal (absent/present) and role duration (clear/uncertain). Participants may separately assess perceived fairness, voluntariness, exploitation, requester responsibility and willingness to accept the role. Sampling, scale validity, ethics approval requirements and the analysis plan must be determined before data collection.

Theoretical limit

This approach does not make all responsibilities depend solely on consent: non-elective obligations may arise in childcare, contracts, public office or prevention of imminent harm. Moreover, “the pleasure of realizing an ideal” may motivate voluntary acceptance but does not remove the requester’s obligation of fair distribution. These propositions have not yet been empirically validated.

AR-001 · Limiting Responsibility and Withdrawing from a Role

Status: A conceptual draft developed from the author’s views on capacity, free will, misuse of goodwill and fair task allocation. It is not legal opinion, verified field findings or a universal moral rule.

Expansion and contraction are not opposite directions of the same decision

A person’s voluntary expansion of their circle of responsibility does not show that they will carry the same burden indefinitely even if circumstances change. Reducing responsibility must nevertheless be assessed differently depending on its source. A voluntary role, contractual duty, legal obligation and urgent care need cannot be equated.

Three separate questions

  1. Undertaking: With what degree of choice, information and under what conditions was the role accepted?
  2. Continuation: Are the person’s health, time, skills and resources still adequate today in light of obligations to their immediate circle?
  3. Withdrawal: Who would be affected by leaving the role, and are reasonable transitional steps available to reduce harm?

Six criteria for assessing withdrawal

  1. Changing capacity: Have health, family circumstances, income, time or skills changed significantly?
  2. Nature of the commitment: Is there an explicit duration, role description, contract or legal duty?
  3. Dependency and harm: Who would be affected by sudden withdrawal, to what extent and for how long?
  4. Alternatives: Is sharing, reduction, postponement, support or transfer of work possible?
  5. Notice and transition: Are reasonable notice, transfer of records and a transition period possible?
  6. Fairness: Does keeping the role with the same person continually obscure the responsibilities of others in similar circumstances?

Three fictional comparisons

Association: A person who has voluntarily run events for two years wants to stop because of health and family burdens. Management pressures them by saying, “If you don’t do it, nobody will.” Analytical question: Can the institution’s failure to train a replacement become an indefinite obligation for the volunteer to continue?

Political organization: A person accepts a role because it aligns with their ideals; the decision-making arrangements subsequently change and the role expands. Analytical question: Does initial consent automatically cover changed duties and purposes? How should agreement and renewed consent be assessed?

Care: The health of someone caring for a relative deteriorates. Analytical question: How can support, shared responsibility and safe alternatives be established to maintain care without exhausting the individual? The dependant’s rights cannot be disregarded here.

Propositions and testable expectations

P1: As a role’s scope exceeds the initial agreement, absence of opportunities for renewed consent may be associated with perceived pressure. P2: Participation in task sharing by people with similar capacities may reduce the sense of injustice accumulating in one individual. P3: Predefined handover and notice procedures may reduce risks of harm both to the person withdrawing and to those benefiting from the role. These are untested hypotheses, not evidence of causation.

Theoretical limit and ethical caution

A “right to withdraw” is not an absolute and unconditional power to abandon others; additional obligations may exist, particularly concerning children, care-dependent people, safety-critical roles and legal commitments. Sacrifice, on the other hand, is not unlimited either. The model proposes a context-sensitive assessment balancing protection of individual capacity and reduction of foreseeable harm to others . The legal consequences of each case require separate investigation.

Next research step: A vignette design comparing “predefined duration / indefinite duration” and “safe handover plan present / absent” for the same role. No participant data have been collected.

AR-001 · Solidarity, Loss of Capacity and Remembering Sacrifice Within the Family Circle

Status: A compilation of the conceptual contributions completed by the author at this stage. The propositions below are not empirically validated findings.

1. Distinguishing the family from other circles of responsibility

Within the family, the narrowest circle of responsibility, the starting point for explaining sacrifice in times of need is not suspicion of exploited goodwill. Family members may take on responsibility by seeing one another as parts of a shared whole. This approach does not claim that misuse within families is impossible; it simply requires that inquiries into task allocation and exploitation, more prominent in subsequent circles, are not automatically transferred to family relations.

2. “Game over”: Responsibility changes direction when capacity is lost

Illness or loss of economic resources may prevent one member from maintaining previous contributions. The relationship of responsibility does not then disappear; other family members may undertake additional burdens alongside their own responsibilities. The form of this redistribution relates to family members’ actual capacities and needs. “Game over” here does not mean that the individual’s worth or social belonging ends.

3. The future is not yet known when a sacrifice is made

Contributing to the family when a need arises may be seen by the individual as an unavoidable responsibility and accepted willingly. At that moment, family members’ future educational, occupational or economic circumstances are not yet determined. Past behaviour should therefore not be read as a pre-calculated claim based on differing opportunities that will emerge in the future.

4. Speaking about sacrifice afterwards

When family members establish separate lives, past sacrifices may be discussed; this does not necessarily mean demanding compensation or additional rights. A person may recall a contribution when they feel its full value has not been recognized. Here, feeling unappreciated differs from actually being unappreciated. Later accounts of sacrifice do not, on their own, invalidate its initial voluntariness.

5. Diverging opportunities and perceptions of opportunity

One family member continuing education while another works does not by itself show that one was forced to abandon an opportunity. Education may seem difficult and work may be willingly chosen. Alternatively, unexpected family responsibilities may have limited education or other opportunities. Past contributions may be interpreted as “missed opportunities” when family members’ circumstances subsequently diverge; objectively verifying this interpretation is a separate research question.

6. Pretext, reason and subjective meaning

In the distinction proposed by the author, a pretextrelates to an explanation consciously constructed in anticipation of future social expectations of success, whereas a reason relates to shouldering an unplanned family responsibility. Life histories that appear similar from outside may involve different initial intentions. Frequently recounting one’s sacrifice does not by itself prove conscious construction of a pretext.

Theoretical limit and subsequent work

This section distinguishes sacrifice as experienced at the time from its interpretation years later, rather than calculating sacrifices within families. Future empirical studies should treat consent, necessity, perceived appreciation, diverging opportunities and the purpose of retelling as separate variables. The author’s contributions on this issue have been compiled in this examination, allowing other research topics to be addressed without awaiting additional comments from the author.
