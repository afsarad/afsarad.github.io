The Purpose of Life: God’s Pleasure, Hope, Sincerity and Surrender

An extended sociological reading of the Risale-i Nur Collection

Adem Afşar · 22 November 2026

The ultimate ideal is God’s pleasure; hope is a sincere wish, sincerity guides conduct, and surrender concerns the outcome.

Reading framework: These 15 sections are concise sociological studies open to further development, not completed chapters of a full-length monograph. The religious explanations of Bediüzzaman Said Nursi are treated as primary-source material; conclusions about hope, humility, social belonging and responsibility are presented as Adem Afşar’s interpretations. “Circles of responsibility” and “Human beings remain human everywhere and at all times” are used here as Adem Afşar’s conceptual propositions, without claiming that they were first advanced in the literature or empirically verified through fieldwork. References to works and sections do not substitute for verified, page-specific direct quotations.

1. A lifetime as a form of capital

Life is limited, yet questions about how to use it seem endless. Living, earning a livelihood and caring for loved ones are indispensable. Beyond them, a person may ask which purpose gives life its direction.

Context of the primary text: In the First Remedy of the Twenty-Fifth Lem’a, Bediüzzaman Said Nursi likens life to capital. Yet the passage is not a general lesson in time management: it belongs to a work offering spiritual consolation and patience to people experiencing illness. The Third Remedy also discusses life’s transience alongside illness and the afterlife. This religious setting must remain visible.

Adem Afşar’s sociological interpretation: Thinking of life as capital concerns more than the efficient use of an individual’s time. People devote time to family, work, learning and helping others, thereby sustaining relationships and social responsibilities. Yet control over time is not equally distributed: care obligations, working conditions, health and economic resources shape the range of choices available. Measuring a lifetime solely by visible achievement may therefore overlook such inequalities. This paragraph is a sociological reading prompted by the source, not a claim made directly by the source text.

Source boundary: Twenty-Fifth Lem’a, First and Third Remedies. The source is summarised in context rather than quoted verbatim.

Reading across the collection: an initial source map

Sözler: The Eleventh Word presents nine purposes of human life, resisting any reduction of life’s meaning to duration or productivity alone. Gratitude, reflection, knowledge of God and worship form part of this framework. The same work contains parables about the use of entrusted capital; each must be read in its own context.

Lem’alar: The Twenty-Fifth Lem’a considers the spiritual significance of time amid illness and adversity. Comparing the lives of healthy and ill people only by productivity would narrow the source’s purpose.

Asâ-yı Mûsâ: The scarcity of life’s capital and the abundance of necessary tasks raise the question of where people direct their attention and effort. The passage’s historical and religious setting must not be equated directly with a modern critique of news consumption.

Mesnevî-i Nuriye: Images of life passing rapidly complement the question of how awareness of transience informs human conduct.

Tarihçe-i Hayat: Accounts of lives dedicated to religious service allow study of how ideas are lived within a community. The narrator’s or students’ assessments must, however, be distinguished from Bediüzzaman Said Nursi’s own statements.

Research status: This is an initial cross-text source map, not a completed survey of the entire collection. Mektubat, Şualar, the letters and the remaining works still require systematic examination. Edition and page references must be verified individually.

2. The ultimate ideal: God’s pleasure

Adopting God’s pleasure as life’s ultimate ideal does not require withdrawing from worldly duties. Honesty in trade, compassion in family life, justice in public affairs and sincerity in learning may become different expressions of one moral orientation. A religious text’s claim about faith and the way that claim is lived in everyday relationships must be studied on distinct levels.

3. Sincerity: appearing and being

The Twenty-First Lem’a places sincerity at the centre of religious service and discusses how rivalry, material interests and the desire for recognition may damage it. That an act of help is seen does not prove it was performed for display. Equally, an unseen good deed does not establish intention with certainty. Sociology studies observable conduct; it cannot measure the heart with absolute certainty.

4. Not expecting a reward versus not making it the goal

Trying not to expect a reward differs from reaching a point where reward is no longer the purpose of conduct. The peace one feels after helping is not necessarily the reason for helping. Hope for paradise and prioritising God’s pleasure need not exclude one another; the ideal examined here is not turning goodness into a ledger of debts.

5. Hope: sincerity and the wish for a favourable outcome

In Adem Afşar’s original proposition, hope is the sincere and humble expectation of a favourable outcome. A person is free to ask, but asking does not make an outcome inevitable. Such hope distinguishes desire from a claim to certainty. Wanting a hoped-for outcome does not mean treating it as something owed.

6. Surrender: accepting every outcome

A second dimension of hope is acknowledging that the final outcome is not under human control. Surrender does not mean abandoning one’s duties; it means giving up the claim to absolute control after doing what one can. A person prays, works and takes precautions without claiming to determine the final result.

7. The difference between limitation and worthlessness

Acz, recognising one’s limited power, does not imply human worthlessness. A person acknowledges limits of knowledge, time and ability. Accepting such limits may create room for learning and solidarity. It may become easier to ask for help, offer help and acknowledge mistakes.

8. Subtle forms of egoism

A person may enjoy recognition even while helping someone else. Membership in a religious community does not remove every ordinary human tendency. The Twenty-First Lem’a’s warnings about fame and status are sociologically significant for this reason. The issue is not that people can feel pleasure but that pleasure may become a concealed measure of conduct.

9. Humility: performance or moral commitment?

When humility becomes merely a visible role, it may turn into another way of gaining approval. Someone may be praised for humility and take pleasure in that praise. This possibility does not invalidate humility; it shows why the relation between intention and action requires continued reflection. Sincerity cannot be measured only by how others see us.

10. Trust in God and the duty to act

Surrender does not require accepting injustice. Protecting a child, seeking medical care, asking for fair wages and demanding justice are responsible actions. Equating trust in God with passivity may conceal human duties. Leaving the outcome to God does not eliminate the need to use available means.

11. Circles of responsibility and equal human worth

A person may have direct duties of care towards those nearby while also recognising moral obligations towards distant people. The forms of responsibility differ; human worth does not. A practical duty to feed a nearby child does not mean a distant child’s life is less valuable. This distinction prevents compassion from being confined to group membership.

12. Community, belonging and questioning

A community may offer trust, solidarity and shared meaning. Yet belonging should not depend on silencing questions. Religious teachings and community practices must be distinguished: a text’s call to sincerity does not prove that everyone who reads it acts sincerely. Claims about community behaviour require separate observation and research.

13. Human beings remain human everywhere and at all times

Adem Afşar’s proposition does not claim that everyone behaves identically in every circumstance. It highlights recurring tendencies—self-interest, fear, hope, the wish for recognition and solidarity—across different eras and institutions. Religious ideals therefore do not automatically become social conduct; they must be translated into action repeatedly.

14. A daily account of one’s lifetime

One may ask: Did I protect someone’s rights today? Did I try to learn something I did not know? Did I turn my good deed into a claim of superiority? Did I correct a mistake? When my hoped-for result did not occur, did I continue to fulfil my responsibilities? Such questions do not deliver certainty about the heart; they sustain moral self-examination.

15. Conclusion: hope is an orientation, not a debt

The value of a lifetime cannot be measured only by success, visibility and reward. The ideal of gaining God’s pleasure may deepen responsibility towards oneself and society. Hope is a sincere wish, sincerity is a continuing moral effort, and surrender relinquishes any claim of absolute entitlement to an outcome. A person does what is required, preserves hope and leaves final judgement to God.

Sources and method

Primary sources: Bediüzzaman Said Nursi, Lem’alar, Twenty-First Lem’a (sincerity, rivalry, reputation); Twenty-Fifth Lem’a (life as a limited capital); Şualar, Fourteenth Şua, Fourth Matter (responsibility and limited time); Mektubat, Twenty-Second Mektup (brotherhood). These are references to works and sections rather than claims about a particular printed pagination. No unverified verbatim quotation is reproduced.

Method: This study distinguishes the religious teachings in the works of Bediüzzaman Said Nursi, Adem Afşar’s sociological analyses of those teachings, and the original concepts and propositions he develops from his own observations and reflections. It does not claim to have conducted direct field research on religious communities.
