A Discussion of Meaning and Authority through Risale-i Nur

If these two meanings are not distinguished, one side’s expression of respect may be understood by the other as a claim to a new sacred book. Yet we cannot assume that an expression of respect can never develop into an excessive claim to authority. We need first to examine what the words mean, and then the position given to the work.

In approaching this question through Risale-i Nur, consulting Bediüzzaman Said Nursi’s own statements can provide firmer ground for both defenders and critics of the work.

1. Does the completion of prophethood mean the completion of understanding?

In Islamic belief, the finality of the prophethood of Muhammad (peace and blessings be upon him) is a fundamental conviction; verse 40 of Sūrat al-Aḥzāb is among its explicit foundations.[A] Yet the end of prophethood and the end of people’s reflection, learning and explanation concerning the Qur’an are different things.

We must distinguish the source of religion from the human activity of seeking to understand that source. The existence of a later commentary does not, by itself, constitute a claim to new revelation or a new sacred book. The central question is how the work positions itself and what authority readers give it.

It should also be remembered that the Qur’anic root translated as revelation does not mean the communication of a book to prophets in every context. In its explanation of al-Naḥl 16:68, Diyanet’s Kur’an Yolu treats the communication to the honeybee as inspiration in the sense of abilities and dispositions given to a living creature. In its commentary on al-Qaṣaṣ 28:7, the communication to Moses’s mother is described as probably referring to inspiration.[B]

These examples do not prove that every claim to inspiration is true. They show that treating inspiration and prophetic revelation as identical from the outset is unwarranted.

2. The Collection’s own statement: inspiration and interpretation

In the footnote to the Eighth Verse section of the First Şuâ in Şuâlar, we find the following statement, rendered here in English:

That is, the two differences indicate its rank. Risale-i Nur is not revelation, but inspiration and the derivation of meanings.

In the footnote to the Twenty-First Verse or Verses section of the same work, the distinction is stated again:

Source passage 2 · Read on this page

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · English rendering of the Turkish source · Work and page reference

It is not revelation and cannot be. Rather, it is inspiration and the derivation of meanings.

İstihrac should here be understood as drawing meanings and conclusions from a text. The expression distinguishes developing explanations on the basis of the Qur’an from placing a new book beside it at the same rank.

The context of these footnotes also matters. The First Şuâ discusses allusive interpretations and interpretations based on abjad or numerical letter values, according to which Qur’anic verses point to Risale-i Nur. The validity of these interpretive methods can be discussed separately. Whether interpretations are contested and whether a work defines itself as revelation are different questions. The statements examined here draw an explicit boundary concerning the latter.

A reading that elevates the Collection to the rank of revelation should therefore be confronted with these distinctions within it. Equally, reading every mention of inspiration as a claim to new prophethood must take the distinction into account.

3. Inspiration and human understanding

In the Seventh Şuâ of Şuâlar, known as Âyetü’l-Kübrâ, Bediüzzaman Said Nursi identifies two differences between revelation and inspiration. The second is explained as follows, in our English rendering:

Source passage 3 · Read on this page

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · English rendering of the Turkish source · Work and page reference

Revelation is without shadow, pure, and particular to the elect. Inspiration, however, has shadows; colours mingle with it, and it is general. Like the inspirations of angels, humans and animals, it has many kinds and numerous varieties, providing a ground for the multiplication of divine words as numerous as the drops of the seas.

The phrase referring to the elect denotes a particular group. The expression about shadows and mingling colours suggests the relationship between inspiration and the recipient’s capacities and circumstances. The preceding discussion also emphasises a particular relationship according to the recipient’s capacity.

We can develop the following interpretation from this passage: believing in divine assistance does not require identifying human understanding with divine knowledge. Someone’s belief that they receive inspiration and a claim that their understanding and expression of it are beyond discussion for everyone are different claims.

The article “İlham” in the TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi also presents differences of opinion within Islamic thought. Some scholars accord inspiration epistemic value, while many theologians do not accept it as conclusive evidence for religious rulings that bind everyone.[C] Belief in the existence of inspiration and the conversion of a claim to inspiration into binding authority over others therefore require separate consideration.

4. An explicit warning against calling inspiration a Qur’anic verse

The following warning appears in the Fourth Telvih of Telvîhât-ı Tis‘a, the Ninth Part of the Twenty-Ninth Mektub in Mektubât. This is our English rendering:

Source passage 4 · Read on this page

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · English rendering of the Turkish source · Work and page reference

One of the most dangerous errors of this kind is to imagine the particular meanings that come to one’s heart in an inspired manner to be the Word of Allah, and to call them verses.

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi goes on to describe applying the name particular to Qur’anic verses to such inspirations as an error. The passage concerns mistaken assessments of one’s spiritual station and of meanings experienced in certain spiritual states.

This warning makes the issue more than an external criticism. Within the Collection itself, there is a boundary against raising inspiration beyond its rank to the position of a Qur’anic verse.

A reader’s love for the work cannot therefore justify exceeding the boundary the work draws. Recognising this boundary can also help critics assess what the text actually says.

5. What does writing with Allah’s assistance establish?

In a defence contained in Emirdağ Lâhikası-II, expressions such as “it was intimated to the heart” and “it occurred to me” are discussed. We read, in our English rendering:

Source passage 5 · Read on this page

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · English rendering of the Turkish source · Work and page reference

What I mean by saying this is: ‘It is not my skill or my intelligence. It means something in the nature of thoughts arising in the mind. This is an expression like those everyone uses.’

Sünuhat means meanings or thoughts arising in the mind or heart. The explanation involves moving away from a claim to personal accomplishment and understanding the arising meaning within grace.

A believer may attribute success in an undertaking to Allah’s assistance. Yet believing that all events occur within Allah’s will and creation is different from claiming that every human utterance is thereby authenticated by Allah. An action’s occurrence does not in itself establish its truth or moral approval.

This distinction is particularly necessary with strong and potentially ambiguous expressions such as “Allah caused it to be written”. We must clarify whether the speaker means divine assistance, inspiration, or a new communication claimed to be protected from error and binding on everyone.

Before clarifying these meanings, assigning all readers the same belief claim, or assuming that membership alone reveals someone’s intention and creed, narrows the discussion. A serious theological judgement such as shirk, associating partners with Allah, should not take the place of clarifying an ambiguous statement.

6. The Qur’an’s comprehensiveness and the responsibility to learn

Thinking about the Qur’an’s guidance encompassing past and future life alongside the effort to understand the universe is the second dimension of this discussion. At the beginning of the Second Station of the Twentieth Söz in Sözler, Bediüzzaman Said Nursi writes, in our English rendering:

Source passage 6 · Read on this page

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · English rendering of the Turkish source · Work and page reference

According to one view, the Clear Book is the Qur’an. This noble verse states that everything, wet and dry, is within it. Is that so? Yes, everything is within it. But not everyone can see everything within it, for things are present at different levels. Sometimes their seeds, sometimes their kernels, sometimes their summaries, sometimes their principles, sometimes their signs are present: explicitly, allusively, symbolically, obscurely, or by way of a reminder.

The opening qualification, “according to one view”, must be retained: Bediüzzaman Said Nursi is proceeding from a particular interpretation. The following discussion stresses expression appropriate to the Qur’an’s purpose, the need at hand and the context.

It would not be an accurate account to turn this approach into a claim that all future scientific results are explicitly written in the Qur’an in technical detail. The passage itself distinguishes levels such as explicit statement, principle, seed and indication.

Believing that the Qur’an addresses all times does not necessarily imply that human inquiry is no longer needed. On the contrary, one may consider the development of human knowledge and experience necessary to understand the questions the text opens under new circumstances. This final connection is the interpretation developed here.

7. Reading the universe and learning the sciences

In the First Part of the Introduction to the Twenty-Fifth Söz, Bediüzzaman Said Nursi defines the Qur’an with the following expression, rendered here in English:

Source passage 7 · Read on this page

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · English rendering of the Turkish source · Work and page reference

The Qur’an is an eternal translation of this great book of the universe.

Kitab-ı Kebir-i Kâinat means the great book of the universe. The expression establishes a relationship of meaning between the Qur’an and the created world. In such a reading, reflection on the created world is not excluded from religious contemplation.

In the Second Station of the Twentieth Söz, we also read, in our English rendering:

Source passage 8 · Read on this page

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · English rendering of the Turkish source · Work and page reference

Every perfection, every science, every advancement and every branch of knowledge has an elevated truth, and that truth rests upon a divine name.

Examples of geometry and medicine follow. Further on, the role of knowledge in human life is expressed as follows:

Source passage 9 · Read on this page

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · English rendering of the Turkish source · Work and page reference

Certainly, in later times, humankind will turn to the sciences and branches of knowledge. It will draw all its strength from knowledge. Judgement and power will pass into the hands of knowledge.

This is a religious conception of knowledge and progress. A reflection we can develop from it today is that learning the disciplines which examine the universe and human beings can proceed alongside faith. The Qur’anic references to signs shown in the world and within human beings, and the invitation to travel through the earth and consider creation, can also be considered in this connection: Fuṣṣilat 41:53 and al-ʿAnkabūt 29:20.[D]

I express the idea that “the universe is the unfolding of the Qur’an” in this essay as follows: Qur’anic guidance and inquiry into the created world can nourish one another. Studying the universe, human beings and society may deepen a person’s relationship of meaning with the verses.

This is my interpretation; it cannot be presented as a literal translation of a verse or a definition agreed upon by all commentators. Nor should the refutation of a particular scientific interpretation be treated as the refutation of the Qur’an. Those who interpret are responsible for their own explanations.

Learning physics requires measurement and experiment, understanding history requires source criticism, and examining society requires suitable research methods. Seeing the universe as a book can make recourse to these methods meaningful; it does not replace them.

8. The sociological issue: meanings acquired by the same expression in different settings

Within a community, “written through inspiration” may express confidence in a work and respect for its author. In another setting, the same expression may be heard as a claim to unquestionable authority. These are possibilities requiring investigation, rather than established characteristics shared by all community members or all critics.

Sociological inquiry can ask concrete questions: can readers ask about a statement they do not understand? Are premature judgements made about their faith when they disagree with an interpretation? Does respect for the text obstruct requests for explanation? Is criticism constructed by attributing a claim to someone that they did not make?

Seeking to understand spiritual experience and questioning the grounds of religious and intellectual claims can proceed together. Bediüzzaman Said Nursi’s distinctions between inspiration, the derivation of meanings and revelation provide a textual basis for this conversation.

Believing that the Qur’an is the final revealed book does not contradict continuing to reflect on it. Yet the distinction between the explanations produced through reflection and revelation itself must be preserved. People may value their understanding while recognising the activity of understanding as their own.

Brief glossary

  • İlham: In this discussion, a meaning believed to arise in the heart or mind through divine assistance.
  • Vahiy: In this essay’s comparison, divine communication to a prophet which is a source of religion. Wider Qur’anic uses of the word are explained separately.
  • İstihrac: Drawing meanings and conclusions from a text or evidence.
  • Sünuhat: Meanings or thoughts arising in the mind or heart.
  • Hâşiye: An explanatory addition or footnote.
  • Allusive interpretation: An interpretation that understands a text to contain indications beyond its explicit meaning.
  • Cifir / abjad: Methods of establishing relationships of meaning through the numerical values of letters.
  • Cüz’î: Particular, limited, relating to a specific part or matter.
  • Havass: An elect or particular group.
  • İcmal: A concise account without entering into detail.
  • Düstur: A principle or foundational rule.
  • Sarahaten: Explicitly.
  • Remzen: Through indication or symbolism.
  • Kitab-ı Kebir-i Kâinat: The great book of the universe.
  • Ulûm ve fünun: Sciences and fields of knowledge or art.

This essay does not seek to pronounce theological judgements on individuals or communities. It examines distinctions between text, interpretation and claims to authority.
