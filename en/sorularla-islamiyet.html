Independent book project

Islam Through Questions

Faith, meaning and living together

Adem Afşar

Work in progress

A question-and-answer book exploring Islam through the questions of everyday life, in language accessible to everyone. It examines, through concrete examples, how faith can contribute to a person’s inner life, responsibilities and social solidarity.

What question gave rise to this book?

Is Islam solely a matter of fitting daily conduct into prescribed patterns? Or does the bond of meaning between divine address and each person’s particular qualities, opportunities and responsibilities also belong at the centre of life? This study approaches these questions from belief in God’s unlimited power.

The idea explored by the author is that the Qur’an can convey a message encompassing each individual’s unique qualities and responsibilities. Awareness that the meaning a person develops binds that person is examined as a basis for living with respect for shared rights, without compelling others to think alike.

The book’s subject areas

Believing and understanding

Faith, doubt, free will, responsibility and the human search for meaning.

Encountering the Qur’an

Encountering divine address in a language one understands, close translation, personal meaning and access to knowledge.

A comprehensive guide to religious practice

Basic religious knowledge; cleanliness, worship and practical questions of daily life.

Modern habits and ethics

Consumption, digital life, work, honesty, trust and the rights of others.

Women, men and family

Biological differences, equal human dignity, rights, responsibilities and sharing the burdens of life together.

Religion and society

Solidarity, social order, opinion leaders and understanding apparently contradictory behaviour.

Difficult questions and new issues

Chaos, justice, differences of interpretation and faith amid changing conditions of life.

How are questions and answers approached?

The Risale-i Nur Collection is a principal reference, particularly in exploring questions of faith and meaning. The explanations of Bediüzzaman Said Nursi are discussed in plain language, identifying their context and location in the work. Qur’anic verses, hadith, explanations from the collection and the author’s own assessments are distinguished from one another.

Answers concerning religious practice draw on verifiable sources of Islamic jurisprudence; differences of opinion are explained where relevant. A ruling not directly present in Risale-i Nur is not attributed to the collection.

Faith in everyday life

The book asks how faith can find positive expression in honest trade, care for what is entrusted to us, solidarity in difficult times and personal accountability. It aims to show how these contributions take shape through everyday conduct.

7. How can asking a question be distinguished from rejecting faith

The fact that a person has a question does not, by itself, explain what that person believes. First, we need to understand what the question concerns. Not knowing the meaning of a word, finding an explanation inadequate and making a claim about God’s existence are different situations. Responding to them all in the same way may prevent us from hearing what the person is saying.

The need to learn is a starting point

In the Twenty-Third Word of Sözler, Bediüzzaman Said Nursi discusses the human need to learn as a lifelong characteristic. The Fourth Point of the First Discussion emphasises developing through knowledge and prayer. Learning appears here alongside recognising oneself and the opportunities one has been given, and reflecting on one’s relationship with God. This explanation should be read within the text’s framework of faith and worship. [R1]

Drawing on this framework, we can ask a question about our own lives: when we say that we do not understand, are we seeking an opportunity to learn, or trying to assert a superiority that silences the other person? The same care applies to whoever answers. Becoming angry at a question does not answer it. Explaining the point again, identifying the source and acknowledging what one does not know can help inquiry move forward.

Identifying precisely what the question concerns

Suppose a young person says they cannot find the source of a religious statement they have heard. The first task is to investigate where the statement appears. The reputation of the person who passed it on cannot replace the source. Someone else may have noticed different words in two translations. In that case, we can examine the context in which the word is used and the reasons for the translation choices. Both examples involve a specific question of information that needs clarification.

It also matters that people describe their own questions accurately. Being able to say, “I have not yet understood this expression,” helps a person continue studying without reaching a hasty conclusion about the whole text. The listener should not add an unspoken intention to that sentence. Rather than making a definitive judgement about another person’s inner life, we can assess the words spoken and the reasons offered.

How does meaning unique to a person affect inquiry

The idea I explore in this book is that divine address can encompass each human being’s unique qualities and responsibilities. I do not present this idea as a direct ruling from the Risale-i Nur passage cited here. I ask it as my own proposal: might the same words speak to a responsibility in our lives that only we know about?

A person who experiences such an encounter can take it seriously while distinguishing what the text says from the meaning formed within their own life. Saying, “This reading awakened this responsibility in me,” differs from demanding that everyone accept the same conclusion. Investigating one’s own meaning does not confer authority to govern someone else’s conscience.

What can this care contribute to life together

In a family or circle of friends where questions can be discussed, people may feel less need to hide what they do not know. They can investigate something they have misheard or reconsider an explanation they memorised. Within such relationships, faith can encourage patience, truthfulness and listening without causing hurt. The effort to learn can thus also contribute to greater trust between people.

This answer proposes addressing the substance of a question. Recognising the human need to learn can be considered alongside belief in God’s unlimited power. Not yet understanding an explanation does not have to mark the end of inquiry.

8. Does belief in divine decree remove human choice and responsibility

Divine decree should not be turned into an explanation that allows people to evade responsibility for their actions. In the Twenty-Sixth Word, Bediüzzaman Said Nursi considers divine decree together with the human capacity to choose. In this account, decree counters the pride of claiming all goodness entirely for oneself, while the capacity to choose counters evasion of responsibility for wrongdoing. [R2]

Where does the capacity to choose appear in everyday life

When people discover that an object entrusted to them has been damaged, they may consider telling its owner or concealing the damage. They also know their decision will affect another person’s trust. Being unable to determine every circumstance does not make their words and conduct insignificant. In this example, responsibility is considered alongside what the person knows and what they are able to do.

The expression “cüz’-i ihtiyarî” in Risale-i Nur is explained in this answer as the limited human capacity to choose. Human beings do not possess unlimited power to determine everything. Yet it does not follow that their choices have no significance. The First Discussion of the Twenty-Sixth Word explicitly criticises invoking decree to escape responsibility. [R2]

Is God’s knowing the same as a person being compelled

In the Second Discussion of the Twenty-Sixth Word, Bediüzzaman Said Nursi examines the relationship between God’s knowledge and human choice. His explanation distinguishes something being known from its being compelled. He also cautions against treating divine eternity as a very distant starting point in the past, discussing instead the encompassing of past, present and future together. This is a brief summary of the work’s approach. [R3]

Firm belief in God’s unlimited power and knowledge does not mean that we know every cause of an event. Saying, “It was going to happen anyway,” therefore does not provide a sound reason to abandon an action we have not yet undertaken or close the account on a wrong we have committed. We continue to assess the conduct before us with the information and opportunities available to us.

How does trust in God affect effort

In its discussion of trust in God, or “tevekkül”, the Twenty-Third Word of Sözler explains that causes and practical means should not be abandoned altogether. Resorting to means is considered within the framework of prayer through action. Recognising the outcome as coming from God is considered together with doing what can be done. [R4] This approach offers a measure against calling passive waiting and evasion of responsibility trust in God.

Consider someone applying for a job. They prepare, submit their application on time and work on areas they need to improve. Not every factor determining the result is within their control. If the outcome is disappointing, they can keep working without making this single event the sole measure of their worth. This everyday example illustrates how effort and trust can be considered together without claiming to guarantee a result.

We cannot infer hidden guilt from another person’s suffering

When discussing divine decree, we should not treat someone’s hardship as proof of an unknown fault. We cannot claim to know all the unseen causes of an event. Understanding the needs of someone in pain, working to remedy injustice where it exists and showing solidarity are responsibilities concerning our own conduct. This is a principle of the approach I adopt here.

Likewise, not every obstacle a person faces can be explained solely by a lack of individual effort. Deprivation, coercion by others and unfair practices can narrow a person’s options. These conditions also need investigation when we discuss choice. Responsibility should not be turned into a means of immediately blaming those who have suffered harm.

How can faith contribute to shared life

People who seek to recognise their own part in events can work to correct a wrong rather than conceal it behind the language of decree. When successful, they can remember the opportunities available to them and the contributions of others alongside their own effort. This attitude can help people recognise others’ labour at work, share burdens within a family and assist their neighbours.

This answer does not treat belief in divine decree as a conclusion that leaves people inactive. Drawing on the relationship between responsibility and trust set out in the source texts, it considers doing what can be done, accepting one’s share of responsibility for wrongdoing and recognising that no individual controls every outcome alone.

9. How can prayer trust in God and work come together in a person’s life

People can pray for an outcome while also working towards it. Investigating what they can do can be considered alongside trusting God. The central issue is recognising which actions are their responsibility and accepting that they cannot determine every outcome alone.

The connection between asking and making an effort

In the Fifth Point of the First Discussion of the Twenty-Third Word, Bediüzzaman Said Nursi examines different aspects of prayer. He discusses taking the available means as prayer through action, giving the example of ploughing the soil. In this account, a person’s work is understood as an orientation towards the outcome sought. Prayer with the tongue and heart is discussed separately. Opening one’s needs to God is considered alongside awareness that He hears the person. [R5]

People who want to grow produce in a garden prepare the soil, sow at a suitable time and continue caring for the plants. Doing so does not mean they have brought about every condition of growth. Their effort has a real part to play, but not everything is under their control. This everyday example can help us reflect on the relationship between work and prayer in the text. It contains no promise that a particular crop will necessarily be obtained.

Where does trust in God belong within responsibility

The Third Point of the same Word explains that trusting God does not mean abandoning the available means altogether. Taking those means and acknowledging the outcome as coming from God are discussed together. [R4] This approach connects a person’s effort with trust. While seeking to recognise what they can do, people need not regard the entire burden as theirs alone.

When craftspeople discover a defect in their work, they can explain the situation and investigate how to correct it. Following up on their promise to a customer and not concealing a known deficiency concern their own conduct. Saying, “I prayed,” does not answer whether those actions were taken. Faith can support care in one’s work and respect for another person’s rights. This example is my assessment of everyday life, rather than a direct statement from the source text.

Must the response to prayer be exactly what we request

In the Fifth Point, Bediüzzaman Said Nursi distinguishes a prayer being answered from the exact thing requested being given. He considers the fulfilment of a person’s request within the framework of God’s wisdom. Prayer is explained as a relationship of worship that cannot be reduced solely to obtaining a particular worldly outcome. [R5] This approach should not be presented as a promise that every wish will come true at the time we specify.

We should also take seriously the feelings of someone who has not obtained the outcome they wanted. They may feel sorrow, reconsider their situation or pause to regain their strength. An unfulfilled outcome does not allow us to claim knowledge that this person did not believe sufficiently. Instead of using our own interpretation to pass judgement on their inner life, we can listen to their difficulty and explore what support they need.

How can this understanding affect shared life

People who connect work with responsibility can also recognise the labour of those working alongside them. They may see that a product does not come into being through their effort alone. Remembering those who prepared the materials, shared knowledge and helped carry out the work can contribute to fairness. Gratitude can then extend beyond words to thanking people and giving labour its due.

Prayer establishes a relationship in which people can express their needs; work brings before them what they can do; trust in God accompanies acceptance that they cannot govern the entire outcome. How these take shape in everyday life appears in their choices. For people waiting for a task to be completed, it matters that those choices are experienced as honesty and trustworthiness.

10. How can faith support a person in loneliness loss and difficulty

When people lose someone close to them or feel alone, they may try to make sense of life again through their faith. Turning to God can provide a basis for expressing pain and sustaining hope. Understanding this relationship begins with taking their loss seriously and listening to them.

Hearing a person who is in pain

After the loss of someone close, seemingly small things in everyday life may change. A chair once shared is empty, the evening conversation stops, and a person no longer knows whom to tell their news to first. We should not overlook this concrete absence when discussing faith. Expecting people to recover immediately can make it harder to understand what they are experiencing.

In this book, I do not look for faith’s contribution in people feeling obliged to conceal their pain. I focus on being able to pray while crying, to believe in the hereafter while expressing longing, and to accept help while acknowledging difficulty. I do not present these as stages everyone experiences in the same order. A person’s relationship with their loss may be unique to them.

What perspective does the collection offer

In the Second Point of the First Discussion of the Twenty-Third Word, Bediüzzaman Said Nursi describes a relationship between faith and a person’s view of the past and future. He constructs an imagined allegory involving a dark bridge and a changing field of vision. He then explains viewing death as entry into eternal life and the grave as a door to eternal happiness. [R6] This is a framework of meaning developed within the work’s belief in the hereafter, rather than a record of an actual event.

The Fifth Point emphasises a praying person’s awareness of turning to a God who hears their inner thoughts and can respond to their needs. It also addresses the connection between feeling without companionship and this awareness of closeness. [R5] Here we are conveying the source text’s religious explanation. We do not claim that every reader will experience the same feeling at the same moment.

How can meaning unique to a person emerge during loss

The idea I explore of a message unique to each person raises a question here too. When encountering the text, a reader may recognise a responsibility that remains from their relationship with the person they have lost. Another may think of an act of kindness they want to continue in their loneliness. These different responses do not remove the value that the meaning experienced has for the individual.

Yet a person should not turn that meaning into a command determining when somebody else must stop grieving. They can say, “I found strength in this reading,” without expecting someone else to find the same strength through the same path immediately. This assessment is my own approach. I do not add a claim that the Risale-i Nur passages cited here explicitly teach the idea of a message unique to each person.

Faith becoming social support

Visiting a neighbour who lives alone, listening or helping with a daily task can give tangible expression to words of comfort. Someone who has lost a loved one may not need long explanations. They may want a person to sit quietly beside them or help with an unfinished task. Trying to understand what they want is also part of solidarity.

Faith can encourage this support to continue beyond the first few days. People can remember their neighbour even after their own lives have returned to their ordinary course. In our examples, the positive contribution appears when recipients can respectfully access the support they need, rather than through being compelled to believe something. Accepting help does not mean giving up one’s inner world.

What does sustaining hope mean

Sometimes hope means recognising a small task that can be done tomorrow, before expecting a major change. People can return to their responsibilities without forgetting their loss. Faith can support the thought that their life is not made up solely of this one experience of pain. How that happens should be considered within their circumstances and relationships.

Turning to God and receiving support from people can coexist. Being able to express one’s own pain and hear another person’s pain can make room for compassion in relationships. In this answer, we seek faith’s contribution to individuals and society in sustaining hope and not leaving one another alone.

Short glossary

Cüz’-i ihtiyarî

The limited human capacity to choose. Its relationship to responsibility is discussed in this section.

Tevekkül

Trusting God and acknowledging the outcome as coming from Him while taking the available action and using the available means.

Ezel

A concept concerning divine eternity: God’s knowledge is not located at a starting point in past time.

Fiilî dua

An expression relating prayer to making the necessary effort while seeking an outcome.
