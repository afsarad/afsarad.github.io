Abstract

The spread of artificial intelligence technologies is not only changing how people access information but also opening up new possibilities for thinking, producing, learning and problem-solving. Yet much competition in the artificial intelligence market takes place through advertising, perception management, user acquisition and commercial positioning rather than comparisons of technological capacity based on concrete results. This study evaluates the competitive strategies of artificial intelligence platforms in terms of consumer behavior and social perception; it examines the heterogeneous user base, differences in technological expectations and the importance of user–platform alignment. Its central thesis is that the true social value of artificial intelligence should be sought in its ability to expand people's existing intellectual and productive potential rather than to outperform competitors. Instead of treating the human–artificial intelligence relationship as one of unilateral adaptation or dependency, the study proposes a model of intellectual synergy based on interaction between original human thought and the analytical and processing capacities of artificial intelligence.

Keywords: Artificial intelligence; intellectual synergy; human–machine interaction; sociology of technology; digital economy; productive capacity; user experience.

1. Introduction: A New Human Possibility Beyond Technology

Throughout human history, technological developments have emerged from attempts to expand physical and mental capacities. The wheel facilitated physical movement, the printing press the reproduction of knowledge, and the computer calculation and data processing. Unlike these developments, artificial intelligence does more than make a task faster: it also transforms how people work by classifying information, connecting disciplines, developing alternative ideas and addressing complex problems.

The number of experts, thinkers and producers of knowledge across disciplines whom a person can encounter in a lifetime is limited. Moreover, meeting these people, benefiting from their ideas and exchanging views regularly are not possible for everyone. Through models based on the knowledge accumulated by humanity in different periods and disciplines, artificial intelligence significantly extends these limits of access.

A conceptual distinction is necessary here: artificial intelligence is neither an actual community made up of human experts nor the perfect sum of all expertise. It is a set of systems that largely process patterns learned from training data, sometimes use external tools and generate probabilistic outputs. It therefore cannot be equated with human experts' direct experience, field knowledge, intuition and professional responsibility. Nevertheless, by processing patterns learned across fields of knowledge, it can provide users with a broad space for intellectual work.

This study evaluates the social importance of artificial intelligence not only through its technical capacity but also through how widely access to that capacity is extended and how it participates in human production processes.

2. Competition in the Artificial Intelligence Market: Genuine Superiority or Perception Management?

Competition among artificial intelligence companies is becoming increasingly visible as they seek to attract users' attention and increase subscription revenues. Advertising sometimes employs methods that belittle competitors, portray the company's product as an overwhelming force and communicate technological superiority through visual metaphors.

Imagine an advertisement in which concrete, wooden or fabric blocks representing competitors are placed before a heavy truck. The problem is not the company's claim to strength. It is natural for commercial enterprises to promote their products and present competitive advantages. The problem is that the comparison is not made on equal terms.

A truck crushing stationary concrete blocks does not prove that it is superior to another truck. Similarly, an artificial intelligence platform portraying its competitors as inert objects does not constitute proof of technological superiority. Such advertisements may create a sense of psychological dominance rather than offer a technical comparison.

Genuine technological competition should involve solving the same problem under similar conditions using comparable criteria. Accuracy, consistency, source reliability, processing capacity, speed, cost, suitability for user needs and long-term project management may be among the principal criteria. Making a product look powerful and proving that it is actually superior are not the same thing.

3. A Heterogeneous User Base and the Economics of First Experience

Artificial intelligence users do not share the same knowledge, expectations or purposes. One may use it to colorize an old photograph, another to obtain general information on a legal question, and another to prepare text or generate an image. More advanced users turn to these systems for research, academic writing, software development, data analysis, strategic planning and the management of long-term projects.

This diversity creates both opportunities and responsibilities for technology companies. Many new users may not yet have the experience to assess the technical limits of artificial intelligence and the actual differences in capacity between platforms. The results of a first encounter can generate considerable surprise and satisfaction, but this initial experience does not demonstrate that a platform succeeds at every task.

This situation can be considered the 'economics of first experience': users are drawn to a product because it quickly completes a task they previously struggled with; companies attempt to convert that initial satisfaction into a sustainable subscription relationship. Commercial profit is not itself the problem. The problem is exploiting users' lack of knowledge and experience through unrealistic claims of superiority.

Insufficient understanding of usage quotas, subscription tiers, processing speeds, differences between models and features available only on certain plans can make the real cost of a service difficult to assess. Companies therefore need to explain their products' limits and conditions of use clearly as well as promote them.

4. User–Artificial Intelligence Alignment: A Model of Mutual Development

As their use of artificial intelligence develops, users not only learn about the technology but also reorganize their own working methods. Initially, they learn how to ask the system questions. They then discover which tasks yield successful results, where errors can occur and which methods are more efficient. Over time, they develop their own working routine.

In this process, it is not only the user who adapts to artificial intelligence. The system can also adapt its responses to the available context, preferences and working methods. A model of unilateral adaptation is therefore insufficient to explain the relationship.

A model of mutual adaptation and development preserves human ways of thinking, experience, intuition and aims while drawing on artificial intelligence's capacities to process information, compare, classify and produce. Users can develop their thinking by evaluating the alternatives offered, while the system can support working methods suited to their needs.

The aim of this model is not to make humans resemble technology or to replace them with it. It is to establish a basis for collaboration in which people can direct technology toward their own purposes and challenge its suggestions when necessary.

5. Intellectual Synergy: Humans Generate, Artificial Intelligence Processes, Humans Reassess

The central conceptual proposal of this study is 'intellectual synergy': the emergence of a new productive possibility through interaction between original human thought and experience and the analytical, linguistic and processing capacities of artificial intelligence.

In the first stage, a person develops an idea from events, observations, education and experience. In the second, artificial intelligence organizes and conceptualizes the idea, suggests alternative formulations, makes comparisons and reveals possible connections. In the third, the person compares the revised text or suggestions with their own intellectual background, rejects points of disagreement, fills gaps and reshapes the idea when necessary.

The third stage is decisive for preserving intellectual independence. A text generated by artificial intelligence may be fluent, comprehensive or academic in appearance without accurately reflecting the user's thinking. Quality cannot be measured solely by linguistic correctness: conceptual consistency, originality of approach, reliability of evidence and alignment with the author's argument must also be assessed.

Human rereading and critical evaluation of artificial intelligence-generated text are therefore indispensable to joint production. Artificial intelligence does not own the thought; it is a tool that contributes to its development, testing and expression.

6. The Dependency Debate: Intellectual Responsibility or Capacity Expansion?

Many criticisms of artificial intelligence use rest on concerns that people may gradually lose their abilities to think, question and decide. These concerns are not entirely unfounded. Intellectual passivity can develop if users accept answers without verification, suspend their own judgment and hand decision-making responsibility to the system.

However, it is also wrong to assume that this possibility applies to all users and uses. Drawing on artificial intelligence and surrendering intellectual responsibility are not the same thing. By explaining their own idea to artificial intelligence, people can explore different formulations, request counterarguments, test assumptions and discover previously unnoticed connections.

In such cases, artificial intelligence does not remove people's responsibility to think; it allows them to compare their thinking with more alternatives. The dependency debate should focus not merely on how frequently technology is used but on the user's position in intellectual decision-making processes.

7. Reorganizing Time: Bringing Production into Everyday Life

One transformative effect of artificial intelligence concerns the relationship between production and time. Traditionally, preparing a substantial book or research project requires long periods devoted to research, note-taking, classification, writing, editing and organization. By accelerating many of these processes, artificial intelligence can allow production to become part of everyday life.

Thoughts do not arise only during designated working hours. New ideas may emerge while working in a shop, traveling, taking a meal break, resting or going about daily tasks. Recording, classifying and developing ideas as they emerge can increase continuity of production.

What matters here is not arranging one's entire life around technology. On the contrary, technology should be able to adapt to an existing way of life. Artificial intelligence can help people develop ideas more systematically without abandoning everyday responsibilities.

Within this study, the author's experience of preparing his first book can serve as a personal example of this possibility. Recording ideas at different moments of everyday life helped sustain classification, text editing and reassessment. This personal experience is not a research finding generalizable to all users; it is an illustrative example of how human–artificial intelligence collaboration can change the organization of everyday time.

8. Expanding Access to Intellectual Capacity

One important social consequence of artificial intelligence is that it creates new opportunities to access specialist knowledge and productive tools. In the past, preparing a book for publication, conducting extensive research or developing software could require access to people with different specialisms and considerable time. Today, artificial intelligence can support individuals at some stages of these processes.

This development does not mean that anyone can replace experts. Academic scrutiny, professional responsibility, field experience and verification remain necessary. However, individuals gain greater opportunities to turn an idea into a project, a project into a product and a product into a more extensive undertaking.

This possibility is particularly significant for people who struggle to access expert support for economic, geographical or institutional reasons. Evaluations of the social benefits of artificial intelligence should consider not only companies' economic size but also how many people's productive capacity the technology increases.

9. The Future Criterion of Competition: What Do You Enable Your User to Achieve?

Much competition among artificial intelligence companies concerns user numbers, subscription revenue, model capacity and technical performance. These are important measures, but another question will be more decisive for users: which tasks does the artificial intelligence I use enable me to accomplish, and how much does it increase my productive capacity?

A platform's success should be measured by the actual benefits it provides users as well as by its ability to outperform other platforms. Understanding users' thinking accurately, adapting to their working styles, explaining limits honestly, connecting disciplines and maintaining consistency in long-term projects are central elements of this benefit.

Rather than trying to crush their rivals, companies would embody a more meaningful understanding of competition by showing how their users accomplish tasks they previously could not.

10. Conclusion: Technology's Real Success Lies in Human Production

Artificial intelligence is an important technological development transforming access to knowledge and production processes. Yet the direction of this transformation will not be determined by technical capacity alone. Companies' commercial choices, users' awareness, educational systems, conditions of economic access and society's relationship with technology will also be decisive.

The argument advanced here is that the human–artificial intelligence relationship should be evaluated through intellectual synergy rather than as a struggle for superiority or dependency. Humans contribute experience, intuition, purposes, values and original thought. Artificial intelligence processes different fields of knowledge to help develop and organize these thoughts and establish new connections. People preserve their intellectual integrity by reassessing the results.

The true social value of artificial intelligence should be sought not in making itself look larger than its rivals but in helping people who previously lacked the opportunity to produce. Turning a long-held idea into a book, conducting extensive research while continuing daily life or realizing a previously unattainable project are examples of technology's concrete social benefits.

Does artificial intelligence prove its strength by crushing competitors or by giving its users productive capacities they did not previously possess? Its true value for humanity lies in the answer to the second question.

Technology that expands the reach of human thought rather than eliminating it makes a genuine contribution to social progress.
