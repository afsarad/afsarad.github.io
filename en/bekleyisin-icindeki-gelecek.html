The Future Inside the Wait: Why Did a Train Delay Feel Longer in Childhood?

Adem Afşar
Interdisciplinary sociological analyst
23 November 2026

As children, a train running half an hour—or even an hour—late could feel like an exceptionally long wait. As adults, the same kind of delay may trouble us less, even when it lasts longer. Age alone, or the sense that time now passes faster, may not fully explain this change. Alongside where our attention goes, the future possibilities we imagine while waiting may also matter.

For a child, a journey may be more than moving from one familiar place to another. What might happen along the way, what could be seen at the destination, and what might come next may not yet be clear. The world is less explored, so a journey can feel like movement toward the unknown. When the train is delayed, the child may be waiting not only for the journey to begin but also to reach a future that arouses curiosity. That anticipation may make the delay feel heavier.

In adulthood, more places, journeys and encounters become familiar. We may have a clearer idea of where we are going, what we are likely to find there, and how a delay will affect our plans. The destination may still matter, but the unknown may occupy less space. A delay can become less like a passage into a world of possibilities and more like an interruption to a familiar plan. The clock may measure the same interval, while its emotional weight changes.

What does research suggest?

The experience of waiting is not made up only of the minutes measured by a clock. Attention to time, uncertainty and distress are related to how a wait is experienced. The judgment that “time is passing slowly” is also different from measured duration: how a person compares the felt interval with their expectations can matter. Experiments by Tanaka and Yotsumoto found that temporal expectations can affect judgments about the passage of time. [1]

Maglio and Kwok directly examined uncertainty about what awaits at a destination. They found that ambiguity about a future event can expand the subjective duration of the present journey leading to it. [2] This finding does not by itself explain every childhood train delay. It does, however, support the idea that “What will I find there?” can influence how the present wait feels.

Research across the lifespan also suggests that people’s outlook on the future and their priorities may change. Under some conditions, people who perceive their future as more limited may give greater priority to meaningful relationships and current goals than to exploring new possibilities. This does not mean curiosity inevitably disappears with age. Outlooks on the future differ from person to person, and age alone is not a sufficient explanation. [3]

The anticipated future can shape the present wait

Two elements should be distinguished: the novelty and uncertainty of the future, and the curiosity, excitement or anxiety we feel about it. They are not the same, and they do not always produce the same emotion. We may eagerly anticipate something pleasant, or feel anxious about an uncertain outcome. In either case, a future that has not yet happened enters the present. The wait fills with expectations, questions and imagined possibilities.

In the train example, the delay itself may not be what changes; the person’s relationship with the future destination may change. In childhood, the train may open onto a world not yet fully known. In adulthood, the journey and likely encounters may be more familiar. The curious anticipation within the wait may therefore lessen, and the delay may no longer feel as distressing.

This observation suggests the following proposition: How long and burdensome a wait feels may depend not only on the expected duration, but also on how wide a field of future possibilities it opens in the mind. The less explored, more intriguing and more open-ended the future appears, the more present the wait to reach it may feel. As the world and its journeys become more familiar, some expectations may become more predictable, and the sense of discovery within the wait may weaken.

This is not a general law that “children always experience time more slowly.” A child may find a boring journey long and lose track of time while playing. An adult may also feel time dragging while waiting for an important, uncertain outcome. The interpretation here is not that age alone changes time, but that experience, knowledge, expectation and one’s horizon of the future may shape the experience of waiting together.

To understand why a train delay feels long, it is not enough to look at the clock. We should also ask where the person is going, what they expect to find there, how familiar the journey is, and which possibilities occupy their mind. Sometimes, what we are waiting for is not only the train, but the future it will take us toward.

Scope note: Research has examined the relationship between uncertainty about the future and judgments of duration, as well as the role of temporal expectations. Explaining childhood train waits through the idea of an “undiscovered future” is Adem Afşar’s interpretive proposition, developed from those findings; it is not the result of a direct field study of children travelling by train.
