The Autumn of Social Life

The Invisible Order That Sustains Beauty

During my morning walk, as I reflected on the subtle changes the seasonal transition had left around me, a detail I had not noticed before caught my eye in the flower beds beside the road. Plastic barriers placed to prevent the soil from being disturbed, drip-irrigation pipes watering the plants and various arrangements concealed by the greenery had emerged with autumn. The infrastructure covered by colours and leaves was now visible in all its bareness.

Where beauty had once rested our eyes, there remained the materials that made that beauty possible but did not themselves carry the same aesthetic effect.

What prompted my thoughts was not the presence of these materials or how the landscaping had been arranged. My mind moved from the scene before me to social life. Could something similar exist in human relationships, institutions and our ways of living together? What mechanisms operated beneath a life that appeared harmonious, warm and orderly? Why did we often notice them only when circumstances changed?

Autumn had not laid new pipes among the flowers. It had made visible what was already there.

The order we take for granted

We live our daily lives without considering all the conditions that continually make them possible. We take a shop opening, a school operating, a family meal being prepared or an institution providing services as ordinary events. As the outcome repeats, the effort producing it slips from view.

Yet every seemingly orderly day contains people who organise their time, postpone their own needs, assume responsibilities and resolve difficulties. Alongside them are divisions of labour, material resources, rules and sanctions that come into play when needed.

Continuity can thus make its own foundations invisible. After a while, we begin to perceive what we find ready every morning as something that needs no preparation.

This invisibility is not distributed equally. The person who sets the table knows it did not set itself. Someone who works extra every day to prevent disruption in an institution sees the effort on which others’ comfort depends. What one person takes for granted as order may be another’s continual labour.

When does infrastructure become visible?

In their work on infrastructure, Susan Leigh Star and Karen Ruhleder note that functioning systems can recede into the background during everyday use and become visible when they break down (1996). Thinking about the electricity grid when the power fails or the communication system when a connection drops provides examples.

The scene I encountered on my walk can be related to this idea, but no breakdown was necessary here. A change of season had lifted the covering over a functioning arrangement. My interpretation extending from this to social life is this: noticing the invisible does not always require a collapse. The end of a task, a person’s departure or a change in familiar circumstances may also suffice.

For example, while everyone in a workplace is thought to work harmoniously, tensions may increase when an employee who quietly resolves disagreements leaves. That person’s contribution is understood through their absence. A family’s regular caregiver becoming ill may create a similar visibility. Tasks previously taken for granted suddenly emerge as demanding and indispensable responsibilities.

Such changes remind us that hidden calculations are not all that lies behind order. Sometimes what becomes visible is previously unnoticed labour.

The distance between beauty and its foundations

Nevertheless, the infrastructure of social life does not consist solely of sacrifice. Closeness and harmony may also contain mutual needs, interests, habits, dependencies and fears of loss.

We can love someone and also need their support. We can embrace an institution’s purpose while also valuing the income from working there. We can feel that we belong to a community while benefiting from the opportunities it offers. These motives may coexist in the same person’s life.

The presence of need or benefit therefore does not automatically invalidate love and commitment. People benefiting from one another is an ordinary dimension of living together. What needs assessment is how this happens: Is there reciprocity? Can the parties object? Does one exploit the other’s helplessness? Is the benefit obtained at the cost of violating someone else’s rights?

There is a moral distance between having an interest and sacrificing everything for it. Reducing every relationship to the same explanation without respecting this distance makes people harder to understand.

The social stage and its preparation area

In examining everyday interaction, Erving Goffman distinguishes between the image people present before others and the back region where this presentation is prepared. Behind the scenes, there may be preparation, correction, rest and activities not shown to the audience (1956).

This approach is useful for thinking about how social appearances are produced. People do not express every emotion in the same way in every setting. An employee controlling their anger, a teacher trying not to convey anxiety to a student or someone listening attentively despite being tired regulates their behaviour according to the requirements of the relationship.

From these examples, I believe we cannot regard every difference between an appearance and its preparation as hypocrisy. Regulating emotions and trying not to harm others are also among the foundations of social life.

Yet the same distinction opens another possibility to scrutiny: behind an outward image of respect may lie humiliation; behind the rhetoric of solidarity, exploitation; or behind an appearance of voluntariness, compulsion. In such cases, knowing how the appearance is produced changes the moral assessment.

Seeing behind the scenes is not enough on its own to pass judgment. We need to examine what is being done there, for whom and at what cost.

Who sustains the peace?

The absence of arguments in a family may not mean everyone is satisfied. If one member continually remains silent, postpones their needs and soothes others’ anger, that person may be sustaining a substantial part of the apparent peace.

Similarly, a workplace’s smooth operation may depend on some employees’ uncompensated labour. While a community appears to have reached a joint decision, some people may refrain from objecting for fear of exclusion.

Here, we must investigate the conditions of harmony before its existence. Do people agree because they can speak, or remain silent because they cannot? Does togetherness continue through mutual respect, or because leaving or objecting carries a heavy cost?

These questions do not require assuming that all silence results from oppression. They help distinguish different reasons for silence. The same outward appearance can emerge from very different relationships.

Asking about the cost of beauty also becomes meaningful here. Who becomes exhausted to sustain the order we see? Whose labour goes unnoticed? Who must constantly be understanding? Whose comfort depends on others’ inability to express themselves?

Rules, trust and morality

Rules are another invisible foundation of social life. Even among people we trust, there may be a need to define duties, keep accounts and share responsibility.

A written agreement in a partnership does not show that the parties do not care for each other. Nor does oversight of an institution mean everyone working there acts in bad faith. People can forget, make mistakes or be inclined to justify their own behaviour. Clear rules can reduce disagreements arising from uncertainty.

However, the rules themselves are also open to assessment. Whom they protect, whom they burden and who can change them matter. Maintaining order does not prove that a practice is fair.

Seeing the infrastructure should therefore lead neither to treating every restriction as unnecessary nor to legitimising them all. Noticing the existence of a barrier is a beginning. We need to consider what it protects, whom it obstructs and whether it could be arranged differently.

The autumn of social life

By the autumn of social life, I mean periods when the conditions sustaining familiar appearances change. These may involve material loss, the end of a position, illness, retirement or the disappearance of a shared goal. The expression is used here as a metaphor; it does not claim that seasons in nature can be applied directly to society.

A decline in the attention surrounding someone after their authority ends prompts reflection on some foundations of that attention. Yet we cannot regard every departure as proof of opportunism. Shared activities and occasions for meeting may also end with the role. Judging a relationship’s character requires more than observing a single change.

Similar care is needed with crises. It is said that difficult times invariably reveal people’s “true faces.” Yet harsh circumstances can produce new behaviour as well as reveal existing tendencies. Fear, exhaustion and loss of resources can alter a person’s room for action.

For this reason, we cannot treat everything we see in autumn as the sole and definitive explanation of the past. Nevertheless, change allows us to ask questions we did not ask amid our previous comfort.

Not everything that looks ugly is rotten

Returning to the opening scene reveals an important distinction. Plastic pipes being aesthetically unappealing does not show that their functions are harmful. In social life, however, we can easily turn the word “ugliness” into a moral judgment. We must be careful about this transition when using the metaphor.

An institution’s records, duty rosters, maintenance work or oversight processes may be less attractive than its outwardly visible success. Yet these are activities that carry the responsibility for that success.

By contrast, mechanisms that violate others’ rights, punish objections or use people merely as instruments cannot be absolved because they work. Functioning and being legitimate are different assessments.

It is also insufficient to look for corruption solely in the presence of self-interest. People can respect social limits while seeking to meet their needs. What is destructive is devaluing the rights, trust and responsibilities that make living together possible for one’s own benefit. In such a case, the shared life that ought to be protected becomes material for personal gain.

The responsibility of noticing the invisible

The scene I encountered on my walk did not invite me to distrust people. It led me to think more carefully about the foundations of things I found beautiful.

This attention can sometimes show that a relationship rests on exploitation; at other times, it can reveal labour we have failed to notice for years. Sometimes we see a rule that needs changing, sometimes a responsibility that needs protecting. Sometimes, while questioning others’ behaviour, we notice the invisible burdens that secure our own comfort.

None of this requires us to lose faith in beauty. Understanding how it is sustained allows us to consider the conditions for sustaining it more fairly and durably.

Pipes becoming visible among the flowers when autumn arrives do not negate the beauty we saw in summer. They do teach us, however, that this beauty was more than flowers alone.

In the autumn of social life, a similar opportunity lies before us: to recognise the labour behind appearances, share burdens, question oppression and repair the foundations of living together.

Loving beauty also requires taking responsibility for the order that sustains it.
