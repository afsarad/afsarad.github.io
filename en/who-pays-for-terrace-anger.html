Nine clubs referred to the disciplinary board. Who pays the price for anger in the stands?

Adem Afşar
Interdisciplinary sociological analyst
26 November 2026

The referral of nine Turkish Süper Lig clubs to the disciplinary board renewed scrutiny of how clubs maintain order in the stands. Supporters bring clubs revenue and backing. The cost of a crowd incident can also fall on clubs and on spectators who took no part in it.

As reported in the national press, on 24 November 2026 the legal office of the Turkish Football Federation (TFF) referred Gaziantep FK, Galatasaray, Samsunspor, Amed Sportif Faaliyetler, Kocaelispor, Fenerbahçe, Konyaspor, Beşiktaş and Arca Çorum FK to the Professional Football Disciplinary Board (PFDK). The announcement cited grounds including abusive chanting, crowd incidents and failure to keep stand stairways clear. The referrals are confirmed. The alleged breaches listed in the announcement were submitted for the board’s review.

Who can supporters contact at their club?

The TFF’s licensing rules require applicants to appoint a supporter liaison officer to their professional staff. This officer is the main point of contact for supporters. Article 9 of Law No. 6222 also provides for clubs to designate a representative responsible for supporters from among their board members. The statutory representative assists public and private security officers with match safety.

In November 2024, a Council of Europe delegation met supporter liaison officers from six Süper Lig clubs, including Beşiktaş, Fenerbahçe and Galatasaray. Galatasaray also publishes a contact channel for supporters’ associations on its official site. These examples show that some clubs have put formal channels into practice. Comparable league-wide data could not be found on access for supporters outside associations, how often they receive replies or how their views shape management decisions.

Who bears the cost of crowd incidents?

A Galatasaray valuation report published on Türkiye’s Public Disclosure Platform lists individual match tickets, season tickets, suites and VIP sales as sources of matchday revenue. A separate case illustrates a direct cost of crowd incidents. On 12 November, the PFDK fined Samsunspor 280,000 Turkish lira for crowd incidents and barred spectators from the relevant section from attending the next home match. Depending on the incident, a club can also face stadium closure or an order to play behind closed doors. When the statutory conditions are met, the person responsible may also face an individual ban from attending matches.

Adem Afşar’s sociological analysis

For supporters, the most visible benefit of following a team begins with the pleasure of watching the game. A match can provide motivation and an outlet for everyday tension. Taking sides, sharing joy and disappointment, meeting new people and expressing oneself in ways rarely used in everyday life are part of the experience. Outside formal club membership, this relationship usually rests on an unwritten commitment. Even so, it is a social force capable of bringing thousands of people together regularly.

Clubs that rely on this support should speak with supporters regularly and attend to order in the stands before kick-off. A single spectator’s actions can impose costs on a club that invests in youth development, manages a substantial budget and helps raise Türkiye’s profile through international competition. The same incident can affect other spectators’ access to matches. An accessible channel to report a safety concern and receive a reply is one practical way to prevent problems.

A thought experiment. Imagine an expensive signing has fallen short of expectations and supporters are asking management for an explanation. Then a referee makes a genuine mistake in a match. If club executives devote their public comments to the refereeing decision, discussion in the stands and on social media may shift in that direction. The mistake and the supporters’ reaction can both be real while questions about who made the signing, on what grounds and at what cost fade from view.

Management’s short-term interest might be to face less scrutiny of its decision. If the tension leads to a disciplinary sanction, the cost can fall on the club and on other spectators. This example makes no allegation about the clubs referred to the board. The public referrals do not establish such a strategy. Examining that possibility in a specific case would require looking at the timing of statements, whether the same pattern recurs in similar circumstances and whether management answers questions about the transfer.

The strength of the relationship depends on what supporters can ask their club. A spectator who reports a safety issue should also be able to ask about transfers and the budget. The club should hear and answer objections to its own decisions, just as it listens to objections about a referee’s call.
