Must We Know Every Piece of News?

Curiosity, Attention, Responsibility and Trust in God in the Risale-i Nur

An event in a distant part of the world can enter our household's conversation within minutes. Sometimes a verified report, sometimes a commentary, sometimes something we hear from an acquaintance leads us to consider possibilities that have not yet materialised: “What if war breaks out? What will happen to our work? How will we earn a living?”

Behind these questions may lie a desire to protect ourselves and those we love. Yet acquiring information, making preparations and continually carrying the anxiety of the same possibility are different situations. As we follow the news, we also need to consider which responsibilities in our own lives we are fulfilling and which we are postponing.

In the Fourth Matter of Meyve Risalesi, the Eleventh Şuâ in Şuâlar, Bediüzzaman Said Nursi addresses this question through the war news of his own period. In the Twenty-Third Söz in Sözler, he explains trust in God through a parable of two passengers carrying loads aboard a ship. Reading these texts together allows us to consider both attending to our duties and entrusting outcomes beyond our power to God.

We cannot simply equate the radio in these texts with today's telephone. We can, however, draw connections concerning how news attracts attention, curiosity occupies time and partisanship affects judgement. These connections are our sociological interpretations developed from the source texts.

1. What could be more important than war news?

The Fourth Matter begins with a question addressed to Bediüzzaman Said Nursi. In asking why he does not show curiosity about war news, his companions say:

Source passage 1 · Read on this page

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · English translation of the source passage · Source and page reference

Yet some devout and learned people leave the congregation and the mosque and rush to listen to the radio. Is there an event greater than this? Or is there harm in occupying oneself with it?

The weight of the question is clear: when the world is in a great war, how should not following these developments be understood? Bediüzzaman Said Nursi's response first turns to the time available to a human being:

Source passage 2 · Read on this page

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · English translation of the source passage · Source and page reference

The capital of a lifetime is very small. Necessary tasks are very many.

The issue is not merely which news is interesting. Among which duties, and in what order of priority, will a person use their limited lifetime? Does the fact that an event concerns the whole world by itself establish that continually occupying ourselves with it is more important than our own duties?

2. Interconnected circles of duty

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi does not describe human relationships as disconnected spheres:

Source passage 3 · Read on this page

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · English translation of the source passage · Source and page reference

Like circles contained within one another, there are interconnected circles extending from every person's heart and stomach, body and household, neighbourhood and city, homeland and country, to the earth and humankind, and as far as living beings and the world.

Mütedâhil means interconnected or contained within one another; nev-i beşer means humankind; zîhayat means possessing life. A person is connected with their body, family, place of residence and all humanity. The following sentences show that the wider circles are not entirely excluded from responsibility:

Source passage 4 · Read on this page

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · English translation of the source passage · Source and page reference

Every person may have a kind of duty in each circle. But in the smallest circle there is the greatest, most important and permanent duty. In the largest circle there may be the smallest and temporary duty, occurring from time to time.

Muvakkat means temporary. The emphasis is that we should not assume our duties are equally frequent or equally extensive in all circles. The magnitude of an event does not necessarily mean that our personal duty concerning it is equally great.

For the present day, this distinction suggests a question: is talking at length about an event enough to show that we are fulfilling a responsibility concerning it?

3. The attraction of the wider circle

The danger identified in the text concerns not so much the existence of distant events as their capacity to push necessary duties nearby into the background:

Source passage 5 · Read on this page

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · English translation of the source passage · Source and page reference

But through the attraction of the larger circle, it causes a person to abandon necessary and important service in the smaller circle and occupies them with unnecessary, idle and outward affairs. It destroys the capital of their life in vain. It spends that precious lifetime on things of no value.

Malayani here means idle occupations that provide no necessary benefit; âfâkî refers to the broad sphere of events outside the person. We should not conclude that every external news report is unnecessary. The warning concerns an occupation that causes necessary services to be abandoned.

Our sociological interpretation concerning the present is this: while constantly receiving information, someone may postpone a conversation they need to have, work they need to finish or a relative who needs attention. Following the news then becomes a question not only of acquiring information but of how attention and time are used.

4. From something heard to a disaster not yet experienced

Consider this example: a person hears from an acquaintance that meetings have become more frequent in certain institutions. They interpret this account as a sign of approaching war. Their thoughts then turn to their own livelihood: “How will we live if something like that happens?”

In this example, what is heard, the conclusion drawn and the anticipated personal harm need to be distinguished. An indirect account of meetings does not by itself confirm that war is imminent. Nevertheless, a person may fill in the uncertainties mentally and begin carrying today the weight of a situation that has not yet occurred.

Consulting reliable information and taking precautions within one's means have concrete implications for protecting a livelihood. Repeatedly discussing the same possibility without producing new information or an actionable decision is different.

We are not belittling anyone's anxiety. Our question is: do the thoughts through which we seek to protect tomorrow support the duties we can fulfil today, or do they disrupt them?

5. Carrying on one's back the load already carried by the ship

In the Third Point of the First Part of the Twenty-Third Söz, Bediüzzaman Said Nursi explains trust in God, first guarding against a misunderstanding:

Source passage 6 · Read on this page

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · English translation of the source passage · Source and page reference

But do not misunderstand. Trust in God does not mean rejecting causes altogether. Rather, it consists in observing causes while knowing them to be a veil of the Hand of Power; regarding recourse to causes as a kind of prayer through action; asking for effects only from Almighty God, knowing outcomes to be from Him and being grateful to Him.

Esbab means causes; esbaba teşebbüs means making use of necessary causes and means; dua-i fiilî means prayer through one's work and effort; müsebbebat means the effects or outcomes that follow causes.

Trust in God therefore does not mean abandoning precautions and effort. A person makes an effort and knows the outcome to be from God. The parable that follows makes this distinction visible:

Source passage 7 · Read on this page

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · English translation of the source passage · Source and page reference

Once, two men loaded heavy burdens onto both their waists and their heads, bought a ticket for a large ship and boarded it. As soon as one entered, he put his load down on the ship, sat on it and watched over it. The other, being both foolish and proud, would not put his load down. He was told: “Put your heavy load down on the ship and rest.” He said: “No, I will not put it down. It might be lost. I am strong. I will protect my property on my waist and head.”

The passenger who refuses to put down his load receives this reminder:

Source passage 8 · Read on this page

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · English translation of the source passage · Source and page reference

This safe royal ship which carries both us and you is stronger. It protects better. Your head may spin and you may fall into the sea with your load. Moreover, you will gradually lose your strength.

An important detail in the parable is that the passenger who puts down his load does not abandon it: he “watches over it.” The responsibility of looking after it remains. But he does not try to carry on his own body the load already carried by the ship.

Here we can draw a connection with the present. Someone can acquire information and take precautions against a future difficulty. But they cannot guarantee the outcome by mentally carrying every development in the world. Repeatedly thinking about the same possibility does not always amount to making new preparations.

This parable should not be used to belittle the person in our anonymous example or to apply its negative descriptions to everyone who feels anxious. The question we can address to ourselves is: are we doing what falls to us, or are we also trying to carry on our own shoulders outcomes beyond our power?

6. Recognising oppression while taking sides

In the Fourth Matter, Bediüzzaman Said Nursi also points to another danger in following the news:

Source passage 9 · Read on this page

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · English translation of the source passage · Source and page reference

And sometimes a person who follows these struggles of war with curiosity takes one side to heart. They look favourably upon its acts of oppression and become a partner in its oppression.

Şerik means partner. This sentence concerns more than the amount of time spent. It also raises the question of how partisanship can affect moral judgement.

Wanting one side to succeed can turn into approving everything it does. The identity of the actor can then become more decisive than the nature of the act. The warning in the text concerns partisanship advancing to the point of tolerating oppression.

In today's news and discussion settings, we can ask ourselves: do we criticise the same behaviour when the opposing side does it and excuse it when our preferred side does it?

7. The central purpose of the texts: faith and eternal life

Reading these sections merely as advice on using time efficiently or reducing anxiety would leave their central purpose incomplete. In the continuation of the Fourth Matter, Bediüzzaman Said Nursi centres the question of faith and eternal life. This fundamental responsibility is the priority he seeks to protect against the attraction of the news.

In the Twenty-Third Söz, the connection between faith and trust in God is also made explicit:

Source passage 10 · Read on this page

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · English translation of the source passage · Source and page reference

Thus faith entails affirming divine unity, divine unity entails submission, submission entails trust in God, and trust in God entails happiness in both worlds.

Tevhid means affirming God's oneness; teslim means submission to Him; saadet-i dareyn means happiness in this world and the hereafter; iktiza etmek means to entail or require. This sentence shows the religious basis of the ship parable. It is not merely a general relaxation technique; it is understood within a framework of faith and trust in God.

The attention recommended here is therefore not simply a means of getting more work done. It concerns remembering that human beings do not remain in the world forever, not neglecting duties concerning faith, and valuing one's lifetime with that awareness.

8. Fulfilling one's duty and knowing outcomes to be from God

Reading the two texts together enables us to consider the warning against neglecting duty alongside the explanation of trust in God through recourse to causes. A person seeks to identify and fulfil what they ought to do. At the same time, they recognise that they cannot carry on their own shoulders every outcome beyond their will and power.

Which duty are we fulfilling as we keep informed? Does curiosity lead us to reliable information and necessary action? Can we recognise wrongdoing by the side we support? After taking precautions, do we continue carrying the weight of the same possibility?

These questions are not intended to measure anyone's faith or pass judgement on a community. They invite reflection on our everyday orientations through the criteria offered by the texts.

Not forgetting our own duty while learning what happens in the world; knowing outcomes to be from God while making the necessary effort: these two reminders can be considered together in everyday life surrounded by news.
