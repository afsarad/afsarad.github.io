The Power of Advertising in Choosing an AI Platform: Visual Superiority, Comparability and Realized Benefit

Abstract. This conceptual study addresses the relationship between visual metaphors of superiority in comparative advertising for artificial intelligence platforms and users’ actual work requirements. It asks how the image of power offered by advertising may obscure functional comparability and proposes testable hypotheses concerning heterogeneous user expectations, subscription quotas and completion of long-term tasks. The text reports no experimental results; it offers a research framework based on observation.

Keywords: Artificial intelligence platforms, comparative advertising, visual metaphor, sociology of consumption, user expectations, realized benefit.

“Do the creators of advertisements never consider that viewers might question claims of superiority based on objects that cannot meaningfully be compared?”

Introduction: The advertising viewer’s question

Competition among AI platforms involves more than model accuracy, speed or technical capacity. Advertisements tell users what they may accomplish in the future; sometimes choosing a platform is itself presented as a sign of achievement. This study begins with a viewer’s simple but decisive question: “Do the creators of advertisements never consider that viewers might question claims of superiority based on objects that cannot meaningfully be compared?” This is more than an objection to an advertisement’s accuracy. It questions advertisers’ assumptions about viewers’ capacity for critical thinking. Our object of inquiry is not the general benefits of AI technology, but how platforms present themselves, users’ expectations and those expectations’ encounter with actual experience.

1. Research question and methodological limitations

This article is a conceptual sociological study developed from observing advertising. It does not systematically archive all relevant advertisements, conduct experiments with a representative consumer sample or investigate companies’ internal marketing decisions. It therefore proposes testable explanations rather than definitive judgments about corporate intentions. The analysis distinguishes three materials: observed advertising narratives, interpretations drawn from them and user experience. In particular, predictions formed after seeing an advertisement must not be confused with conclusions reached after using the product. Otherwise, what one thought previously may be unconsciously rewritten using subsequently acquired knowledge.

2. The criterion of comparison: A giant truck and a family car

The initial observation concerns an advertising narrative depicting one platform as a gigantic construction vehicle or truck and competitors as ordinary family cars. Because the original footage has not been archivally verified, no judgment is made about a particular brand or campaign; the scene reported by the user is treated as a case description. A truck’s size does not make it better suited to every transport need. That cars and construction vehicles serve different purposes shows how the advertisement’s power metaphor also creates a comparability problem. A similar issue arises when comparing AI platforms: a larger model, more features or an impressive promotion does not prove that a particular user’s task will be completed at lower cost and without interruption. The concept proposed here is a “disconnect between visual superiority and functional comparability.” This label is an analytical proposal developed from the author’s observation, not a new theory validated in the literature.

3. Disproportionate images of power and the critical viewer

Visual metaphors can turn complex technical differences into readily understood symbols. However, understanding an analogy does not mean its claim of superiority has been proven. Visual disproportion may inspire admiration and attention in some viewers while arousing suspicion in others. Research has shown that visual incongruity can have a nonlinear effect on advertising evaluations (Mohanty and Ratneshwar, 2016). Thus, “a bigger image means stronger persuasion” cannot be a universal conclusion. Our main question is: Does the advertisement aim to persuade viewers capable of technical comparison, or assume that an immediate impression of power will suffice? Distinguishing these possibilities requires statements by advertisement designers and audience responses.

4. Comparing past advertising language with the present

One of the author’s historical observations is that earlier media experiences involved less frequent naming of competing brands and more indirect expressions such as “compared with its equivalents.” This cannot be claimed for every period and country. For example, the US Federal Trade Commission’s 1979 policy regarded clear, non-deceptive competitor comparisons as helping consumers obtain information (FTC, 1979). The issue is therefore not simply whether competitors are named. It is what common criteria underpin the comparison and whether consumers can test it. The meaning of “we outperform competitors” remains unclear without criteria such as accuracy, task type, time, price, usage limits and error rates.

5. Heterogeneous users: One promise, different expectations

A user seeking a one-page text and another pursuing a months-long software project do not have the same need. The same advertisement may create different expectations in them. Distinguishing users who rapidly embrace novelty, prioritize functional benefits or wait and observe is an explanatory tool developed from our earlier discussions. These three tendencies are not fixed personality types; the same person may behave differently across tasks. Rogers’s diffusion-of-innovations approach (2003) helps us consider social differences in adoption timing; the distinction proposed here focuses on users’ reasons for decisions. The success of platform selection should therefore be evaluated through a defined user task rather than one general ranking.

6. What does advertising sell: Capability or future success?

People purchasing AI subscriptions consider not only features accessible today but also work they hope to complete in the future. Advertising’s emphasis on “results,” “speed,” “ease” or “superiority” evokes an experience of productivity not yet lived. The distinction between need and desire is helpful here. The actual need may be to finish a particular job; desire may become an expectation of completing it with the most powerful tool, in the shortest time and without obstacles. Veblen’s account of status-oriented consumption and Bourdieu’s analysis of social distinction suggest that technological choices can carry symbolic meanings. Yet it would be mistaken to claim that every choice of a new platform stems from ostentation or a desire for status. Functional benefits, curiosity and professional necessity are also valid reasons.

7. The distance between usage quotas and promised outcomes

In an example drawn from personal experience, a comprehensive personal-planner project was begun with a paid platform perceived as marketed around results; work could not continue because the usage quota was exhausted quickly. This is one user’s individual experience, not evidence that the platform produces identical outcomes for all subscribers. Nevertheless, it reveals an important distinction: a platform’s ability to perform particular tasks and a user’s ability to complete a project within the purchased subscription are different achievements. Product features should be assessed together with sufficient access throughout the project, continuity and cost predictability. “Completion cost per job” and “capacity for uninterrupted work” may therefore be meaningful consumer criteria.

8. Early users, reviews and the social production of trust

A new platform’s early users share experiences with others. Positive accounts can multiply rapidly; negative experiences can spread likewise. However, early users differ in technical skills, purposes and payment arrangements. One person successfully completing a simple task does not prove another will succeed with a complex project. The brand-perception example from our earlier discussions is illuminating: consumers may confuse a product’s concrete attributes with trust surrounding its name. Brand trust is not entirely groundless; it can arise from past experience. But it does not by itself guarantee completion of a particular job today.

9. The testable usefulness of prediction

This study’s contribution extends beyond criticizing advertising. It seeks to test whether viewers’ inferences before purchasing have practical value. First, users are asked to define a concrete task: expected output, acceptable error rate, time, budget, data privacy and necessary integrations. Next, the advertisement’s explicit claims are recorded separately from interpretive impressions. Third, contractual terms and usage limits are examined. Finally, the same task is tested under conditions as equivalent as possible. Predictions are recorded and dated in advance; results are compared through task-completion rates, total cost, additional intervention needed and interruption time. Alongside “Did the advertisement have an effect?” one can thus investigate “Did critical preliminary evaluation improve selection quality?”

10. Research hypotheses

H1: Extreme power metaphors lacking technical criteria may reduce advertising credibility among viewers inclined to make technical comparisons. H2: The same advertising promise creates different expectations among people with different purposes and experience levels. H3: Users who examine quotas, costs and task suitability before subscribing may experience a smaller expectation–experience gap than those choosing solely on advertising promises. H4: Measuring platform success only through individual-task performance may conceal completion and sustainability problems in long-term projects. These are propositions for future surveys, experiments and comparative usage tests, not findings.

11. Ethics, comparison and interpretive limits

Comparative advertising is not inherently unethical. Clear and accurate comparisons can provide useful consumer information. The problem arises when a sense of superiority replaces verifiable criteria. Moreover, visual metaphor does not always intend to deceive; it may be a natural part of creative expression. Research should examine message structure and audience interpretation rather than assume advertisers’ intentions. An advertisement appearing exaggerated and its being legally proven misleading are also separate issues. This study makes no allegation of legal infringement against a particular company.

Conclusion: From impressions of superiority to measurable benefit

A gigantic truck overshadowing small cars can be a powerful visual narrative. Yet the question of which vehicle suits which task remains. For AI platforms too, the central question should be “Which user can complete which task, under what conditions and to what extent?” rather than “Who is biggest?” The observation motivating this study objects to equating visual size with functional superiority. The sociological proposition developed from it is that advertising-induced impressions of superiority and users’ realized benefits should be measured on separate levels. Critical observation can deliver concrete decision benefits only when combined with predictions recorded beforehand and subsequently verified experience.
