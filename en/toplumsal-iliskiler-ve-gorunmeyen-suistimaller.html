Social Relationships and Invisible Abuses

An injustice need not be concealed to remain invisible. Sometimes everyone knows, but what is happening is not considered worth discussing. Repeatedly benefiting from someone's unpaid labour, granting privileges to an acquaintance or appropriating a shared resource for personal use may be accepted as the ordinary functioning of relationships. What becomes invisible then is not only the behaviour. The cost it imposes on someone else also escapes attention.

In this essay I do not reduce corruption to people thinking a little more about their own interests. Sustaining one's life and looking after one's interests are ordinary parts of human existence. What I call corruption is a destructive way of thinking that disregards the labour humanity has devoted to civilisation. I mean an attitude that discards the value of trust, justice, respect for labour and responsibility in our shared life. Invisible abuses are among the areas where this attitude can become established in everyday relationships.

The Late Student's Relief

There is an example I particularly like and often recount. A student late for school walks quickly, distressed, bewildered and afraid. All the other students have entered their classrooms. The empty streets intensify the anxiety. The student is not merely late, but also believes they are the only person who is late.

Just then, the student sees a classmate who is also late. The distress that was overwhelming the student suddenly eases. Yet the start of the lesson has not changed, the lateness has not disappeared and the missed time has not returned. What changed is the knowledge of not being alone. Seeing that they are not the only person to have made a mistake brings relief.

The point I emphasise in this example is the difference between correcting a wrong and reducing discomfort about it. A person may feel better without changing their behaviour. Sometimes this relief comes from the presence of someone else behaving the same way. The question they should ask themselves thereby moves away from the centre of attention.

Of course, a student's relief upon seeing a friend is not itself corruption. Reduced anxiety may also reflect human solidarity. This account is neither an experimental result nor a moral diagnosis of an individual. I use it to consider how sharing a wrong may weaken the need for self-questioning. Rather than equating lateness with serious abuse, I draw attention to a change in evaluation that could be investigated in both cases.

When the Standard Changes but the Wrong Remains

The fact that others also engage in a behaviour does not automatically remove its consequences. Learning that one is not alone does not remedy the loss of the person wronged. Yet a sense of common participation may change how the behaviour is evaluated. Instead of explaining what they have done, a person may now settle for saying that others do it too.

Imagine a workplace where a promise to a customer is broken and the response is, “Everyone works like this here.” This does not explain why the customer was kept waiting or how the harm will be remedied. It seeks to reduce responsibility by placing the conduct within prevailing habits. Likewise, when someone occupying a shared area with personal belongings points out that others do the same, the problem of access remains unresolved.

The danger is that a person evaluates their own conduct solely by looking at those around them. When local practice becomes the only measure of legitimacy, a shared wrong may become a mutually reinforcing justification. Responsibility is thought to diminish as others join in, whereas repeating the same behaviour more widely may increase the total cost imposed on others.

The Invisible Labour of Civilisation

Civilisation cannot be understood only as grand buildings, tools and technical achievements. Trusting that a promise will be kept, claiming payment for one's labour, using shared space and challenging injustice are also accumulated achievements of living together. These possibilities depend on standards of what people can expect from one another.

In my interpretation here, corruption's destructiveness appears when people disregard in their own conduct the requirements of this inherited achievement from which they benefit. While expecting promises made to them to be kept, they may readily forget their own. While demanding payment for their labour, they may treat another's labour as an ordinary sacrifice. While wanting justice for themselves, they may see a relative's privilege as natural.

This attitude cannot be explained solely by a desire for greater benefit. It involves excluding from one's assessment the shared conditions that make one's own life possible. The value of inherited labour, the effects of present action on others and future losses fall behind immediate convenience. This rupture in responsibility lies at the centre of my definition of corruption.

How Does the Snowball Grow?

I liken corruption's spread to a snowball. A person may feel less discomfort on seeing others behave similarly. New participants may draw justifications from the same environment. Over time, behaviour that initially required explanation may become a habit continued without explanation.

The weakening connection between past, present and future matters in this growth. Past labour is treated as a ready-made opportunity. The cost present behaviour imposes on others is underestimated. Future consequences are considered someone else's problem to solve later. The person focuses only on immediate relief or benefit.

This is not a law stating that everyone will behave in the same way. Within the same setting, some may object, set boundaries and try to repair the harm. The snowball analogy describes not inevitable spread but the possibility of people furnishing one another with reasons to evade responsibility. Investigating where and how the spread can be stopped therefore matters as much as examining its spread.

The Abuse Everyone Knows About

Abuse may be sustained through overt coercion or through expectations of preserving a relationship. Someone's repeated willingness to help does not mean unlimited consent to every subsequent request. Yet others may treat previous acceptance as justification for all future duties. Repeated help thus becomes the person's fixed role.

When the person objects, discussion may turn from how the task is distributed to why they no longer behave as before. The person resisting the request, rather than the unfair request itself, is seen as damaging the relationship. The distributional problem remains invisible. Successfully completed work may not reveal what the person undertaking it experienced.

However, not every unequal assignment of tasks is abuse. Explicit commitments, specialist expertise, urgent need or others' unseen burdens may explain the allocation. Assessment cannot rest solely on who works more. A request's grounds, duration, compensation and the consequences of refusal must be examined together.

The Problem of Visibility in Close Relationships

In small, relatively closed communities, people may meet the same individuals in different relationships. An employer, neighbour, relative or association leader may belong to the same social network. In such a context, challenging an injustice may have consequences extending beyond one relationship. This possibility is an explanation requiring investigation; it is not a claim that every small settlement functions in the same way.

Closeness may also provide support and solidarity. Knowing one another is therefore not itself an indicator of corruption. The real question is whether the relationship makes it easier to articulate one's rights. Where familiarity replaces accountability or is used to demand silence from victims, the problem should be sought there.

Nor can everyone remaining silent be assumed to approve. A person may fear losing income, support or safety. Directly attributing corruption to that person may obscure the pressure they face. We must distinguish the attitude sustaining abuse from the situation of someone whose opportunities to resist have narrowed.

Comparison with Sources

In their work on normative conduct, Cialdini, Kallgren and Reno examine the distinction between what people commonly do and what they approve of. Their littering studies discuss the behavioural importance of which norm is at the centre of attention.[1] This distinction can be connected with our essay insofar as it cautions against confusing a practice's prevalence with its acceptability. However, those studies have not directly tested my definition of corruption through civilisation's accumulated achievements.

Bandura's moral-disengagement approach examines how people justify harmful behaviour, diffuse responsibility or minimise the harmful consequences of their actions.[2] The similarity lies in people's ability to loosen the connection between what they do and their own moral evaluation. However, using this concept for every everyday mistake or applying it to a particular person as a diagnosis would be inappropriate.

These two sources do not make the student example a verified psychological experiment. The example is my narrative tool for thinking. The comparison with sources establishes conceptual connections with explanations of norms' influence and disengagement from responsibility. Empirically testing the interpretations offered here requires further work.

Making Questioning Possible Again

Simply telling people to be better does not provide sufficient guidance against invisible abuses. It is necessary to explain which task is requested of whom, why and for how long; recognise contributions; and allow objections to be heard. Separately evaluating completion of shared work and fair distribution of its burdens may make the problem visible.

Continually repeating that wrongdoing is widespread is not itself a solution. Without care, such an account may reinforce the idea that “Nobody behaves differently.” We must therefore also discuss ways to act responsibly, remedy harm and protect those who object.

Valuing civilisation's accumulated achievements does not mean continuing every inherited practice unquestioningly. Recognising and changing an unjust practice may help build a fairer shared life. What I criticise is not change, but destructive indifference to others' rights and the continuation of shared life.

Not Being Alone Does Not Remove Responsibility

Returning to the late student, the friend's arrival relieved a sense of isolation; it did not undo the lateness. In social relationships too, others' participation in the same wrong does not automatically erase one's share. Shared wrongdoing may make the need for evaluation still more important.

My understanding of corruption emphasises people's disregard for civilisation's accumulated labour as they retreat into their immediate selves. As this attitude grows like a snowball, people may reassure one another, but losses of trust, labour and justice continue. Understanding invisible abuses means seeing again who pays the price behind this reassurance.

Preserving shared life cannot be framed as an expectation dependent on everyone being perfect. Space must be opened for recognising mistakes, accepting responsibility and repairing harm. Taking ownership of civilisation's labour begins by taking seriously what our present conduct means for someone else's life and tomorrow's world.
