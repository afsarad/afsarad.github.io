The Human Trace Left in Place: Archaeological Heritage, Personal Meaning-Making and Conservation

Adem Afşar · 13 November 2026

AbstractIntroductionMethod and scopeOriginal remainsPersonal meaning-makingConservationDiscussionConclusionReferences

Abstract

This study discusses how the material relationship between archaeological remains and past human actions can be approached in terms of visitors’ personal meaning-making and the conservation of cultural heritage. Selected theoretical works, a visitor study and primary conservation documents are compared conceptually. The analysis treats historical knowledge, emotional closeness, personal meaning, immediate satisfaction and conservation responsibility as distinct levels of evaluation. Original remains are not expected to affect everyone in the same way, nor is personal interpretation equated with historical accuracy. Visitors’ ability to develop their own meanings through established knowledge and open questions is discussed as a process that may continue after a visit. Its benefits are not presented as empirical findings. The article argues that conservation should look beyond present satisfaction and consider future generations’ opportunities to form different relationships with the same material traces. It presents this relationship as a theoretical synthesis, noting that causal effects and long-term visitor benefits require separate investigation.

Keywords: Sociology of space; archaeological heritage; personal meaning-making; contact value; conservation of cultural heritage.

1. Introduction

When a building disappears, the conditions for approaching the lives once lived there change. Narratives, photographs and documents about it may survive, but later generations’ opportunities to encounter the same material environment diminish. Archaeological remains invite us to consider this relationship over longer periods. A wall, threshold or vessel becomes part of a present encounter as a material entity that participated in past human actions. This study discusses how such encounters relate to personal meaning-making and the value this relationship may hold for conserving cultural heritage.

The significance of a brick in a wall to a visitor may extend beyond its age, material or construction technique. Thinking that a person once held and placed it as part of everyday work may mediate an emotional closeness to the past. What matters in this thought experiment is not whether the builder’s touch left a visible mark. Knowing or perceiving that the object participated in a past action allows a person today to think about the human being who performed it. The encounter may be meaningful to the visitor even without reaching a definite conclusion about the builder’s emotional state.

The thought “That person was human, and so am I” can be considered one starting point for this closeness. Visitors may draw on their own fatigue, joy, care or everyday activities to imagine a past experience. Nevertheless, past human actions may have developed within social relationships and assumptions unfamiliar today. The study considers a sense of familiarity together with openness to historical difference. The personal value of the visitor’s connection and the historical accuracy of a claim about the past are separate matters for evaluation.

Archaeological imagination opens the capacity to work creatively with traces of the past to investigation.1 Sensory archaeology brings the bodily and affective dimensions of an encounter into view.2 The debate between historical empathy and archaeological analogy, meanwhile, problematizes projecting present experiences onto the past.3 In relation to these fields, the study aims to establish a conceptual connection between an object’s material past, visitors’ personal meaning-making and the preservation of future encounters.

The study’s central question is how the same spatial trace can mediate different individuals’ distinctive relationships with the past. Personal interpretation is not treated here as an inner world independent of social conditions. Education, everyday experiences, public heritage narratives and the way a site is presented may influence which details people notice and which questions they can consider. Personal meaning-making thus becomes open to sociological investigation.

The starting assumption is that this encounter is not confined to specialists. A visitor to an archaeological site may formulate personal questions while learning historical information. Remembering, talking and reconsidering after the visit may lead to different interpretations. Evaluating the value of a visit solely through satisfaction at the time may therefore be insufficient. Whether personal meaning developing over time contributes to the perceived value of the visit is a question requiring further research. This text does not claim to have empirically demonstrated such an effect.

In this framework, conserving original remains is considered in terms of sustaining the material basis of future encounters. A replica or digital model can convey appearance and information; it cannot reproduce the original object’s history of actually participating in particular past actions. This does not imply that every visitor will experience a stronger emotion. The justification for conservation is not that everyone should feel the same thing, but that opportunities should remain open for different people to form their own relationships. This justification should be considered alongside scientific knowledge, local communities’ attachments and other conservation values.

The study thus seeks to connect three levels: material traces of past human actions, visitors’ personal meaning-making that can develop over time, and responsibility for keeping these encounters possible in the future. Within this framework, destroying a remain will be discussed as both the loss of a material entity inherited from the past and the disappearance of conditions for encounters that have not yet occurred.

2. Method and scope

This is a theoretical and conceptual study based on comparing selected sources. Its sources comprise related academic approaches and primary policy documents on conservation and interpretation. Literature was accessed through open-web searches, publisher pages, institutional records and available full texts. Versions examined include published articles and policy documents, as well as an accepted author manuscript, a publisher’s book excerpt and an author draft. These version differences are identified in the citations. The initial search and review date is 13 November 2026. This stage cannot be described as a systematic review: neither a comprehensive database search nor a selection process encompassing all publications was undertaken.

The examined texts serve different purposes. Theoretical articles are used to discuss connecting with the past through objects and explanations of value. Visitor research is considered to test general assumptions about experience against findings and limitations. Conservation documents demonstrate the existing normative basis for heritage value, participation, interpretive diversity and intergenerational responsibility. A goal stated in a policy document is not assumed to have been successfully implemented in practice.

The analysis rests on three operations: distinguishing related concepts, comparing explanations in the sources and testing the proposed relationship against counterexamples. Acquiring knowledge, emotional closeness, personal meaning, immediate satisfaction and conservation responsibility are not treated as the same outcome. The framework also includes visitors who find replicas meaningful, are unmoved by original objects or subsequently change their interpretations.

Examples such as a brick placed by a builder are tools for conceptual explanation. They are not presented as systematic field observations, interviews or measured visitor data. Similarly, “That person was human, and so am I” does not claim knowledge of past people’s mental worlds; it describes the possibility of a person today developing closeness through shared humanity.

The study’s limitations are that it produces no empirical data and is not based on a comprehensive literature review. The synthesized approach is not presented as a new, validated model replacing previous theories. In particular, longitudinal research on post-visit meaning-making and theoretical work on future cultural heritage require closer examination. The synthesis is therefore not characterized as a relationship established for the first time in the literature or as a validated causal model.

3. Original remains and relationships with the past

3.1. The existing basis of the conservation debate

Refusing to confine archaeological heritage value to material properties is not a new proposal in conservation. The Nara Document on Authenticity calls for assessments of value and authenticity within their cultural contexts; alongside materials, use, techniques and location, it includes feeling and spiritual qualities among sources that may inform assessment.4 This approach demonstrates the limitation of basing the study solely on interest in original material. The builder’s brick is an example explaining a particular relationship, not the sole valid criterion of authenticity for every culture.

The 2008 ICOMOS Charter on interpretation and presentation also aims to encourage individuals and communities to reflect on their own perceptions of a site, form meaningful connections with it and sustain their interest. It also calls for interpretation to rest on documented knowledge and include alternative historical explanations.5 These principles provide a basis for considering personal meaning-making within an informed and plural interpretive environment.

3.2. The nature of relationships with the same object

A direct theoretical counterpart to this relationship appears in Thomas’s account of “contact value.” Thomas discusses the value arising from objects’ mediation of indirect connections with people, events and concepts; he does not regard authenticity as a property that produces value on its own. The inability to access the past directly is one factor explaining the significance of connections mediated by objects.6 This approach provides the conceptual basis for a possible connection with a brick touched by a builder. Starting from that basis, this study considers the relationship between visitor experience and conservation responsibility.

The relationship opened to discussion here is between an original object’s participation in particular past actions and visitors’ interpretations of that past. A brick having passed through a builder’s hands belongs to the object’s history; a visitor feeling closeness by contemplating this belongs to the encounter’s experiential dimension. These levels cannot be reduced to each other. The object’s historical character does not guarantee that everyone will feel the same emotion; an intense emotion does not itself verify the object’s authenticity.

This distinction does not diminish the importance of an emotional connection. Without establishing what the builder felt, a person can attend to the builder’s action and imagine different possibilities of life. Visitors may draw on everyday experiences while acknowledging that the past person’s social world could differ from their own. What is evaluated here is not only the person’s historical conclusion but also the relationship with their own world formed while thinking about a past person. That relationship itself develops within education, narratives and the conditions of the encounter.

3.3. Thinking about past experience and examining present encounters

Hamilakis does not treat sensory experience as culturally independent or as operating identically everywhere. He distinguishes recognizing past people’s sensory and emotional capacities from claiming that they experienced a particular object in the same way as a person today.2 This approach shows that closeness through shared humanity need not erase historical differences. The expression “That person was human, and so am I” is used here with this distinction maintained.

Lydon likewise distinguishes reconstructing past emotions from researchers’ own emotional engagement with the past, questioning the complete exclusion of the latter’s importance.3 Applied to visitors, this distinction makes “What did the builder actually feel?” and “How does the visitor produce meaning while thinking about the builder?” different research questions. This article focuses on the second; it does not derive the answer to the first from a visitor’s feelings.

In Shanks’s author draft, archaeological encounter is more than completing missing pieces into an image resembling the past; thinking and feeling with remains are considered within the reconstitution of the relationship between past and present.1 Imagination in this study therefore cannot be limited to everyone adopting an image prepared by one person. Images, narratives and encounters with original remains are discussed as forms capable of mediating different kinds of participation.

These affinities show that shared humanity, sensory encounters and present personal engagement already have counterparts in the literature. The article’s task is to evaluate these themes in terms of visitors’ ability to develop their own interpretations and the future-oriented justification of conservation decisions, rather than renaming them to proclaim a new theory.

4. The social conditions of personal meaning-making

4.1. The problem of explaining different experiences

A remain’s participation in past human actions does not mean all visitors will evaluate it identically. The relationship between an object’s past and visitors’ interest in it requires explanation. A person may interpret a wall through construction techniques, everyday labour, aesthetics, family life or another subject. These orientations are possibilities to investigate, not responses assigned in advance to particular occupational, educational or age groups.

In Schwan and Dutz’s study of nine German museums, visitors emphasized original objects’ thought-provoking and curiosity-arousing qualities, found supplementary explanations helpful and accepted replicas under certain conditions. Intense emotional effects did not appear as a general outcome for all original objects.7 These findings do not support the assumption that every original remain necessarily creates a strong emotional bond. Moreover, findings based on museum visitors’ accounts cannot be generalized directly to archaeological sites or long-term post-visit experience.

For this study, personal interpretation is not a result produced inside an individual independently of society. What an object is, why it is conserved and what relationships it may prompt are often understood through learned knowledge. While drawing on personal experience, people may also draw on public historical narratives, family, education and conversations with others. Which factors operate and how cannot be determined from thought experiments alone.

4.2. Participation in meaning-making

Visitors’ ability to formulate their own questions is a form of participation that may be valued within the proposed framework. Its value does not depend on producing the same knowledge as specialists or remaining independent of them. Listening to explanations, re-examining a detail, asking a guide and discussing a companion’s thoughts may all be part of developing a personal interpretation. It is therefore inappropriate to frame expert knowledge and personal imagination as mutually exclusive alternatives.

In the proposed presentation approach, visitors are introduced to a building’s known history and function, while uncertainties are clearly identified. They may then be invited, for example, to consider how everyday life might have unfolded in that space. The question does not require a particular emotion or personal story. People may choose not to reflect or may first ask for more information. Such choices do not automatically mean that the visit has failed.

This openness must maintain the distinction between knowledge and interpretation. Developing an imagination personally valued by a visitor is different from presenting it as a verified explanation of the site’s history. The former opens the experience’s personal value to investigation; the latter requires additional historical evidence. The article concerns how reflection in the first domain may be supported without abandoning scientific knowledge.

4.3. Reinterpretation after a visit

Visitors’ initial interpretations may change in later conversations. Such change does not mean the original experience was worthless or that interpretive authority has passed to someone more knowledgeable. Encountering different explanations may lead people to retain, expand or abandon their first thoughts. “Satisfaction” is not treated here as a goal that everyone must reach at the same level and then bring to a close.

Potentially distinct outcomes for subsequent empirical research are: satisfaction with a visit, learning particular historical facts, finding a place personally meaningful, reflecting again after the visit and developing conservation attitudes. These outcomes will not be assumed to rise together. A visitor may learn much without forming a strong emotional bond; another may remember a place without developing conservation behaviour. These counterpossibilities help test the conditions under which the framework is explanatory.

4.4. Moving toward researchable questions

To open the theoretical argument to empirical investigation, changes after a visit and how they are narrated must be followed. A possible study could record details noticed and questions formed immediately after the visit, then ask several weeks later which thoughts visitors returned to. Later interviews should distinguish spontaneous recollections from reflections prompted by researchers’ questions. The possibility that such follow-up research itself changes the experience must also be considered.

Observing a single group presented with open questions would not suffice to demonstrate that information provision supports personal meaning-making. Comparable presentation conditions and differing visitor expectations should be considered. No claim is made that the possibilities in this section have occurred before interviews are conducted or an appropriate research design established. A theoretical article’s task is to explain relationships and define questions that can test them.

5. Conservation and future encounters

5.1. From present satisfaction to future generations

How strongly present visitors are affected by a remain cannot be the sole criterion for conserving it. Their indifference may relate to inadequate presentation, personal expectations or encounter conditions. Moreover, a relationship not formed today may still form in the future. The reverse also holds: intense interest today does not show that everyone in the future will form the same bond. Thus, while acknowledging the importance of personal experience, conservation’s justification should not require a particular experience.

The Burra Charter relates conserving places of cultural significance for present and future generations to intergenerational equity. It also treats sustaining significant associations and meanings between people and places as a matter of conservation.8 Within this existing approach, this study focuses on the material conditions of personal encounters that have not yet occurred. Although one cannot know which details a future person will value, the irreversible loss of remains can be said to close off certain possibilities for forming relationships.

This argument is not derived solely from an object being old or affecting a visitor. It also requires an ethical premise: future generations’ opportunities to relate to cultural heritage deserve consideration in decision-making. Accepting this premise allows discussion of responsibility beyond present personal satisfaction. The proposition is explicitly normative; it is not presented as a scientific finding arising automatically from a visitor survey.

5.2. Knowledge, personal connections and opportunities for interpretation

Three complementary tasks can be defined between conservation and presentation in this framework. The first is to safeguard the original material existence and context of an object or building. The second is to present known historical information and uncertainties accessibly. The third is to leave room for visitors to develop their own questions from that knowledge. These tasks should not be treated as interchangeable: a good explanation cannot recover a lost original object, and a conserved object does not ensure an intelligible encounter for everyone.

The Faro Convention addresses individual and collective enjoyment of heritage, interpretive diversity and participation. It also states that creating digital content should not adversely affect the conservation of existing heritage.9 These provisions are used here as the approach’s international policy background, not as an assessment of directly enforceable rights in a particular country. This study likewise does not frame digital access and material conservation as opposing alternatives.

5.3. The contribution of representation and the losses of destruction

Photographs, recordings, replicas or digital models may be important means of forming relationships for people unable to reach remains in situ. Personal meaning formed through these media is not assumed to be less valuable. Comparison should consider not only which experience is emotionally stronger but also what material and informational conditions underpin each kind of relationship. An original brick and its image may offer different encounters; both can be meaningful to an individual.

This distinction helps explain the harm of deliberate destruction. When records and narratives survive demolition, not all relationships with the past end. However, some conditions for encounters with the original building’s particular materials, arrangement and location disappear. Subsequent reconstruction may begin a new material and social history; it cannot retrospectively reproduce the destroyed building’s participation in past actions. This loss should be assessed alongside other scientific and community losses associated with the remains.

5.4. Limits of the argument

Valuing future encounters does not imply that every material element must remain unchanged in every circumstance. Decisions must balance safety, maintenance resources, living communities’ needs and different heritage values. The criterion proposed here is that such decisions should not be based solely on current income, visitor numbers or immediate satisfaction. They should also explain which material connections and future opportunities for relationships they sustain or foreclose.

Conservation cannot be equated with fixing cultural meaning within a single narrative. Sustaining material foundations can be considered alongside the possibility of changing interpretations. Later generations are not expected to evaluate the same remains through our interpretation. Intergenerational transmission can thus be defended as transmitting conditions for informed and personal encounters rather than the compulsory transfer of a particular emotion.

6. Discussion

6.1. Material continuity and changing meaning

The proposed synthesis focuses on considering material continuity together with the possibility of changing meaning. Conserving an object need not bind it to a single interpretation. The same brick may raise different questions at different times. One visitor may focus on construction labour, another on the organization of everyday life, and another on the habits of their own time. These possibilities are treated as encounters between an object’s past and visitors’ social experiences, rather than complete transmission of meanings already contained in the object.

In this approach, visitors forming their own interpretations does not mean those interpretations are more valuable than all others. Personal value and comparative superiority are different claims. People may benefit more from an expert account, a friend’s question or a digital presentation. What is defended is recognition that developing personal questions is a legitimate part of visiting, not a requirement that everyone produce an original story.

6.2. Keeping meaning open

A visit ending at a particular moment does not require meaning-making to end then. Visitors may leave a site satisfied and later reconsider the same detail in another conversation. A new interpretation may change the first experience’s scope without invalidating it. Personal satisfaction should therefore be examined as one evaluation that may change over time rather than as the measure of a single required outcome.

This view does not imply that open questions always produce better learning or stronger connections. Uncertainty may arouse curiosity for some visitors and difficulty for others. From a design perspective, one question is how to combine understandable basic information with the freedom to choose one’s own path. The effects of such an arrangement should be evaluated through research encompassing different visitor groups and presentation conditions.

6.3. Visitor benefits and the public justification of conservation

Acknowledging the value of personal meaning-making need not reduce conservation decisions to the sum of individual preferences. Visitors’ assessments of their own experience, the use of public resources and responsibility toward future generations are justified at different levels. Even if remains receive few visitors or limited emotional responses today, they may be significant scientifically, socially or for future encounters.

No necessary chain of behaviour is established here between meaning-making and conservation. It has not been shown that someone who values remains will act to protect them. The article’s normative argument concerns including future opportunities for relationships in decision-making. Whether conservation actually supports personal meaning-making and whether meaning translates into conservation behaviour are separate research questions.

6.4. Scope of the contribution

Contact value, interpretive diversity and intergenerational conservation are existing debates. This article does not claim to discover them for the first time. Its synthesis connects them within the same assessment, clarifying which questions must remain distinct: What is the object’s past? How do visitors relate to it? How does the personal value of that relationship change? Which encounter conditions are sustained for future generations?

The practical implication of these distinctions is to move beyond asking whether a site is informative, impressive or profitable. Access to documented knowledge, participation in different interpretations and continuity of original material context can be evaluated together. This has not been validated as a performance-measurement tool; it is a conceptual proposal that may provide a basis for developing a new assessment tool.

7. Conclusion

Encountering archaeological remains may offer personal relationships alongside knowledge of past human worlds. In the example of a brick placed by a builder, this possibility becomes clear through contemplating the object’s actual participation in a particular human action. Such an encounter cannot be confined to one interpretation requiring specialist expertise; different people may develop different questions from their own experiences.

This study has considered three levels together: remains’ material past, visitors’ personal meanings formed and changed within social conditions, and future generations’ opportunities to encounter these material traces. These levels must not be reduced to one another. Authenticity alone does not guarantee intense emotion; personal satisfaction does not mean historical accuracy; present visitor satisfaction does not exhaust the reasons for conservation.

The article argues that conserving original remains should sustain material conditions for different future meanings as well as transmitting present meanings. Rather than expecting subsequent generations to experience the same emotion or interpretation, conservation may be justified in ways that leave room for their informed and personal encounters. Although losing remains does not end every narrative, it may remove the foundation for relationships with material entities that participated in a particular past.

The proposed approach rests on a conceptual synthesis of selected sources. How post-visit reflection becomes a personal benefit, the role of different presentations in that process and the relationship between meaning-making and conservation behaviour have not been empirically tested. Longitudinal visitor research is needed to determine the framework’s explanatory strength and limits.
