Political Stability and Social Dissatisfaction

Adem Afşar · 7 November 2026

At first glance, citizens complaining about their lives while major investments are undertaken may seem contradictory. Someone benefiting from new transport opportunities may struggle to meet household expenses the same day. A voter supporting continuity in government may be upset by treatment at a public institution. These feelings coexisting in one person do not mean that the person does not know what they want. Social life is too multifaceted to fit a single measure of satisfaction.

The importance I attach to political stability also draws on encounters with initiatives left unfinished in the past. Completing a promised undertaking influences how a new promise is received. However, trust created by completed projects and assessment of current hardships must be considered together. Ignoring the first diminishes social memory; failing to hear the second makes future needs harder to understand.

The social value of continuity

Preparing a major project, securing agreement among institutions, finding resources and completing implementation may take a long time. The risk of starting over at every stage is not solely the project team’s problem. Those awaiting the service also bear delays’ time and opportunity costs. Political continuity’s value thus becomes tangible for citizens seeking to plan everyday life.

I am not arguing that the same administration remaining in office alone guarantees success. Continuity offers an opportunity insofar as it preserves accumulated expertise, enables responsibility to be followed through and helps institutions work toward shared goals. How fully that opportunity is used can be understood through completed work, service quality and resource use.

The World Bank also treats political stability and government effectiveness as separate dimensions. The former concerns perceptions of the risk of political authority being destabilized through violence or unconstitutional means; the latter concerns public services, policy implementation and the credibility of decisions. An administration’s tenure is therefore not a direct equivalent of these indicators.[1] This distinction requires explaining the outcomes with which we associate continuity’s value.

Trust created by fulfilled promises

Finding a leader worthy of support may shape citizens’ future expectations. In the understanding of leadership I value, standing behind decisions and taking responsibility for work begun are central. Citizens need not know every investment’s technical details. They can nevertheless follow the progress of promises and how these eventually affect their lives.

A fulfilled promise may increase initial trust in a subsequent project. This is an understandable evaluation based on personal experience. Yet past success cannot prove every future decision correct in advance. A new investment’s necessity, cost and effects must be examined on its own terms. Trust can help make this scrutiny more open and constructive rather than unnecessary.

Leadership’s lasting value also appears here. Determination gains social significance when combined with achievable goals, adequate knowledge and institutions accepting responsibility. Explaining problems and changing methods when necessary can demonstrate that a promise is taken seriously. Sustaining a goal and improving the way to reach it can complement each other.

Why do completed services become ordinary?

A service initially fills a major gap. Over time, it may become an ordinary part of daily life. People evaluate it through the quality of its opportunities rather than imagining its absence. Once a transport connection exists, questions of waiting time, transfers, accessibility and fares may take priority. This is a thought experiment illustrating changing expectations, not a measured finding that all citizens experience the same process.

This creates an important challenge for government: a service regarded as a major achievement yesterday may be seen today as a baseline to maintain. Becoming ordinary does not mean becoming worthless. It may also show that people have begun organizing their lives around that opportunity. Public responsibility thus includes reliable continuation after a service opens.

Evaluations may therefore differ between those who knew deprivation and those born into the service’s availability. One compares present opportunities with past shortcomings; another compares them with other cities or personal future goals. Explaining differences in satisfaction solely through personality or intentions is incomplete without understanding the comparison being made.

National gains and household experience

A public investment’s significance for society does not mean every household benefits simultaneously and equally. A worker’s transport may become easier while housing costs rise. Someone living near a new service may experience it differently from someone far away. A project’s long-term benefits and citizens’ current payment difficulties may unfold on different timescales.

Describing national achievements must therefore accompany understanding household difficulties. Benefiting from one service does not invalidate hardship in another area. Similarly, encountering a problem in one field does not prove every nationwide effort unsuccessful. The concrete question is who can access which service and who bears which burden.

This approach also accounts for limited public resources. Every demand may not be met simultaneously. Explaining priorities’ rationale, honestly describing delays and attending to fair distribution of burdens may strengthen a decision’s acceptability. Understanding citizens’ expectations does not mean meeting every expectation unconditionally.

Support and criticism can coexist in one person

Voters may support the government because they approve the country’s general direction while seeking changes in education, livelihoods or local services. Such an attitude may demand both preservation of existing gains and remedying shortcomings. Reading voters solely as supporters or opponents obscures these two dimensions.

While defending continuity, I also consider citizens’ right to criticism valuable for understanding government’s future. Voicing a problem may show that someone wanting it solved is still seeking a responsive authority. Of course, criticism’s accuracy requires investigation. However, the critic’s political alignment should not determine whether a complaint is examined.

Opposition citizens’ experiences should receive consideration in public services alongside objections from government supporters. Services’ shared character requires this. Fair access to the same institution for people with different political preferences is a fundamental measure preserving the possibility of living together. Strong government’s social significance should also be sought in its ability to extend this assurance across groups.

Being heard and receiving a response

Addressing dissatisfaction may sometimes depend on a simple administrative response before a major investment: not losing an application, providing a reasoned answer, correcting an error or explaining waiting times. Seeing an institution acknowledge a problem is an important part of citizens’ relationship with public authority. The distance between registering a complaint and resolving it should also be tracked.

The OECD’s 2024 trust report, based on research in 30 countries, addresses the importance of everyday service experiences, responsiveness, fair treatment and the perception that citizens’ voices are heard.[2] This international framework is not used here as evidence of a Turkey-specific proportion or causal relationship. The article argues that relationships with citizens should be evaluated alongside the quantity of services.

An institution listening to a complaint does more than answer one person. It may also learn where similar problems concentrate. A long-serving administration has an opportunity to accumulate this knowledge and turn it into improvements. Continuity can thus develop into a capacity for government to learn from experience rather than merely repeat past practices.

Extending stability into the future

The importance I attach to political stability rests on society moving forward while preserving its gains. Completed work deserves recognition, while new needs arising afterward deserve careful attention. Fulfilling yesterday’s promise creates trust. Responding to today’s problems is one responsibility determining how that trust continues into the future.

Viewed this way, social dissatisfaction shows that not every objection means weariness with stability. Some trust the current administration and expect better; others develop different preferences because they see insufficient benefit in their own lives. Understanding these differences is a more realistic starting point than imagining society shares a single emotion. Continuity’s lasting value grows insofar as it responds to the needs of changing lives.
