Adem Afşar · Conceptual Study · 1 November 2026

Do people determine their behaviour through their own choices, or are they guided by the expectations of the society in which they live?

Is freedom merely the ability to choose? Or is being able to assume the consequences of a choice also an inseparable dimension of freedom?

This study discusses the relationship between individual choice and social responsibility through the effects of norms, culture and moral consistency.

1. Are human beings the same everywhere?

Human beings are the same everywhere; the conditions they produce to sustain their lives differ.
— Adem Afşar

This proposition does not claim that people exhibit the same behaviour in every society.

Basic human needs such as food, shelter, security and sustaining life may have common features.

However, the institutions, values, habits and social relationships created to meet these needs differ according to historical and geographical conditions.

Common biological characteristics and cultural differences should therefore be treated not as opposites, but as phenomena to be examined together.

2. Freedom and choice

Freedom is closely related to the individual’s ability to make choices.

Not every choice, however, takes place under equal conditions.

Economic resources, education, law, social pressure, family relationships and political conditions can broaden or narrow the options before an individual.

The fact that behaviour appears to have been chosen therefore does not prove the choice was made independently of all pressures.

Formal freedom of choice

Choosing an option being legally or apparently possible.

Practical opportunity to choose

The individual being able genuinely to consider and implement that option under existing conditions.

3. The formation of social norms

Social norms shape which behaviours are considered appropriate, expected or acceptable in a given society.

Individuals may learn these norms through family, education, everyday relationships and social institutions.

Émile Durkheim’s approach to social facts is important for examining social rules that exist outside individuals and can affect their behaviour.

A norm’s acceptance by society, however, does not in itself show that it is morally right.

4. How human is a person when alone?

A person is as human as they are when alone.
— Adem Afşar

This proposition does not mean that an individual loses their quality of being human when alone.

What is questioned here is the extent to which the values an individual professes under others’ scrutiny coincide with the behaviour they display when scrutiny is absent.

For example, there is a difference in moral consistency between valuing honesty only when there is a chance of being caught and maintaining the same standard when no one is watching.

However, it would be wrong to reach a definitive judgment about someone’s entire moral character from a single action performed alone.

5. Moral consistency and social control

Social control can contribute to behaviour conforming to particular rules.

Nevertheless, the reason for following rules is not always the same.

An individual may exhibit similar behaviour because they fear punishment, wish to avoid social exclusion or genuinely embrace the relevant rule.

Since outwardly similar behaviours can have different reasons behind them, looking only at their appearance is insufficient.

6. Are every society’s norms the same?

Societies can differ in their historical experiences, beliefs, economic conditions and cultural relationships.

Behaviour regarded as ordinary in one society may therefore be disapproved of in another.

The existence of cultural differences, however, does not mean every practice is exempt from criticism.

Questions concerning individuals’ right to life, safety, freedom and human dignity must be assessed separately, beyond social acceptance.

7. Social acceptance and moral rightness

Behaviour widespread in a society and morally defensible behaviour may not always coincide.

The historical normalisation of discrimination or inequality in particular societies does not show those practices to be fair.

Sociological explanation must therefore be distinguished from moral assessment.

Sociological question

Under what conditions do people adopt and maintain a particular behaviour?

Moral question

How should this behaviour’s consequences for others be assessed?

8. Does freedom remove responsibility?

The individual’s ability to make choices about their own life is an important dimension of freedom.

Some choices, however, do not affect only the person making them.

The question of responsibility arises in behaviour affecting other people’s rights, safety or shared living conditions.

John Stuart Mill’s discussions of freedom provide an important basis for comparison in examining the relationship between individual choice and harm to others.

However, the boundaries of the concept of harm and society’s authority to intervene must be discussed separately.

9. Connection with circles of responsibility

An individual’s responsibilities to themselves, their family, their immediate surroundings and wider society can arise in different forms.

This approach is examined in greater detail in the study entitled Circles of Responsibility.

Prioritising close relationships does not justify disregarding other individuals’ fundamental rights.

The relationship between freedom and responsibility is therefore not limited to an individual’s immediate surroundings.

10. Opposing views and limits

The approach to moral consistency defended in this study remains open to certain questions.

Human behaviour can change under different conditions. Fear, pressure, deprivation and lack of knowledge can affect individual decisions.

When assessing someone’s behaviour, therefore, we should consider not only its outcome but also their circumstances.

Furthermore, an individual’s opposition to society’s prevailing norms does not always mean irresponsibility.

In some cases, opposing an unjust social rule may be a requirement of moral responsibility.

Main research question

Within what limits can an individual’s moral consistency be assessed by comparing their behaviour under social scrutiny with their behaviour when scrutiny is absent?

11. Conclusion

People may share basic needs for living, but the ways of meeting them differ according to social conditions.

Individual freedom relates to the opportunity to choose. Responsibility requires assessing the consequences of those choices and their effects on others.

Moral consistency helps us question the relationship between the values an individual professes and their actions.

Human freedom becomes visible in the ability to choose; responsibility becomes visible in the relationship established with the consequences of that choice.

This conclusion is the study’s conceptual assessment, not a direct quotation from another thinker.

12. Academic sources

The following works are initial sources to be compared in detail in subsequent work. This list does not mean that all the text’s claims have yet been referenced at page level.

  1. Durkheim, Émile (1895). Les Règles de la méthode sociologique. A foundational work on defining and examining social facts.
  2. Mill, John Stuart (1859). On Liberty. A foundational work on individual freedom, social intervention and the harm principle.
  3. Fromm, Erich (1941). Escape from Freedom. An examination of relationships between freedom, insecurity and the turn towards authority.
  4. Bandura, Albert (1991). Social cognitive theory of moral thought and action. In: Handbook of Moral Behavior and Development, Volume 1: Theory. Lawrence Erlbaum Associates. A theoretical study of the self-regulatory dimension of moral behaviour.

13. Academic development plan

Subsequent work will systematically distinguish the concepts of social norms, moral judgment, individual freedom and responsibility.

The approaches of Durkheim, Mill and Fromm will be compared through their original works; page numbers of the editions used will be verified.

The conditions under which the proposition concerning moral consistency can become a researchable hypothesis will also be discussed.

This text is a conceptual study under development, rather than a completed peer-reviewed academic article.
