Crying and Dancing to the Same Song: Sorrow, Rhythm and Emotional Transformation in Hakim’s “Nar”

Adem Afşar · Interdisciplinary Sociological Analyst · 23 November 2026

In a song about romantic pain, the word “fire” rises again and again, while guests at a wedding or henna night may dance to the same recording with delight. One listener follows the lyrics and feels close to the abandoned narrator; another, not knowing Arabic, is carried by the rhythm without decoding the words; a third places the feeling of a personal separation, unemployment or bereavement inside the sound. Hakim’s 1994 song Nar offers a striking example of these responses occurring side by side.

The question is not who listens “correctly”: How can one song be a lament, a bodily exhilaration and a shared celebration at the same time? From a social-psychological perspective, the answer lies neither in the lyrics alone nor in the rhythm alone, but in the meeting of words, sound, bodies, listeners’ histories and the social setting in which the music is played.

Method and limits: This is an interpretive essay grounded in published research on music and social psychology; it is not a survey or field study of listeners. The author’s observation that Hakim’s song is danced to enthusiastically at engagements, weddings and henna nights in his local social environment is presented as a local observation, not as representative of all listeners. The lyrics are not reproduced in full; their meaning is summarised.

“Nar”: the pain told and the meaning left open

Nar means “fire” in Arabic. The song’s narrator describes being unable to reach the beloved, being forgotten, and experiencing unreciprocated attachment through calling, need, sleeplessness and tears. Repetitions of “fire” lift the feeling beyond one event and turn it into an image of growing intensity that surrounds mind and body. The official video description credits Amal el-Tayer with the lyrics, Essam Tawfik with the composition and Hamid el-Shaeri with the arrangement.

The lyrics do not explicitly say: “You were not worth it; I am glad you left; now I am dancing.” That sentence from our earlier conversation is not a literal translation. It is a possible listener’s counter-response to explain the lively arrangement: instead of freezing inside pain, the listener returns to the body and to life through rhythm. The interpretation is possible, but it is not evidence of Hakim’s or the lyricist’s intention.

The same caution applies to “manipulation.” The lyrics establish that the narrator feels neglected and left without reciprocity. They do not independently prove that the beloved deliberately manipulated the narrator. The song makes the narrator’s complaint public; it gives listeners room to recognise the pain, share it or create distance from it. Expressing a feeling powerfully and dramatically does not, by itself, amount to manipulation.

Shaʿbi: more than a translation of “folk music”

Shaʿbi literally means “of the people” or “popular.” It should not be treated as a single, unchanging folklore genre. Shaʿbi is an urban popular-music field that became especially visible in Cairo from the late 1960s onward. It carries everyday life, love, livelihood, desire and street language, bringing rural and local musical elements together with new sounds circulating in the city. Its history of production and listening runs from the cassette era to digital circulation, and from street weddings to mass media.

The social meaning of this music is inseparable from debates about class. Research based on 21 months of ethnographic fieldwork in Cairo’s shaʿbi scene from 2018 to 2020 records an instance in which Hakim’s song was chosen for dancing at a party in an educated, cosmopolitan home. The same study discusses how shaʿbi can be associated with lower classes, while more “acceptable” or “cleaned-up” commercial forms are fashioned to reach other classes. This instance does not represent the preferences of every educated Egyptian listener. It does, however, show that class distance does not determine musical taste absolutely: the listening context and arrangement matter too.

Hakim’s career is described as moving from singing at street weddings to mass success. In an Afropop interview, Hakim speaks of shaʿbi as a genre that deals with everyday life, love, longing and jealousy, and of working through conversation and learning with musicians. His creative relationship with Hamid el-Shaeri suggests that popular street performance and studio production are not sealed-off worlds. The idea of a “synthesis between trained musicians and street musicians” is a productive interpretation, but we do not present it as a verified biographical claim about Hakim’s formal musical education.

Mawwal: a vocal space opened before the beat

Mawwal is a vocal form in Arabic music, particularly in Egyptian contexts, often sung in colloquial Arabic, with a free rhythm and room for improvisation. Unlike a dance beat bound to a regular metre, the performer can stretch syllables, bend the melody and use wordplay or vocal ornament to establish complaint, longing or emotional intensity. In shaʿbi repertoires, a mawwal sometimes serves as an introduction before a faster, metrical main song: first a free, verbal emotional space, followed by the regular rhythm carried by the darbuka and other percussion.

This distinction matters because rhythm is not merely a background to the song. A regular beat can carry the lament established in free vocal phrasing into a different bodily time. But we do not classify Nar as a mawwal without identifying such an introductory improvisatory section in the recording. Mawwal is used here not as the song’s genre label, but as a comparative concept that helps explain a historical and aesthetic passage between lament and dance rhythm in Egyptian popular music.

Arabic-speaking and non-Arabic-speaking listeners: one recording, different access

An Arabic-speaking listener can follow the narrator’s story of abandonment and unreciprocated love, and hear the narrative weight of the fire refrain, being forgotten and sleeplessness. A listener who does not know Arabic may not access the words, but can follow vocal intensity, melodic tension, repetition, percussion and accents that move the body. They hear the same track, but do not have equal access to the same layer of the music.

Language knowledge is not the only factor. A non-Arabic speaker may know the song through a translation; an Arabic speaker at a wedding may join the dance without following the text. Another listener has not been left by a lover but connects the narrator’s deprivation with losing a job, losing someone close or working for a long time without recognition. The events do not become identical; their emotional shapes intersect. The source differs, while longing, lack of reciprocity, feeling devalued or seeking consolation in loss can feel familiar.

The listener does not translate the event in the song into their own life word for word. They take a piece of feeling from their own experience and connect it to the song’s rhythm and sound. For someone who has lost a loved one, the music may call up the weight of absence rather than romantic rejection. For someone who has become unemployed, the salient feeling may be effort without return rather than abandonment. Consolation does not mean the same outcome for everyone: music may bring closeness and relief to some, movement and energy to others, and deeper sadness to others.

How can pain accompany exhilaration?

Listening is not a single-channel process. While the words report pain, tempo, percussion, regular repetition and melodic movement can invite the body to dance. The listener receives both signals at once. The recording need not mean that “sadness turns into happiness.” It may be a musical situation that moves the body while telling of sadness, even holding pain and pleasure together. Psychological research on sad music finds different rewards reported by listeners, including imagination, empathy, emotion regulation and consolation; responses nevertheless vary by person and situation.

The bodily side of rhythm becomes especially visible at a wedding or henna night. Rhythm can bring steps and clapping into a shared time. Experimental studies of dance and social bonding report that synchronised movement can increase feelings of closeness among participants. A person is not only listening to a separation story; they are also moving with people around them. This togetherness produces a shared feeling distinct from the narrator’s personal pain: “We are living this moment together.”

The author’s observation that Nar is danced to enthusiastically at engagements, weddings and henna nights in his local community is meaningful here. In these settings, the song’s function is not to make guests relive the abandonment story as if it were their own event. The track joins the celebratory rhythm; it can be used to enjoy the beat, enter the group dance and show bodily energy. Even when listeners know the words, dancing does not mean that they are “celebrating the pain.” Dance can accompany the story without erasing its pain, or carry the body for a while into another experiential space.

Telling pain loudly: lament, performance, solidarity

Complaint and lament are not only private disclosures; they are forms of address to listeners. The story “I loved, called and waited, but found no response” makes the abandoned person’s experience audible before others. Repetition may be answered or sung along with by some listeners, allowing an individual’s pain to become part of a shared voice. This can be read less as a final verdict condemning the other person than as a dramatic call that makes the narrator’s experience visible.

Not every culture or community does this in the same way; a broad claim such as “Arab culture expresses pain exuberantly” would be inaccurate. The urban history of Egyptian shaʿbi, wedding repertoire, mawwal tradition, oral forms of complaint and modern pop arrangements form a particular field of expression. That field changes over time and varies with class, generation, media and place.

Blues, rock’n’roll and New Orleans: similarities and differences

Your example of the Blues offers a useful comparison for thinking about sorrow and rhythm together. Blues in the United States has historical connections to African American work songs, field hollers, spirituals and other Black musical traditions. But it would be inaccurate to portray blues as an unchanging genre directly produced by enslaved people working in cotton fields. The Library of Congress explains that blues took shape in the American South in later periods, around the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, in different work and community settings. The shared point is that pain is carried through rhythm and sound; the histories and social conditions are not the same.

Your Elvis Presley example also shows how rhythm and lyrical expectation can diverge: the lyrics of “Blue Suede Shoes” revolve around shoes and the wish to protect them, while the song’s physical energy and rock’n’roll gait make it danceable. In Nar, movement similarly exists alongside painful lyrical content in the same recording. We do not treat one example as the direct equivalent of the other; the histories of the genres differ.

A New Orleans jazz funeral offers a sharper comparison. The procession to the cemetery often begins with a slow, mournful dirge; after burial, the music may speed up and the procession can turn into dance and street celebration. This is a transition from one stage to another within a social ritual. In Nar, the shift does not occur in the sequence of a ceremony, but in the simultaneity of the painful narrative and lively rhythm in one song. In both cases music can carry loss or pain into a social body; one shows transformation within a ritual, the other two emotional layers working at once.

The marginal transformation: the person who cries and the person who dances

The sociological surprise is not the claim that the song moves in one direction “from sadness to happiness.” What is marginal and striking is that, within the same musical event, one person may cry with the narrator’s pain, another may dance to the rhythm, and a third may feel both. A listener need not remain on just one side; the same person may move closer to the pain in the words and, a moment later, surrender to the beat.

The counter-response you proposed—“I made so many sacrifices for you; you were not worth it; I am glad you left; now I am dancing”—is not a literal translation of the song, but a possible listener position created by movement. From that position, dance can be read less as delight at the beloved’s departure than as a refusal to let pain govern the whole person: a gesture of returning to one’s body, surroundings and participation in life. This is an interpretation; we cannot claim every person who dances is thinking this.

Human beings are the same everywhere and at all times, but the ways they experience and share emotion are socially shaped. One person’s heartbreak can intersect with another’s unemployment or grief; rhythm can carry these different stories into a shared time. The need for consolation may be familiar, but the form of consolation varies by person. Perhaps this is where Nar draws its power: it does not extinguish the “fire,” yet it can move the body alongside it, leaving listeners room both to approach the pain and to move through it.

A song’s meaning is formed not only by its lyrics, but through the listener’s language, life experience, rhythm and social setting. The same recording may evoke memory and sadness in one person, and shared movement or joy in another. Without reducing these responses to one correct reaction, I ask how people carry emotion through social practices.
