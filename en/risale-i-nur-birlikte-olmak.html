Does Being Together Require Becoming Alike?

A sociological perspective on the Risale-i Nur Collection

Adem Afşar · 17 November 2026

People want to be among others who understand them. Yet being understood and becoming alike are different things. Joining a community may ease our loneliness, while we may also want to retain the freedom to ask our own questions. How these two needs are met is a central research question in the sociology of religious associations: how can people build relationships with others while continuing to take responsibility for their own thinking?

The Twenty-First Lem’a of Risale-i Nur, known as the İhlâs Risalesi, offers a starting point for considering this question. Here, sincerity is linked to making Allah’s approval the purpose of religious service; brotherhood, solidarity and moving away from the desire for personal superiority are emphasised. These are the text’s religious and moral counsels. What they may mean for social relationships requires a separate interpretation.[1]

The presence of a counsel does not demonstrate that everyone who accepts it lives accordingly. We are therefore not presenting field research findings about the everyday life of a particular community. We examine the form of relationship proposed by the text and the sociological questions it raises.

Making room within brotherhood

In the Twenty-First Lem’a, Bediüzzaman Said Nursi writes, in our English rendering:

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · Lem’alar · Twenty-First Lem’a · English rendering

The foundation of our way is brotherhood.

Uhuvvet means brotherhood. The same section foregrounds close friendship, selfless companionship and appreciation of another person’s qualities. Later pages stress helping a brother and complementing his service.[2]

Sociologically, this approach invites us to consider how people position one another. Someone who continually sees another person as a competitor may experience that person’s success as a threat. When the same person is seen as a companion contributing to a shared undertaking, that success may instead mean a strengthening of the common work.

Consider a reading group. One person may be better at explaining the vocabulary, another at researching the historical context, and another at finding examples from everyday life. When these differences become a ranking of superiority, people may turn to protecting their speaking time and position. When they are recognised as complementary contributions, opportunities for learning may expand. This is a constructed example for comparing relationships, rather than an observation of a particular group.

How far does other people’s approval govern our behaviour?

In the İhlâs Risalesi, the desire for fame and public attention is discussed among the factors that may undermine sincerity.[3] This warning raises a question for people today: how far do we judge the value of our work by the attention it receives?

It is understandable to want one’s effort to be recognised. The problem becomes clearer when recognition becomes the principal measure of behaviour. When help is expected to be seen, knowledge to confer superiority, or a contribution always to be associated with the contributor’s name, a shared purpose may become an arena for personal visibility.

Starting from Bediüzzaman Said Nursi’s warning within its religious framework, the following sociological interpretation by Adem Afşar can be developed: sustaining an association depends on more than its members expressing the same purpose. How recognition, opportunities to speak and work are shared also shapes the quality of relationships. People should be able to feel that their contributions are valued and that acknowledging another person’s contribution does not diminish them.

The tension between solidarity and questioning

Reading the text in the present also requires considering a tension. The second principle advises against criticising one’s brothers; the surrounding explanation criticises rivalry, domination and discouraging another person’s efforts.[1]

Can we therefore say that every form of criticism is of the same kind? Is finding faults to humiliate someone the same as giving reasons for identifying a mistake in shared work?

Our present sociological assessment needs to preserve this distinction. For solidarity to endure, being able to discuss mistakes matters alongside avoiding harm to one another. This cannot be presented as an explicitly formulated doctrine of freedom of criticism within the text; it is a contemporary question we put to its counsels.

Similarly, alongside statements foregrounding brotherhood, there are warnings portraying departure from the community as risky in terms of religious struggle.[2] It would therefore be incomplete to read the text solely through sentences that appear to affirm today’s understanding of individual autonomy. Both the ideal of solidarity and the strong warnings intended to preserve association must be considered.

The same text, different lives

Two people reading the same page need not be affected by the same point. One may focus on loneliness, another on rivalry. Age, education, material circumstances and personal experiences may shape the questions readers put to a text. A shared text can provide a meeting place where these differences can be discussed.

Here we must distinguish personal meaning from a claim about what the text says. “This sentence made me think of this” describes the reader’s experience. “The author definitely means this here” requires context and textual evidence. This distinction both makes room for the reader’s inner life and limits our reconstruction of the text to suit our preferred meanings.

Brotherhood is thus a fruitful subject for a sociological reading of the Risale-i Nur Collection. How is association established, how is rivalry managed, and how are different contributions received? Understanding how the textual ideal takes shape in everyday life also requires examining readers’ experiences, relationships and concrete practices.

The distance between being together and becoming alike emerges within precisely these relationships.
