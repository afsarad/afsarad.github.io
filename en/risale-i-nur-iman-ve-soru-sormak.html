Faith and Asking Questions

Is Seeking Understanding the Same as Objecting?

Does asking about a religious matter one does not understand mean rejecting it? Does describing a thought that passes through one’s mind mean accepting it? In a shared reading setting, the response to a question may affect not only what someone knows, but also how they experience their place in that setting.

Different texts in the Risale-i Nur Collection can be brought into relation with these questions. Read together, examined faith, the distinction between vesvese and assent, fairness in debate and the author’s approach to shortcomings in his own expression open a field of reflection that cannot be captured in a single sentence. We examine six passages in context, distinguishing the source text from the sociological interpretations developed through it.

1. Tahkikî iman: Learning continues

In the concluding notice to the Thirty-Three Windows included in Sözler, Bediüzzaman Said Nursi describes how the same text serves different readers. In our English rendering:

Source passage 1 · Read on this page

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · English translation of the source passage · Source and page reference

It makes the faith of someone whose faith is strong but based on imitation into examined faith. It broadens the faith of someone whose faith is already examined.

Kavî means strong; taklidî here indicates acceptance received from others; tahkikî refers to faith grounded through inquiry and evidence. The passage neither equates strong acceptance with examination nor presents examined faith as the end of all learning: it also speaks of faith broadening.

The same notice states that once the intellect is satisfied, the heart, spirit and imagination also seek their share. Faith is therefore not reduced here to memorising a proposition. The author describes a reading intended to foster development in different dimensions of the reader.

Drawing on this passage, a question may also be understood as an expression of a person’s desire to understand an existing conviction more fully. Treating every question in advance as rejection may prevent us from recognising the person’s situation and need. This final assessment is our sociological interpretation.

2. How is a student’s question received?

The Sixth Matter of Meyve Risalesi, the Eleventh Şuâ in Şuâlar, begins with a concrete encounter. This is our English rendering:

Source passage 2 · Read on this page

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · English translation of the source passage · Source and page reference

Some high-school students in Kastamonu came to me. They said, ‘Introduce us to our Creator; our teachers do not speak of Allah.’ I said: Each of the sciences you study continually speaks of Allah in its own particular language and makes the Creator known. Listen to them, rather than to the teachers.

Hâlık means Creator; lisan-ı mahsus means a particular language; mütemadiyen means continually. The discussion proceeds through examples of a pharmacy, factory and provisioning, developing an explanation concerning the Creator from the order perceived in nature. This is a religious interpretation of scientific knowledge, rather than a contemporary experimental research report.

In this encounter, the students’ request is met with an explanation. The response connects with the disciplines they study. Within the text, the question becomes the starting point for explanation.

This does not justify a judgement about all teachers or all reading communities. But it allows us to examine a relationship: what does answering through connections with what a person already knows suggest about learning? What relationship is formed by listening to the questioner as someone seeking understanding, rather than merely as someone to be silenced?

3. A passing thought and assent are different

The Fifth Aspect of the Second Station of the Twenty-First Söz addresses vesvese appearing as doubt in matters of faith. Bediüzzaman Said Nursi writes, in our English rendering:

Source passage 3 · Read on this page

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · English translation of the source passage · Source and page reference

Just as imagining unbelief is not unbelief, apprehending unbelief is not unbelief either. Conceiving error is not error; reflecting on error is not error either. For imagination, apprehension, conception and reflection are distinct from intellectual assent and conviction of the heart; they are different things.

Tahayyül means imagining; tasavvur refers to conceiving a meaning in the mind; tefekkür means reflecting on it. Tasdik is accepting something as true; iz’an conveys inward conviction and firm acceptance. The central distinction is that not every content arising in the mind counts as a belief accepted by the person.

The section begins by describing someone who is distressed because they take an unwanted thought to mean that their faith has been harmed. Its primary addressee is therefore a person troubled by a thought, rather than someone accepting it. We should not turn this account of vesvese into a label applied to everyone who asks a question. A question seeking knowledge, a reasoned objection and an unwanted thought cannot be collapsed into one category.

The continuation’s boundary must also be recognised: Bediüzzaman Said Nursi warns that unnecessary repetition may develop into settled doubt, and that adopting the opposing position in the name of impartiality may create danger. The passage thus distinguishes thought from assent while retaining its purpose of preserving faith.

4. What is gained in debate: appearing right or learning?

While discussing how rivalry in religious service harms sincerity, the Twentieth Lem’a presents a criterion for the ethics of debate. After criticising delight in one’s own victory and satisfaction at the other person’s error, it states, in our English rendering:

Source passage 4 · Read on this page

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · English translation of the source passage · Source and page reference

If truth emerges on the opponent’s side, one benefits without loss by learning something previously unknown and is freed from the pride of the self. Thus a fair seeker of truth sets aside the self’s preference for the sake of truth. Seeing truth on the opponent’s side, one willingly accepts and supports it, and is pleased.

Hakperest means someone devoted to truth; insaf is fairness in judgement; hasım is the opposing party in a discussion. In this passage, an opponent being right is not described merely as defeat: learning something previously unknown is treated as a gain.

This does not mean accepting every opposing view without seeking evidence. The criterion is not submission because of the speaker’s identity, but the ability to accept truth wherever one recognises it.

Applied to the relationship between an explainer and a questioner in a reading community, this criterion invites examination of how a question can become a contest over standing. Acknowledging that one does not know, or revising an earlier interpretation, cannot be understood solely as loss of authority through this criterion. The same responsibility applies to the person raising an objection.

5. Respect for the author and responsibility for understanding

In the same concluding notice to the Thirty-Three Windows, after mentioning the speed of composition, Bediüzzaman Said Nursi qualifies his own manner of expression as follows. This is our English rendering:

Source passage 5 · Read on this page

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · English translation of the source passage · Source and page reference

there will certainly be confusion and shortcomings in the manner of expression that belongs to me. I ask my brothers to view these with tolerance, to correct them if they can, and to pray for my forgiveness.

Müşevveşiyet means confusion; müsamaha means tolerance or understanding; ıslah means correction. The quotation concerns the author’s manner of expression in the particular text. Presenting it as permission to alter all principles of faith would exceed its context.

Nevertheless, the passage allows us to consider that respect for an author does not require treating every expression as already understood. Asking for a word to be explained, questioning the connection within a sentence or pointing out an unclear expression need not amount to belittlement.

Sociologically, distinguishing a person from an expectation of faultlessness attributed to them matters here. How respect is expressed, how requests for explanation are received and how correcting a mistake is understood within a relationship require separate investigation.

6. Preserving the Collection’s framework of faith

These passages do not establish that the Collection evaluates every form of questioning in the same way. The First Part of the Twenty-Sixth Mektub discusses a claim to viewing the Qur’an impartially. In our English rendering:

Source passage 6 · Read on this page

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · English translation of the source passage · Source and page reference

For viewing the Qur’an as human speech and reasoning in that way takes the opposing alternative as its basis. It is a commitment to falsehood, not impartiality; rather, it is taking the side of falsehood.

The attitude criticised here adopts the assumption that the Qur’an is human speech at the outset and presents this as impartiality. The text speaks from a framework of faith defending the Qur’an as divine speech.

We should not exceed that context in either direction. The passage does not establish that everyone asking about a word or a proof is being accused. Nor do the earlier passages on learning and debate establish that the author treats all metaphysical starting points as equivalent. The kind of question, its initial assumption and the text’s addressee need to be distinguished.

A sociological essay grounded in the Collection should not dissolve these differences into a single slogan. Its explanatory and investigative dimensions should be read together with its purpose of preserving faith.

7. Listening to the questioner in shared reading

Reading these six passages together enables concrete questions about relationships with readers seeking understanding: is the purpose of a question heard? Is a request for explanation distinguished from rejection of a claim? Can participants return to sources when something is unknown? Can a correction be accepted without being turned into a personal defeat?

These are not findings from field research on a particular religious community. They are questions opened for inquiry through distinctions in the Collection. The relationship between responses to questions, learning, trust and belonging needs separate investigation in concrete settings.

A reading that values service to faith and eternal life can aim to clarify a matter rather than lead people to conceal what they do not understand. That is the approach adopted here: taking people’s responsibility for understanding seriously while respecting the author and the text.
