Why Do We Read the Same Text Again?

A sociological perspective on the Risale-i Nur Collection

Adem Afşar · 17 November 2026

When we finish a book, we say that we have read it. But does finishing a text mean that our relationship with it is complete? Particularly with texts that raise questions about how we should live, there may be a distance between reading and practice that calls for renewed reflection.

At the beginning of the Twenty-First Lem’a of Risale-i Nur, we find this recommendation, rendered here in English:

Read the source quotation here

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · Lem’alar · Twenty-First Lem’a · English rendering

This Lem’a should be read at least once every fifteen days.

The word “lâakal” in the Turkish original means “at least”. The recommendation concerns regularly rereading this section, known as the İhlâs Risalesi. It should not be extended into a requirement to read the entire collection at the same frequency.[1]

This brief sentence opens a substantial sociological question: when knowing an idea is not considered sufficient, what arrangements are made to keep it alive in everyday life?

The distance between knowing and acting

People may know that they should not belittle others. Yet in an argument they may hurt someone through a desire to prevail. They may value solidarity but feel uneasy when a shared success is associated with somebody else’s name.

In such cases, the issue is not always ignorance of the right principle. People may also struggle to recognise the distance between a principle they know and their own behaviour.

Approached in this way, the recommendation to reread the İhlâs Risalesi allows us to interpret repetition as an opportunity to reconsider one’s conduct. This is, however, our sociological interpretation of the recommendation; it does not mean that Bediüzzaman Said Nursi presents an articulated theory of learning in this sentence.

On each return, readers may ask themselves a new question: was I truly pleased by another person’s success? How much recognition did I expect for contributing to our shared work? How much room did I make in my conduct for the brotherhood I advocated?

Meeting the same sentence through a different life

A text’s words may remain unchanged. The reader’s experiences change.

Someone who has never entered a partnership may think differently about a passage on solidarity from someone reading the same lines after a disagreement in a partnership to which they have devoted considerable effort. A counsel that was abstract on first reading may call up a concrete memory on a later occasion.

Repetition may therefore offer an opportunity to reassess experience alongside recalling previously acquired knowledge. People return to an old sentence with a new experience.

There is a limit here, however. Finding an echo of our own experiences in a text does not establish that the text describes only what happened to us. Distinguishing personal associations from what the author says is an intellectual responsibility of rereading.

Reading together, making shared time

Regular reading can acquire another dimension when undertaken together. People who meet at intervals set aside shared time within everyday life. This may create a setting for listening to one another, sharing questions and returning to the same concepts.

From this perspective, the reading gathering should be examined alongside the content of the text. Who speaks and who listens? Can newcomers ask questions? Do they feel comfortable asking for an unfamiliar word to be explained? How is a different interpretation received?

These are not judgements about any particular community. They are questions that can be asked when researching the social character of shared reading. Gathering around the same book does not guarantee the same form of relationship everywhere.

When repetition becomes habit

Regular reading may enable attentive reflection, but it can also become a repetition of familiar sentences without pausing over them. Reading a text frequently does not prove that it is considered carefully every time.

A meaningful criterion can be proposed here: can readers connect what they read with their everyday conduct?

Speaking at length about brotherhood while giving a newcomer no opportunity to speak makes the distance between text and practice visible. Immediately silencing a different interpretation while advocating listening to others creates a similar distance. These are hypothetical examples intended to clarify the idea.

The value of repetition cannot be assessed solely by how many readings have been completed. The questions that emerge, the conduct reconsidered and the changes in ways of living together also need examination.

Making room for personal meaning

Reading a text in a group and reflecting on it alone can complement each other. Shared reading allows us to hear other people’s questions. Solitary reading leaves room for questions people may not yet wish to express.

Expecting every reader to reach the same conclusion at the same time can narrow this process. Being able to say that one does not understand, to defer a judgement and to avoid treating personal associations as binding on others are possibilities that shared reading can preserve. These are contemporary criteria developed in this essay.

The recommendation to reread at the beginning of the İhlâs Risalesi thus invites reflection on how knowledge is sustained in everyday life. While the text stays in place, people’s relationships, responsibilities and questions may change.

What we encounter on rereading may be a previously overlooked sentence, or a detail in our own behaviour that we had not noticed before.
