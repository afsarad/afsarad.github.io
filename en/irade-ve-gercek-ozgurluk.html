Will and Genuine Freedom

Does having two doors before us mean we can pass through both? A door may be open, yet we may lack the time, knowledge or resources needed to take that path. Sometimes, even without an obstacle, we struggle to explain to ourselves why we chose a particular direction. The presence of options, the ability to choose and the ability to implement a choice are dimensions of freedom that must be distinguished.

I propose thinking of freedom as a person's ability to exercise sound agency over their own will . By sound agency, I do not mean one externally prescribed correct life for everyone. I mean recognising one's desires, evaluating options and consequences, forming decisions without pressure and reconsidering them when necessary. This definition does not claim to resolve every philosophical debate about freedom; it offers a starting point for examining everyday decision-making.

Wanting, Deciding and Being Able to Act

Wanting something is not the same as deciding to do it. People may have conflicting desires. They may want both to rest and to finish a task already begun. They may wish to learn a new profession while resisting departure from the security of familiar routines. The work of the will cannot be to fulfil every desire simultaneously.

A decision requires evaluating these desires. Even after that evaluation, however, another question arises: does the person have the conditions needed to carry out the choice? Someone deciding to attend a course may be prevented by working hours, transport limitations or care obligations. Explaining non-fulfilment solely as a lack of will overlooks the conditions for implementing the decision.

We therefore cannot measure freedom solely through the strength of desires. A strong desire may have formed within narrow options. A calmer and more hesitant preference may express a carefully considered personal direction. Outward determination does not alone reveal how the decision was formed.

The Environment in Which Our Desires Take Shape

People do not form their desires entirely outside society. Standards of success learned from family, occupations respected by others, economic resources and expectations of acceptance may shape preferences. These influences do not make every preference false or alien to the person. Learning language and tools of thought from others does not mean we cannot think for ourselves either.

The central question is how we relate to these influences. We may choose a profession on family advice and later discover it suits our abilities. We may also accept the same advice from fear of losing love or support if we act differently. An apparently identical choice may contain different decision-making conditions.

Mill, On Liberty in its third chapter, discusses the difference between choosing one's own life plan and following patterns prescribed by others in terms of exercising human faculties.[1] Drawing on this discussion, my emphasis is not that people should be entirely uninfluenced by others, but that they should participate in evaluating that influence. This is also where the difference between taking advice and surrendering decision-making becomes apparent.

The Difference Between an Open Door and an Accessible Path

Recognition of a right matters. But people's ability to exercise it may differ. If two people can apply to the same educational programme but only one has time and transport, their practical options are not identical. This difference does not negate the right's value; it shows that exercising it may require additional conditions.

In “Equality of What?”, Amartya Sen argues that looking only at people's resources may be insufficient; we must also evaluate what those resources enable them to do. The same resources may not produce the same capabilities because people's circumstances differ.[2]

Applying this approach to freedom, we cannot merely ask, “Is anything prohibited to you?” The question “What do you need to exercise this option?” also becomes important. Yet improved external conditions do not mean a person must fulfil every desire. Expanded opportunities should also preserve room not to choose and to take another path.

Being Able to Say No and the Cost of a Decision

Acceptance of an offer does not establish freedom from all pressure. A person may formally have the option to refuse, yet fear losing housing, income or an important relationship. What must be examined is not merely the word spoken. How practicable the other options are also matters.

Nevertheless, not every cost is coercion. Choosing one task may reduce time available for another. Entering a commitment may limit later choices. Freedom cannot mean exemption from all consequences of decisions. Ordinary consequences, previously assumed obligations and threats intended to force obedience must be assessed separately.

Without this distinction, treating every acceptance as voluntary and every difficult choice as unfree are equally incomplete. We cannot make definitive judgements about people without knowing the conditions of the examples.

Does Self-Governance Mean Constant Self-Suppression?

Exercising agency over one's will does not mean distrusting every desire or renouncing every pleasure. Rest, enjoyment and time with loved ones may also be reflectively adopted preferences. Reducing life to productivity and achievement does not provide a sufficient framework for evaluating freedom.

An important aspect of self-governance is comparing immediate desires with long-term aims. Sometimes, however, what needs reassessment is the longstanding aim rather than the immediate desire. People may realise that a goal considered suitable years earlier has lost its meaning. Changing direction after evaluating one's reasons is as much an exercise of will as persevering under all circumstances.

My proposed distinction is this: we cannot judge a decision to give something up solely by the label attached to its outcome. A person may be fleeing fear, or may have recognised their priorities more clearly. Likewise, continuing may result from deliberate commitment or unquestioned habit. The name of a behaviour does not alone explain its reasons.

Satisfaction, Dependence and Judging from Outside

A person's statement that they are satisfied with life deserves to be taken seriously. An outsider's preference for a different life is insufficient grounds to invalidate that person's choices. People may prioritise security, belonging, curiosity, achievement or tranquillity differently.

On the other hand, satisfaction alone may be insufficient when evaluating freedom. We can also examine whether a person knows other options, can ask questions, can change a decision and under what conditions. These questions should be asked to understand the space in which people form preferences, not to decide on their behalf what happiness is.

The boundary between providing support and taking control matters here. Explaining alternatives, facilitating access to information and allowing time for deliberation may expand the scope of choice. Deciding for someone while saying “Only I know what is right for you” may establish a new dependence under the claim of helping.

Four Questions When Evaluating a Decision

I propose four questions for examining experiences of freedom. First, how far can the person recognise what they want and the influences shaping that desire? Second, do their options exist only on paper, or can they actually use them? Third, do they have the knowledge and time to evaluate short- and long-term consequences? Fourth, can they reconsider their decision or question its grounds?

These questions are neither a conclusive test nor a validated psychological instrument. They are not intended to reduce entire lives to a single freedom score. The aim is to avoid focusing only on the outcome or on whether a person appears strong when evaluating a decision.

Someone may have considerable room for action in some areas and serious constraints in others. Economic independence does not eliminate every social pressure. Nor does family support mean that others make every decision for the person. We must consider how the relationship is constituted and which decision is being evaluated.

Thinking About Freedom and Responsibility Together

Choices may affect other people's lives. I therefore do not define freedom as doing whatever one wants without considering others' rights. But responsibility should not mean surrendering one's entire sphere of decision-making to others either. Explaining the requirements of an assumed duty differs from claiming the right to control someone's entire life.

The central argument is that internal evaluative capacity and external opportunities for action should be considered together. As people learn to question their decisions, the arrangements in which they live should enable access to information, alternatives and channels of objection. Expecting only individuals to become stronger may conceal external barriers. Looking only at external conditions may neglect people's efforts to evaluate their own lives.

I seek genuine freedom in this reciprocal relationship: a sphere of life in which people can recognise desires, weigh reasons, see feasible paths and change direction when necessary. Within that sphere, the will works not only to decide, but to understand which decisions are one's own.
