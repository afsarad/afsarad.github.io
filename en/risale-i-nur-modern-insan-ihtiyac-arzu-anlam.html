The Modern Human Being in Risale-i Nur

Need, Desire and the Search for Meaning

Adem Afşar · 16 November 2026

As people expand their possibilities, can they retain the ability to decide what is enough? This is the first question I want to consider when reading Risale-i Nur alongside the experiences of people today.

Wanting a more comfortable life is understandable. Better housing, learning, health and a secure future for loved ones are ordinary concerns. Yet there is a distance worth examining between pursuing these things and seeking one’s entire worth in possessing more.

There is no single modern human being

“Modern people” do not form a group with identical feelings, beliefs or resources. Someone struggling to make ends meet has different needs from someone living in abundance. Faith, urban life, technology use and a commitment to individual freedom can also coexist in the same person.

Reading the collection in the present therefore requires care about assigning people to fixed categories. My approach is to explore the questions particular passages open up. The everyday examples in this essay are thought examples that make those questions visible, rather than findings demonstrated to apply to everyone.

Where is human worth located?

In the First Point of the First Station of the Twenty-Third Word, Bediüzzaman Said Nursi explains human worth through the bond of faith with the Creator. His statement that faith is a connection or affiliation is central to this account. The text contrasts faith and unbelief in how human worth is understood. This is an explicitly religious explanation; treating it simply as advice for feeling better would diminish its central meaning. [1]

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · Sözler · Yirmi Üçüncü Söz · Birinci Mebhas · Birinci Nokta

Digital copy · Text page 311 · PDF page 309

A human being rises to the highest ranks through the light of faith and gains a worth befitting Paradise. Through the darkness of unbelief, a person falls to the lowest depths and becomes fit for Hell. For faith relates a person to the Glorious Maker; faith is an affiliation. Thus, through faith, a person gains worth through the divine art and the inscriptions of the Divine Names manifested within them. Unbelief severs that connection, concealing the divine art. Their worth is then considered only in terms of matter. Since matter is transient, passing and a temporary animal life, that worth is as nothing.

English rendering prepared for this essay; not a quotation from a published English edition.

Context — summary: The surrounding passage develops human worth through the bond of faith with the Creator.

My question for the present is what people connect their own worth to: their occupation, income, appearance, popularity or acceptance by a group? These can have a place in life. But when a person’s entire judgement of themselves depends on them, losing a job or receiving less attention than expected may threaten their sense of worth as a person. This connection is my interpretation developed from the text.

Meeting a need and establishing a sense of enough

The First Point of the Nineteenth Flash, known as the Treatise on Frugality, connects economical use with respect for blessings and gratitude, setting wastefulness against that relationship. Frugality here has a wider meaning than calculating how much money to save: it concerns how people regard and use what they have. [2]

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · Lem’alar · On Dokuzuncu Lem’a (İktisad Risalesi) · Birinci Nükte

Digital copy · Text page 139 · PDF page 136

The Merciful Creator asks humanity for gratitude in return for the blessings He gives. Wastefulness is opposed to gratitude: it is a harmful belittling of a blessing. Frugality, by contrast, is a beneficial expression of respect for blessings. Frugality is a form of spiritual gratitude, respect for divine mercy in blessings, a sure cause of abundance, a source of bodily health like moderation in eating, a means of dignity that frees one from the humiliation of spiritual begging, and a powerful means of tasting the pleasure in blessings, including those that seem tasteless. Wastefulness opposes these purposes and has grave consequences.

English rendering prepared for this essay; not a quotation from a published English edition.

Context — summary: The surrounding explanation links gratitude, valuing blessings and avoiding waste.

Consider this contemporary thought example: someone with a functioning phone may feel left behind simply because a newer model has appeared. The practical need has remained unchanged while the standard of enough has shifted. The question is not whether to buy a particular product. It is whether the person can recognise a decision arising from practical use or from comparison with others.

Reflection on contentment must also leave real deprivation visible. Telling someone whose housing, food or healthcare needs are unmet simply to want less is an inadequate social response. Income, working conditions and the distribution of opportunities also need examination. This is a distinction I want to preserve in my sociological interpretation: personal moderation and social justice should be considered together.

The possibilities of progress and their direction

In the Fifth Note of the Seventeenth Flash, Bediüzzaman Said Nursi distinguishes a Europe that pursues socially beneficial arts and sciences serving justice and equity from the other understanding of Europe that he criticises. This distinction matters when interpreting his criticism of civilisation: it should not be read as a rejection of every scientific and technical advance. [3]

Bediüzzaman Said Nursi · Lem’alar · On Yedinci Lem’a · Beşinci Nota

Digital copy · Text page 115 · PDF page 112

Let there be no misunderstanding: Europe is twofold. I am not addressing the first Europe, which draws inspiration from true Christianity, pursues arts beneficial to human social life, and follows sciences that serve justice and equity. Rather, I address a corrupted second Europe, which, in the darkness of naturalistic philosophy, mistakes the evils of civilisation for its virtues and leads humanity towards dissipation and error.

English rendering prepared for this essay; not a quotation from a published English edition.

Context — summary: The continuation distinguishes beneficial science and justice from the orientation of civilisation being criticised.

The research question I draw from this concerns the effects of progress on human life. A tool may save time; we must still decide how to use that time. Faster communication does not automatically mean listening more attentively. The relationship between expanding possibilities and using them humanely requires further reflection.

Thinking together and making personal meaning

A reading group can help someone notice a connection they overlooked alone. Asking questions, listening and attending to a shared text can bring participants closer together. Each reader also encounters the text through their own experience and capacity for understanding.

What matters to me here is preserving voluntary participation alongside respect for the individual’s inner life. Making attendance frequency the sole measure of someone’s faith or morality places more interpretive weight on an observable behaviour than it can bear. I express this as my own understanding of living together, rather than as a direct ruling of the collection.

Finding personal meaning in a text does not make that meaning binding on everyone. Recognising the personal character of an interpretation creates room for conversation. When a claim affects shared life, its reasons must also be open to discussion together.

A measure of the relationship with oneself

I value the thought that “a person is as human as they are when alone” as a way of examining one’s relationship with conscience. An action does not lose its value because it takes place before others. Helping together can also produce social benefit. The question is how visible behaviour relates to a responsibility the person inwardly accepts.

I begin this reading of Risale-i Nur without claiming that a single judgement explains everyone today. I want to reconsider our needs, desires, affiliations and standards of worth. The question I want to leave open at the end of this first essay is this: while striving to reach more, do we also know what we want to preserve?
