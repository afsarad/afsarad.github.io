What is sociology, and how does it explain everyday life?

Sociology systematically examines human relationships, social institutions and change. To understand behaviour, it considers family, education, work and surrounding conditions alongside individual intentions. For example, rather than explaining weaker neighbourhood ties solely through indifference, we can investigate working hours, housing patterns and residential mobility together.

Who was Ibn Khaldun, and what is the Muqaddimah about?

Ibn Khaldun was a thinker and historian who lived from 1332 to 1406. In the Muqaddimah, he connects social life, livelihoods and the formation of political power. Reading him today requires understanding the historical conditions of his explanations before applying them to the present. Our book project examines that context alongside contemporary questions.

What is asabiyyah? Solidarity in Ibn Khaldun

Asabiyyah is a central concept in Ibn Khaldun’s account of group solidarity and the capacity for collective action. Here it does not mean irritability, a meaning associated with a related word in everyday Turkish. When examining solidarity today, we should ask both how it supports members and how it affects outsiders’ rights. This second question is a contemporary interpretation developed from the concept.

What is responsibility, and how can personal boundaries be established?

Responsibility concerns attending to the consequences of one’s decisions and actions. Its scope should be discussed in relation to knowledge, authority and capacity to act. Adem Afşar’s “circles of responsibility” approach examines relationships extending from the individual to family and society through this question: How can we respect others’ rights while retaining our own? This approach should be read as the author’s conceptual proposal.

How does migration affect society?

Migration can be examined through housing, employment, education, language and social relationships. The direction and scale of its effects vary with the circumstances of migration and institutional capacity. A single example is therefore insufficient for conclusions about all migrants or all communities. Sociological inquiry should bring concrete evidence, human rights and the institutional conditions of coexistence into the same assessment.

Why should cultural heritage be protected?

A building, object or archaeological site can carry information about past ways of life. Its value lies not only in appearance or monetary worth, but also in its surroundings and relationships with other finds. Reading the human traces left in a place requires investigating what the evidence says without projecting our expectations onto the past. The related essay discusses this relationship through meaning and preservation.

What is the difference between need and desire?

The boundary between need and desire cannot be drawn identically in every situation. A product can fulfil a basic function while also appealing to belonging, recognition or status. Sociologically, the question is not only “Do I want this?” but also “Which comparisons, habits and social expectations shape this desire?” The related essay examines consumption, advertising and tradition.

What does Cevabü’l-ahmak es-sükût mean, and when should one respond?

This saying commonly recommends silence in the face of futile argument. Our discussion focuses on the consequences of silence and response rather than using the expression to label people. Avoiding escalation and preventing misinformation from harming others are different reasons for action. We discuss when speaking may be necessary by considering tone, evidence and the audience’s opportunity to understand.
