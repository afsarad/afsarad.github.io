Where Do EU Rules End and National Discretion Begin?

Adem Afşar
Interdisciplinary sociological analyst
27 November 2026

From 1 November 2026, Montenegro ended visa-free travel for citizens of five countries, including Türkiye. On 27 November, Türkiye published a decision suspending its visa exemption for Montenegrin ordinary passport holders.

Montenegro said the change aligned its visa policy with that of the EU. Türkiye’s published decision gave no reason linking the two moves. Travel between the two countries became more restrictive in both directions before Montenegro joined the EU.

Who makes EU decisions?

Under the ordinary legislative procedure, the European Commission makes a proposal. The directly elected European Parliament and the Council of the European Union, made up of ministers from member states, negotiate the text. A standard qualified majority in the Council requires at least 15 of the 27 states representing at least 65% of the EU population. Accession requires unanimity in the Council, Parliament’s consent and ratification of the accession treaty by all parties under their constitutional procedures.

Montenegro changed its visa regime by government decision. It cannot yet vote as a member in the institutions that set the rules it is asked to adopt. Montenegro began negotiations in 2012, and the Commission assessed in 2026 that they had reached the final stage. Türkiye’s negotiations, opened in 2005, have been at a standstill since 2018. The Commission has raised concerns over the rule of law and fundamental rights. Ankara called the 2026 report biased and unfair.

How far do common rules shape society?

EU treaties reserve competences that have not been conferred on the Union to the member states and require respect for national identity. In culture, the EU may support cooperation, but that power excludes harmonising national laws. Outside exclusive EU competences, Union action must also meet the tests of subsidiarity and proportionality. An objective should be pursued at EU level when individual countries cannot sufficiently achieve it and action at Union level would work better. Measures must go no further than necessary.

Under Montenegro’s former exemption, Turkish ordinary passport holders could enter Montenegro without thereby acquiring the right to enter neighbouring Croatia visa-free. Alignment with the common visa policy explains the institutional rationale. What specific risk made this timing necessary for Türkiye, and were less burdensome transition options considered?

Membership and economic security

In January 2026, farmers in Paris and Warsaw protested against the EU’s Mercosur trade agreement. One concern was competition from lower-cost imports. In February, the European Parliament approved safeguards that could be used if a rise in imports harms producers. The single market creates opportunities, while the costs of a specific trade decision may fall heavily on some producers.

Eurostat reports that between 2015 and 2025 the house-price-to-income ratio rose by 6.8% across the EU, 60.8% in Portugal and 24.5% in Spain. The figures do not establish the causes of the rise. They show how differently housing affordability can evolve within the same union.

Adem Afşar’s sociological analysis

Montenegro’s case involves two timetables. Membership depends on negotiations and ratification. The change in visa rules enters travellers’ plans today. Imagine a person who has arranged leave to visit family. If an application forces that person to change their dates, a small change on the diplomatic calendar becomes a postponed reunion. This is a hypothetical example. The actual effects can be measured through processing times, additional expenses and postponed trips.

Demography, customs, family ties and ways of solving problems develop over time. Applying the same rule everywhere can have quite different consequences. How much influence did Montenegro have over timing and transition arrangements before it could vote as a member? The EU should explain why the rule is needed, Montenegro why it applied the rule on that date, and Türkiye why it made its own decision. An assessment of accession should account for the costs travellers face today.
