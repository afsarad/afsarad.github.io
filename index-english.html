Propositions and Research Questions

Four working questions: Do priorities toward those closest to us remove wider duties? Why can similar events differ in historical meaning? When does solidarity become coordinated action? How does public authority change accountability?

Comparative Literature: Scope, Context and Verification

Comparative scholarship is used here to sharpen questions, not to borrow authority. Similar vocabulary across centuries is not evidence of identical concepts, intellectual transmission, or a confirmed original contribution.

AR-001 · Ethics of proximity

Comparative conversation: Carol Gilligan and Nel Noddings address relationships and care; Peter Singer examines the widening scope of moral concern. These works provide different points of entry into questions about particular obligations and broader ethical concern.

Afşar's question: How can prioritising those closest to us coexist with duties to people beyond our immediate circle?

Boundary: Care ethics, impartial concern and concentric responsibility are not interchangeable theories. Direct textual agreement has not been established.

AR-002 · Historical resemblance

Comparative conversation: Karl Mannheim's work on generations draws attention to historical location and the conditions under which people experience events.

Afşar's question: When two events appear to recur, what changes in their institutions, meanings and consequences?

Boundary: Mannheim's account of generations is not evidence that he formulated the proposed “spiral” thesis. Historical analogies require case-specific analysis.

AR-003 · Asabiyyah and collective action

Comparative conversation: Ibn Khaldun's concept of ʿaṣabiyyah offers a historically situated account of solidarity and political formation. Modern scholarship on group solidarity, including Siniša Malešević, opens a separate comparative discussion.

Afşar's question: Under what institutional and material conditions does solidarity become coordinated action?

Boundary: Medieval political formations cannot be treated as equivalent to contemporary networks or institutions.

AR-004 · Public authority and responsibility

Comparative conversation: Political ethics distinguishes duties attached to an office, the exercise of discretion, and accountability for consequences.

Afşar's question: How should a public officeholder's effort, promises, decisions and results be evaluated together?

Boundary: A specific comparative author or empirical validation has not yet been established for this proposition.

Verification status: These are thematic research leads, not completed source-critical comparisons. Quotations, exact editions, page references and claims of influence must be verified against primary texts before scholarly publication.

Counterarguments and Revision Conditions

A critical objection is recorded here as a question for research; it is not attributed to a named author unless the attribution has been checked.

Proximity versus fairness

Objection: Preference for family and immediate community may reproduce unequal treatment of outsiders.

Revision condition: If the proposed model cannot protect basic rights across circles, its priority rules must be revised.

Difference versus continuity

Objection: Stressing the uniqueness of each historical period may obscure enduring structures.

Revision condition: If historical cases exhibit stable mechanisms despite changing contexts, the framework must accommodate those continuities.

Solidarity versus exclusion

Objection: Strong group cohesion can facilitate discrimination as well as cooperation.

Revision condition: A useful account must distinguish mobilisation capacity from ethical legitimacy.

Effort versus public outcomes

Objection: Good intentions and sustained effort do not by themselves guarantee responsible public administration.

Revision condition: An evaluation model must account for foreseeable harms, institutional constraints and measurable results.

AR-001 · Circles of Responsibility

Central proposition. Individuals may reasonably give priority to those for whom they bear immediate and specific responsibilities without thereby denying duties toward strangers or humanity at large. The proposed model uses nested circles to distinguish proximity, practical capacity, role-based obligations and the reach of consequences.

Conceptual distinction. Priority is not equivalent to moral exclusion. A parent’s particular obligation to a child, for example, need not imply indifference to people outside the household. Conversely, the existence of universal concerns does not automatically erase obligations arising from sustained relationships.

Comparative research. Care ethics and accounts of expanding moral concern offer relevant points of comparison, but their arguments must be reconstructed from their primary texts before specific agreements or disagreements can be asserted. Similar metaphors of circles are not evidence of intellectual influence.

Possible objection. Proximity may rationalise nepotism or unequal treatment. The inquiry must distinguish legitimate personal priority from decisions made under public authority, where impartiality and legal duties may impose stricter limits.

Revision condition. Clarify or reject any proposed priority rule that systematically licenses preventable harm, discrimination, or neglect of binding obligations beyond the inner circles.

Research links: comparative literature · counterarguments · claim register.

AR-002 · Not a Cycle, but a Spiral

Central proposition. Historical episodes may resemble one another in institutional form or social behaviour while differing in meaning because actors, technologies, collective memories, material conditions and normative expectations have changed.

Methodological caution. The spiral is an interpretive metaphor, not an established law of historical progress or a mathematical model. It does not entail that every later event is more advanced, nor that history moves toward a predetermined endpoint.

Research design. Select clearly bounded cases; specify the dimensions on which they appear similar; then examine contemporary sources, actors’ interpretations, institutional arrangements and outcomes. Seek both continuities and discontinuities instead of presuming either.

Possible objection. Overemphasising difference may obscure recurring mechanisms such as resource competition, coercion or institutional breakdown. A robust comparison must allow a mechanism to recur while its social significance changes.

Revision condition. Narrow the proposition where comparative evidence shows stable mechanisms or meanings across the selected periods; avoid claims that all repetition is illusory.

Research links: inter-work relationships · method.

AR-003 · Asabiyyah and Collective Action

Research question. Under what conditions does felt solidarity become sustained, coordinated collective action, and when can solidarity serve exclusionary or coercive ends?

Historical boundary. Ibn Khaldun’s concept of ʿaṣabiyyah belongs to a particular intellectual and political context. It should not be translated uncritically into present-day social cohesion, network effects, party organisation or online mobilisation.

Comparative design. Distinguish emotional identification, shared interests, organisational capacity, resources, leadership, institutions and external pressures. Cases of strong identity without collective action are as important as cases where mobilisation succeeds.

Possible objection. Group solidarity is not intrinsically beneficial; it may produce cooperation internally while intensifying antagonism externally. The analysis must therefore separate descriptive capacity for mobilisation from moral evaluation of its aims.

Revision condition. Rework any claim that solidarity alone is sufficient when cases demonstrate the independent importance of organisation, incentives or institutions.

Research links: literature register · evidence limits.

AR-004 · Public Authority and Responsibility

Central question. How does the scope of a person’s responsibilities change when private agency becomes the exercise of public power?

Analytical distinction. Good intentions, diligence, procedural legality, foreseeable risks and actual outcomes are related but not interchangeable grounds of evaluation. Public authority may create obligations to explain decisions, disclose conflicts of interest and justify burdens imposed on others.

Comparative inquiry. The project should consider role morality, public accountability, democratic legitimacy and administrative ethics without assuming that any one philosophical tradition fully determines the answer.

Possible objection. Judging officeholders solely by outcomes ignores constraints and uncertainty; judging them solely by effort can excuse foreseeable failure. A defensible framework must specify when each consideration matters.

Revision condition. Revise standards that cannot distinguish unavoidable adverse outcomes from negligence, or sincere commitment from accountable performance.

Research links: candidate contribution · critical objections.

AR-005 · Gender Attitudes Across Generations: Kütahya Case Study

Research type. An empirical thesis manuscript examining attitudes toward gender roles across generational categories in a Kütahya sample. The working record reports 234 participants; this website does not reproduce the dataset or the full thesis.

Interpretive boundary. Differences observed among birth cohorts in a cross-sectional survey do not automatically establish generational causation. Age, period, cohort, education, sampling and measurement effects require separate consideration.

Evidence rule. Any statement about statistical significance, group differences or generalisability must be checked against the original tables, test assumptions and sample distribution before being published as a finding. A non-significant test is not proof that no relationship exists.

Research connection. The thesis can inform the methodological discussion in AR-002, but it does not establish the spiral metaphor as a historical theory.

BD-001 · Draft conference abstract (v0.37)

The growing use of artificial intelligence in employment, public services and organisational governance raises questions about who should be accountable for the consequences of AI-mediated decisions. This proposed paper develops “Circles of Responsibility” as a conceptual framework for examining how duties are distributed across individuals, proximate relations, organisations, public authorities and wider society. Its central question is whether automating part of a decision changes, displaces or obscures human and institutional accountability. The proposed method combines normative conceptual analysis with a comparative reading of scholarship on AI governance and algorithmic accountability. It distinguishes system designers, operators, managers, oversight bodies and affected persons in terms of authority, foreseeability, capacity to intervene and access to contestation. The intended contribution is to examine responsibility as a layered relationship rather than attributing every consequence solely to the last actor in a decision chain. This abstract reports no empirical findings. The framework remains a proposal requiring critical comparison with concrete cases and competing interpretations.

Keywords: Artificial intelligence; algorithmic accountability; public responsibility; circles of responsibility; sociology; ethics

Research problem: Where do technical processing, organisational authority and public accountability diverge in AI-assisted decisions?

Method: Normative and conceptual analysis, followed by comparative examination of cases yet to be selected. No data collection or research-ethics approval is claimed.

Proposed contribution: Consider proximate priority alongside wider obligations; distinguish design, deployment, oversight and contestation.

Limitations: This is not an empirically validated model. Legal duties depend on jurisdiction and context. Distributed responsibility must not become a pretext for accountability gaps.

BD-001 · Full-paper working outline (v0.38)

This is an unsubmitted conceptual conference-paper draft. Its structure and arguments remain open to revision; it must not be represented as a completed scholarly publication without primary-source checks and author approval.

1. Introduction and research problem

Decision-support systems may shape choices in employment, welfare, education and public administration. The issue is not only whether a system makes mistakes, but also who can foresee, prevent, contest and remedy them. The guiding question is: how should duties be differentiated among actors with unequal authority and capacity to intervene?

2. Conceptual distinctions

Responsibility has multiple meanings. Causal contribution, professional role, legal liability, moral duty and public accountability should not be treated as interchangeable. A system generating a recommendation does not automatically release a manager or oversight institution from its obligations. Conversely, a designer should not be assigned powers they do not possess.

3. Framework for literature review

Helen Nissenbaum on accountability in computerised society, Virginia Eubanks on the social consequences of automated public decisions, and Frank Pasquale on opaque algorithmic systems offer candidate points of comparison. Their precise arguments and supporting pages have not yet been checked against primary texts. Accordingly, this section does not claim verified quotations or page-specific citations.

4. Adapting Circles of Responsibility

Circles of Responsibility originally considers differentiated priorities and obligations extending from the person and close relations to wider social circles. Transposing it into AI governance does not imply equivalence: familial proximity is not the same as institutional authority. Here the circles serve as a heuristic for mapping actors’ duties and spheres of influence, not as an established empirical model.

5. Proposed analytical criteria

Five questions structure the comparison: (i) Who defined the decision parameters? (ii) Who could reasonably foresee consequences? (iii) Who could intervene or suspend the system? (iv) To whom could an affected person appeal? (v) Who has the authority and resources to provide redress? These are analytical prompts, not a universal legal test.

6. Illustrative case design

A hypothetical hiring-ranking system could systematically disadvantage some applicants. Another hypothetical system could assign automated risk scores in social-benefit applications. Each scenario would distinguish developers, procuring organisations, operational managers, oversight bodies and affected people. These are illustrative scenarios, not reported fieldwork or documented incidents; actual case selection remains pending.

7. Objections and provisional responses

A first objection is that distributing responsibility may create an accountability vacuum; therefore authority and intervention capacity must be specified. A second is that proximity-based language might undermine impartial public service; the proposal distinguishes kinds of duties rather than endorsing preferential public treatment. A third is that different legal systems may assign responsibility differently; the framework expressly recognises this limitation.

8. Method, limitations and ethics

The initial stage is normative and conceptual analysis. A second stage could examine documented cases comparatively, but no sample, dataset, interviews, fieldwork or ethics approval are claimed. Any future use of personal data would require data minimisation, appropriate protection and compliance with applicable law. This draft makes no empirical findings.

9. Discussion and provisional conclusion

Focusing solely on the final human approver or solely on the system designer may overlook how duties interact across a decision chain. The proposed contribution is a structured inquiry into when obligations converge or diverge. Its explanatory value remains unverified until tested against counterexamples and documented cases.

10. Tasks before submission

Verify the current official call and formatting requirements; consult original works and create Chicago notes with checked editions and page references; separate hypothetical scenarios from documented cases; assess originality, scope and word count; and obtain the author’s final approval.

BD-001 · Allocating Responsibility and Testing Counterexamples (v0.39)

Status: Unsubmitted conceptual working draft. The matrix below is neither an allocation of legal fault nor empirical evidence. Each entry is contingent on the documents and institutional setting of a specific case.

11. Distinguish types of responsibility before allocating duties

An actor's causal contribution to a decision does not by itself establish moral blameworthiness or legal liability. Responsibility is not confined to retrospective blame: duties to prevent harm, explain decisions, monitor systems, hear challenges and provide remedies may be held by different actors. We therefore do not propose numerical shares of responsibility.

13. Three counterexamples that challenge the proposal

  1. Nominal human approval: A manager clicks “approve” but cannot inspect the scoring logic or override the recommendation. Assigning all responsibility to the final signatory would miss the underlying distribution of authority.
  2. Unforeseeable failure: A developer performed reasonable tests, yet harm arises from an unforeseen interaction outside documented conditions. Causal contribution does not settle fault; duties to report and remedy the incident may nevertheless arise.
  3. Diffuse authority and no remedy: A provider, institution and overseer each point to the others, leaving the affected person without an accountable contact. The framework fails unless it can identify an effective route to challenge, correction and a responsible institutional addressee.

14. Limits of the Circles of Responsibility analogy

The original idea of differentiated priorities toward close relations cannot justify nepotism or preferential treatment in public decisions. The analogy here concerns overlapping but distinct scopes of duty. Institutional accountability must be analysed through role, authority, foreseeability, intervention capacity and effective contestation—not personal proximity. The possibility that these are conceptually different phenomena remains a serious objection, not something to conceal.

15. Conditions for revision and further research

If documented cases cannot reliably map decision power and remedial duties onto the proposed actors, if similar authority structures produce contradictory assignments, or if the framework adds no clarity beyond existing accountability models, it should be narrowed or abandoned. A subsequent study could examine at least two documented cases, independently checking decision pathways, contracts, oversight records and avenues of appeal. That empirical work has not yet been undertaken.

Voluntariness, Goodwill and Fair Distribution of Responsibility — v0.45

Editorial status: A provisional conceptual extension of AR-001, Circles of Responsibility, based on the author's stated distinctions. Author review, primary-source comparison and empirical testing remain outstanding; no claim of validated originality is made.

1. How responsibility is acquired

An expanding sphere of responsibility is not a uniform process. Distinguish (a) commitments deliberately chosen in light of skills, education, aspirations, available resources and family support; (b) roles accepted because a political association, foundation, educational institution or voluntary organisation advances an ideal the person values; and (c) additional work accepted in response to requests, expectations or repeated appeals. Being offered a role, consenting to it and identifying with its purpose are distinct stages.

2. Capacity and sustainability

Expansion should be evaluated against existing commitments, available time, material and emotional resources, health and decision-making capacity. Meaningful ideals do not eliminate risks of overextension, impaired judgment or strain on family relationships. A redistribution of time and effort does not automatically extinguish prior duties.

3. The unfair concentration of requests

If one helpful person is repeatedly approached while others with comparable skills, authority, resources and existing workloads are not, the allocation of demands warrants scrutiny. A freely offered one-time favour differs from a recurring expectation whose refusal carries a social cost. Provisional proposition: A person's willingness to help does not justify systematically exempting similarly situated people from comparable responsibilities.

Similar apparent abilities do not prove equal obligations: formal duties, existing workloads, access to resources and legal responsibilities may differ. Starting proposition

When help initially accepted in goodwill is repeatedly requested from the same person without approaching similarly situated others, a fairness problem may arise even before the person's capacity is exceeded. Initial consent, repeated expectations, and fair allocation are distinct dimensions.

Four hypothetical cases

  1. Family — repeated care: Three adult siblings have comparable time and financial resources, but only the sibling who readily agrees is asked to meet a parent's weekly needs. AR-001 · The Responsibility of Those Who Request Responsibility — v0.49

Status: A conceptual examination developed from the author's concern that goodwill should not be exploited and similarly situated people should be treated fairly. These analytical distinctions are not empirical findings or legal conclusions.

Reframing the question

“Why did the person accept?” is insufficient. We must also ask: “Why was this person selected? Why were other suitable people not approached? What responsibility does the requester bear for the allocation and its consequences?” Goodwill should not become the sole criterion for assigning work.

A two-sided responsibility relationship

Person undertaking the taskPerson or institution requesting it
Assess capacity, existing commitments and terms of acceptanceExplain the need, scope and duration
Consent, set boundaries and reconsider when circumstances changeRespect refusal; do not treat one acceptance as indefinite consent
Monitor outcomes and risksConsider suitable alternatives, rotation and support
Avoid commitments without necessary authority or resourcesProvide proportionate authority, time, training and resources

Six questions about the legitimacy of a request

  1. Necessity: Is the task genuinely needed or merely convenient to delegate?
  2. Suitability: Have ability, health, time and existing burdens been considered?
  3. Alternatives: Were similarly situated people approached fairly?
  4. Consent: Can the person refuse without social, economic or institutional penalty?
  5. Recognition and support: Are contributions acknowledged and necessary resources provided?
  6. Review: Are duration, sharing and exit conditions clear?

These are interpretive questions, not an automatic scoring system or definitive diagnosis of exploitation. Emergency care, specialist expertise, prior commitments and legal duties may change what alternatives are reasonable.

Distinguishing possible exploitation from legitimate differentiation

Possible pattern: Requests repeatedly target the same helpful person, comparable others are consistently bypassed, refusal carries a relational cost, boundaries remain vague, and the requester makes no reasonable attempt to redistribute the burden. No single indicator establishes exploitation.

Possible legitimate differentiation: Special expertise, an explicit time-limited commitment, formal role authority or others' unseen obligations may justify different allocations. The justification should still be transparent and proportionate.

Two fictional comparisons

Association: Among five capable members, a coordinator always asks the most helpful member to organize an event and never approaches the others. Successful completion does not prove fair allocation. The coordinator is accountable for the allocation process, not only the event outcome.

Urgent family care: An older relative needs immediate assistance and only one sibling is reachable. Temporary responsibility may be justified, but it does not automatically justify assigning all future care to that sibling. Emergency necessity and long-term allocation are distinct questions.

Proposed empirical design

Fictional vignettes could vary requester identity (friend/manager/institution), available alternatives (none/several), frequency (once/repeatedly), cost of refusal (absent/present), and task duration (clear/undefined). Participants could separately rate perceived fairness, voluntariness, exploitation, requester accountability and willingness to accept. Sampling, measurement validity, ethical requirements and analysis plans should be specified before data collection.

Conceptual limits

Not all duties depend on voluntary acceptance: care for children, contractual obligations, public office and preventing imminent harm can generate non-elective responsibilities. Fulfilment of shared ideals may motivate acceptance without cancelling the requester's duty to allocate burdens fairly. These propositions remain untested.

AR-001 · Boundaries of Responsibility and Withdrawal from a Role — v0.50

Status: A conceptual proposal developed from the author’s ideas about capacity, free choice, misuse of goodwill, and fair allocation. It is neither legal advice nor an empirically validated finding.

Expansion and withdrawal are not simply opposite decisions

Voluntarily enlarging one’s responsibilities does not imply an indefinite obligation to continue under changed conditions. Yet voluntary service, contractual commitments, legal duties, and urgent care obligations require different assessments.

Three distinct questions

  1. Acceptance: Under what conditions, with what information, and with what degree of choice was the task accepted?
  2. Continuation: Are health, time, skills, resources, and existing family obligations still compatible with the task?
  3. Withdrawal: Who depends on the work, and what reasonable transition measures could limit foreseeable harm?

  2. Nature of commitment: Was there an explicit term, role definition, contract, or legal duty?

  4. Alternatives: Can tasks be reduced, shared, postponed, supported, or transferred?
  5. Notice and transition: Are reasonable notice, documentation, and handover feasible?

Care: A family caregiver’s health deteriorates. How can support, shared care, and safe alternatives protect both the caregiver and the dependant?

Provisional, testable propositions

P1: When a role expands beyond its initial agreement, lack of renewed consent may be associated with greater perceived pressure. P2: Fair participation by similarly capable people may reduce perceived inequity. P3: Clear notice and handover procedures may reduce harm to both the person leaving and those depending on the service. These are untested propositions, not causal findings.

A “right to withdraw” is not an unrestricted licence to abandon dependants or disregard binding duties, particularly where children, care needs, safety-critical work, or legal commitments are involved. Nor is goodwill an unlimited resource. The framework proposes a context-sensitive balance between protecting individual capacity and limiting foreseeable harm to others. Legal implications require separate jurisdiction-specific analysis.

AR-001 · Family Solidarity, Loss of Capacity, and Remembering Sacrifice — v0.51

Status: A consolidation of the author's contributions at this stage; conceptual propositions, not empirically established findings.

1. The innermost circle is distinct

Within the family, the starting assumption for understanding sacrifice in a time of need is not suspicion of exploited goodwill. Family members may see themselves as parts of a shared whole. This does not deny that exploitation can occur; it cautions against automatically applying the expectations of more distant social circles to family solidarity.

2. “Game over”: Responsibility reverses when capacity is lost

Illness or loss of economic resources may prevent one family member from maintaining previous contributions. Responsibility does not simply disappear: other members may take on additional duties alongside their existing ones. The form of this redistribution depends on actual needs and capabilities. The phrase does not imply that the person's worth or belonging ends.

3. The future is unknown at the moment of sacrifice

When a family needs help, an individual may willingly accept what seems an unavoidable family responsibility. Future differences in education, occupation, and economic circumstances are not yet known. It would therefore be misleading to treat every sacrifice as a pre-calculated claim for future compensation.

4. Speaking about sacrifice later

After family members establish separate lives, past sacrifices may be discussed without a demand for repayment or additional rights. Someone may recount their contribution because they feel its full value has not been recognized. Feeling unappreciated and actually being unappreciated are distinct; later recollection does not by itself negate earlier voluntary commitment.

5. Divergent opportunities and perceived missed chances

If one sibling continues education while another works, it cannot automatically be assumed that the latter unwillingly sacrificed an educational opportunity. Work may have been freely preferred; alternatively, unforeseen family duties may have constrained education. Later differences in opportunity can lead to perceptions of missed chances, which should not be mistaken for demonstrated counterfactual outcomes.

6. Pretext, reason, and subjective meaning

In the author's distinction, a pretext may be a consciously constructed explanation in anticipation of prevailing expectations of success, whereas a reason may arise from unplanned family obligations that a person had to shoulder. Similar biographies can reflect different original intentions. Repeatedly recounting sacrifice does not, on its own, establish a consciously fabricated pretext.

Limitations and next steps

The point is to distinguish the meaning of sacrifice when it occurs from its later interpretation, not to turn family life into an accounting exercise. Any future empirical study should separately examine consent, necessity, perceived recognition, divergent opportunities, and motives for retelling. The author's contribution to this strand is consolidated here without requesting further input.
